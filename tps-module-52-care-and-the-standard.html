Care
+ the Standard

Take care of your people. Hold them accountable. Every officer is told to do both, and most quietly pick one. This capstone shows why that is a false choice and how to do both in the same conversation. It brings together Just Culture, servant leadership, ownership, and the hardest case of all: a good member who is struggling. It prepares you for the leadership philosophy question, and for the first week you wear the badge.

Chapter 47 · Lesson 1 of 5

Care and the Standard —
The False Choice

⏱ 10 min ⚖️ Leadership Identity

Every new officer is told there are two kinds of bosses. The one who takes care of the crew and lets things slide, and the one who holds the line and nobody wants to work for. Then they are asked to pick.

It is a false choice, and believing it is the most common reason good firefighters become mediocre officers. Care and standards are not two ends of one dial. They are two separate dials, and you are responsible for both being turned up.

The board question sounds like this: "How do you take care of your people while still holding them accountable?" Most candidates answer half of it. This chapter is about answering all of it, and about being that officer after the badge is pinned on.

The Four Officers

Low Care · Low Standard
The Absentee
In the office with the door shut. No conflict because no engagement. The crew runs itself, at whatever level it drifts to.
High Care · Low Standard
The Buddy
Loved at the kitchen table. Avoids hard conversations to protect the relationship. The crew slowly learns that nothing is required, and the strongest members get resentful first.
Low Care · High Standard
The Enforcer
Knows every policy. Corrects in public. Gets compliance while he is watching and nothing when he is not. People transfer out and stop telling him things.
High Care · High Standard
The Standard Bearer
Knows their people and expects a lot from them. The crew works hard because they do not want to let this officer down, and because they know the officer will not let them down either.

Kim Scott describes a similar grid in Radical Candor: care personally and challenge directly. The fire service version has higher stakes, because the standard you did not hold on Tuesday is the mistake that hurts someone on Friday.

Holding the Standard Is the Care

Here is the shift in thinking. When you let a firefighter skip the mask check, you are not being kind. You are deciding that their comfort today matters more than their life next month. When you avoid telling someone their performance is slipping, you are letting them walk toward discipline without a warning. The Buddy is not caring for people. He is caring for his own comfort.

And the standard without care does not hold either. People do not give discretionary effort to someone who does not know their kids' names. Trust is what makes a hard conversation land as help instead of as an attack.

❌ Half an Answer — Scores a 3
"I believe in taking care of my people. If you take care of them, they will take care of you. I would have an open door and make sure they have what they need." All care. The assessor writes: no mention of accountability.
✅ Both Dials — Scores High
"I do not see those as competing. Holding someone to the standard is one of the ways I take care of them, because the standard is what sends them home. So I invest in knowing my people first, and when something slips I address it early, privately and specifically. The relationship is what lets them hear it." Both halves, with the link between them.
The Core Idea of This Chapter

You are not choosing between being liked and being respected. You are building enough trust that you can tell someone the truth, and telling the truth often enough that the trust means something.

Just Culture —
Sorting the Behavior

⏱ 11 min 🧭 Fair Accountability

"Hold them accountable" is easy to say. The harder question is: accountable how? If every mistake gets the same response, you are either too harsh on honest errors or too soft on reckless ones. Either way the crew stops reporting problems, and that is when people get hurt.

Just Culture, developed by David Marx and used widely in aviation and healthcare, gives you a sorting tool. You met its decision guide in Chapter 26. Here it becomes your daily operating method. The principle: respond to the behavior and the choice behind it, not to how bad the outcome was.

Three Behaviors, Three Responses

1
Human Error — Console
An unintended slip by someone trying to do it right. The medic who grabbed the wrong vial from a drawer where two look-alike drugs sit side by side. Support the person, then fix the system that made the error easy. Punishing honest error teaches people to hide it.
2
At-Risk Behavior — Coach
A shortcut that has become normal because nothing bad has happened yet. Skipping the backing spotter. Not buckling for the short run. The person does not see the risk anymore. Coach them back to why the rule exists, and ask what is making the shortcut attractive.
3
Reckless Behavior — Discipline
A conscious choice to disregard a risk they knew was substantial. This is where progressive discipline belongs. Protecting reckless behavior is unfair to everyone who does it right.

Three Questions Before You Respond

1
What did they intend?
Were they trying to do the right thing and slipped, taking a shortcut they thought was fine, or knowingly ignoring the risk? Ask them. This is the 3 U's with a safety lens.
Listen before you sort
2
Would someone else have done the same?
The substitution test. If you put another competent firefighter in the same spot with the same information and they would likely do the same thing, look hard at the system before you look at the person.
Protects against blaming the individual for a setup
3
Is it a pattern?
A first slip and a fifth are different conversations. Repeated at-risk behavior after coaching moves toward discipline.
Check your documentation

Do Not Sort by Outcome

Two engineers both back without a spotter. One taps a bollard. The other gets lucky. The choice was identical, so the response should be identical. If you only address the one with the dent, you have taught the crew that the standard is "do not get caught."

❌ Outcome-Based — Loses Points
"There was damage to the apparatus, so that is a written reprimand per policy." No inquiry into what happened or why. No look at whether everyone backs that way.
✅ Just Culture — Scores High
"Before I decide anything, I find out what happened. If he was doing it the way we all do it, then I have an at-risk behavior that is normal on my crew, and that is mine to own and fix with everyone. If he was told to wait for a spotter and chose not to, that is a different conversation. And I follow our policy on reporting the damage either way." Sorts the behavior, owns the system, respects policy.
Your Authority Has Limits

Just Culture is how you think. Your department's policy and labor agreement decide what you are allowed to do. Some events carry required reporting or required steps regardless of intent. Know which ones, follow them, and use the sorting to inform what you recommend up the chain.

When the crew sees you respond to an honest mistake with support and a system fix, they start telling you about near misses. That is the payoff. A crew that reports is a crew you can keep safe.

Chapter 47 · Lesson 3 of 5

Serve and Own —
Two Halves of One Officer

⏱ 10 min 🛡 Philosophy in Practice

Two leadership philosophies get quoted at almost every oral board. Candidates name them and move on. Assessors have heard both names a hundred times. What they have rarely heard is what either one looks like at 0700 on a Tuesday.

Servant Leadership — What It Is and Is Not

Robert Greenleaf's idea is that the leader exists to serve the people doing the work, so they can do it well and grow. In a firehouse it is concrete.

It Is
Removing obstacles
The broken tool gets fixed because you chased the work order. The training they need gets scheduled because you made the call.
It Is
Going last
You eat last, you take the worst bunk, you stay late when someone has to. Small things the crew notices immediately.
It Is
Serving their needs
What a person needs and what they want are sometimes different. A firefighter may want you to ignore a slipping skill. What they need is for you to help them fix it.
It Is Not
Serving their comfort
Servant leadership is not doing whatever keeps the crew happy. The Buddy thinks he is a servant leader. He is serving himself.

Extreme Ownership — What It Is and Is Not

Jocko Willink and Leif Babin's principle is that the leader owns everything in their world. If the crew failed, the first question is what you did or did not do.

It Is
Looking in the mirror first
"I did not make the expectation clear." "I never checked that C shift was trained." Internal locus of control, out loud.
It Is
Owning it upward
When your chief asks what happened, the answer starts with "I," not with a firefighter's name.
It Is Not
Taking the fall for everything
Owning the crew's performance does not mean excusing an individual's choices. You can say "I own that my crew thought this was acceptable" and still hold the member accountable.
It Is Not
A speech
If you announce your ownership and nothing changes, it was a performance.

Why You Need Both

Service without ownership produces a pleasant crew that drifts. Ownership without service produces a driven officer nobody trusts. Together they describe the Standard Bearer: I am here for you, and what happens here is on me.

The Bank of Leadership

You know the Bank of Leadership from earlier chapters. Every act of service is a deposit: remembering the surgery date, covering the shift trade, going to bat for someone's class request. Every hard conversation is a withdrawal. The officer who has made deposits for a year can make a large withdrawal and keep the relationship. The officer who shows up with only withdrawals is overdrawn on the first one.

❌ Name-Dropping — Loses Points
"My leadership philosophy is servant leadership and extreme ownership. I serve my crew and I own everything." Two book titles and no behavior. Every candidate said it today.
✅ Behavior — Scores High
"I think of my job as two things. I work for my crew, which means if the saw has been out of service for a week, that is my phone call to make. And I answer for my crew, which means when our turnout times slipped last quarter, I told my BC it was mine and then changed how we stage gear. Greenleaf and Willink have names for those. I just think it is the job." Two specific acts. The names come last and sound earned.
Humility Is the Hinge

Both philosophies depend on the H in T·C·H·O·E·D·S. You cannot serve people you think you are above, and you cannot own a failure you are too proud to see.

Chapter 47 · Lesson 4 of 5

When Someone Is Struggling —
Both Things Are True

⏱ 11 min ❤️ The Hardest Case

This is the situation that tests everything in this chapter. A good firefighter starts slipping. Late twice. Quiet at the table. A missed step on a call that they would never have missed a year ago. You hear from someone else that things are bad at home.

The Buddy says nothing because the member is going through a lot. The Enforcer writes it up because late is late. Both are failing the member. The Standard Bearer holds two true things at once: this person needs support, and the job still has to be done safely.

The Conversation — Care First, Standard Clear

1
Start with the person
Private, unhurried. "I have noticed you have not seemed like yourself the last few weeks, and I wanted to check on you before anything else. How are you doing?" Then stop talking.
Deposit before withdrawal
2
Name what you have seen
SBI, gently and specifically. "You were late on the 3rd and the 9th, and on the alarm Tuesday you went in without your radio." Facts show that you are paying attention, which is a form of care.
Behavior, not character
3
Diagnose
Run the 3 U's. In this case you are usually looking at Unable: something outside of work is taking capacity. That changes the response from discipline to support.
Do not guess. Ask
4
Offer real support
Know your department's resources before you need them: peer support, the employee assistance program, the chaplain, leave options, a shift trade. Offer to make the first call with them. Chapter 38 covers this in depth.
Specific, not "let me know"
5
State the standard and the plan
"I am going to help you get through this. I also need you safe and on time, because the crew is counting on you and so are the people who call. So here is what we are going to do for the next month." Agree on it together.
Both true, same sentence
6
Follow up
A set date, and informal check-ins between. Document the conversation factually and tell your BC what they need to know.
Care is what you do next week

What It Sounds Like

Struggling Member · Lieutenant Level
Officer
"I wanted to check on you. You have not seemed like yourself lately. How are you doing?"
Firefighter
"I am fine, Lieutenant. Just tired."
Officer
"Okay. I ask because you were late on the 3rd and the 9th, and Tuesday you went in without a radio. That is not you. In six years I have never seen that from you." (Specific, and frames it as out of character)
Firefighter
"...My wife moved out three weeks ago. I have the kids on my days off and I am not sleeping."
Officer
"I am sorry. That is a lot to carry, and I am glad you told me." (Pause) "I want to do two things. I want to get you some help, and I want to keep you safe at work while you get through this. Have you talked to anyone on the peer support team?"
Firefighter
"No. I do not want this all over the department."
Officer
"It will not be. Peer support exists for exactly this, and I am not going to share your personal business. The only thing I pass to the BC is that I am working with you on attendance. I will sit with you while you make the call if that helps. For the next month, I need you here on time and I need you sharp on calls. If you are not rested enough to be safe on a given shift, call me before the shift and we will figure out leave. Can you work with that?" (Support and standard together)
Assessor
Led with the person. Specific behaviors. Diagnosed before deciding. Named resources. Kept the standard. Addressed confidentiality honestly. Set a plan.

Where the Line Is

Support does not mean the standard is suspended without limit. If the member cannot perform safely, they should not be on the rig, and getting them off it through leave or modified duty is the caring act. If the behavior continues after real support, the conversation moves toward formal steps, and you will be able to show that you did everything right first. If you ever have a concern that a member may harm themselves, that is not a coaching issue. Stay with them and follow your department's crisis procedure immediately.

Friend to Boss

This conversation is hardest when the member is your friend. It is also when it matters most, because your friend is the person least likely to hear it from anyone else. Say it plainly: "I am telling you this because I am your friend and because I am your officer. Both of those are why I am not going to look away."

L·E·A·D
Applied to Care and the Standard

⏱ 9 min ⚡ Capstone

This chapter is a capstone. It does not add a new exercise type. It gives you the through-line for answers you already know how to structure, and for the officer you are about to be.

L·E·A·D on a Care-and-Standard Question

L
Label
The philosophy version is Leadership Philosophy. The scenario version is a Personnel Issue. Listen for which one you were asked.
E
Engage
T·C·H·O·E·D·S and your Identity Anchor for philosophy. STAR-P or the counseling arc for a scenario.
A
Apply
Show both dials every time. Sort the behavior with Just Culture. Diagnose with the 3 U's. Name a real support resource.
D
Deliver
Close with two values together. Empathy and Courage is the natural pair: I care enough to have the conversation.

The Complete Response

Model Response · "How do you take care of your crew while still holding the standard?"
Reframe
"I do not see those as two things I have to trade off. Holding someone to the standard is one of the ways I take care of them, because the standard is what gets them home and what gets the public the service they called for."
Care
"So I start by knowing my people. What they are working toward, what is going on at home, what they need from me to do the job well. If the saw is down, that is my phone call."
Standard
"And I am clear from the first shift about what I expect. When something slips, I address it that day, in private, with the specific behavior. Before I decide what it is, I ask. An honest mistake gets support and I look at what set them up for it. A shortcut gets coaching. A conscious disregard gets documented and goes where our policy says it goes."
Example
"Last year one of my most reliable firefighters was late twice in a month. I sat down with him and asked how he was doing before I said anything about the time. He was going through a divorce. We got him connected with peer support, we worked out a shift trade, and I told him I still needed him on time and sharp. He has not been late since, and he told me later that conversation was the first time he felt like anybody noticed."
Principle
"That is Empathy and Courage together. I cared enough to notice, and I cared enough to say the hard part. As an officer I owe my crew both."
Assessor
Rejected the trade-off. Both dials shown. Sorted behavior fairly. Real example with outcome. Two-value close earned by the story.

Your Practice Target for This Chapter

Write your answer to the question at the top of the model response in five sentences: the reframe, one care behavior, one standard behavior, one real example, one two-value close. Then run the struggling-member scenario in The Office and listen to the debrief from their chair. If they did not feel both the care and the standard, run it again.

The First Ninety Days

When you promote, your crew will decide within a few weeks which of the four officers you are. Make a deposit with every member in your first two shifts. State your standards in your first week. Then keep both promises. Everything else in this curriculum works better once the crew knows you mean both.
