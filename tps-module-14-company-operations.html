Incident Command at the Company Level

⏱ 14 min🏛️ IC FundamentalsCompany Officer Role

The company officer is the most important position in the ICS structure at most incidents — not because of organizational rank, but because every complex incident is built from company-level operations. Understanding ICS at the company level means understanding not just how to use the system but what it requires of the officer who occupies the first-arriving IC position before a larger command structure is established.

The First-Arriving IC

At most residential and commercial structural incidents, the first-arriving company officer assumes Incident Command and manages the entire incident — for the first few minutes, until a higher-ranking officer arrives, or for the entire incident if it remains within single-company capability. This means the company officer must be capable of simultaneously: commanding their crew's tactical operations, establishing the command post, initiating the ICS structure (Operations, Safety), communicating with dispatch and incoming units, conducting ongoing size-up, and making the critical early decisions that determine incident trajectory.

Assume Command
Establish the Command Post
Announce command via radio. Identify CP location. Begin size-up narration for dispatch and incoming units.
Size-Up + Strategy
Initial Tactical Decision
Offensive, defensive, or transitional? Based on conditions, resources, and victim information. Announced on radio.
Deploy Crew
Initial Tactical Assignment
Assign crew to the primary tactical function: attack or search. Maintain two-in-two-out. Request resources.
Transfer Command
To Arriving Senior Officer
Brief the incoming IC on: conditions found, actions taken, resources assigned, status of primary search, outstanding needs.
The Danger of Task Saturation

The first-arriving officer who is both the IC and on the attack line simultaneously is doing two jobs that both require full attention. The result is degraded performance at both. Whenever possible, designate a crew member for one task and maintain the other. If single-person command is unavoidable due to staffing, it should be brief and transition to a formal IC as soon as additional resources arrive.

Naming the System — How Command Is Actually Organized

The section above describes what the first-arriving officer does. The promotable officer also knows the named systems that structure it — because a board expects the vocabulary, and because your department runs on one of these models whether it says so or not. Three layers are worth knowing, presented neutrally as they actually relate.

NIMS ICS
The federal, all-hazards Incident Command System (FEMA). Scalable from a single company (Type 5) to a national disaster (Type 1). It is the legal and organizational backbone — unity of command, span of control, common terminology — but it is deliberately generic and not drilled to the everyday working fire.
Blue Card / Hazard-Zone Command
A command training and certification system built on Alan Brunacini's Fire Command and Command Safety and the 8 Functions of Command, aimed squarely at local NIMS Type 4 and Type 5 incidents — the everyday hazard zone. Online coursework plus a simulation lab that certifies the IC as both a mobile and a stationary commander. Accredited under the IFSAC Hazard-Zone Incident Command Standard.
The 8 Functions of Command
Brunacini's doctrinal spine (under Blue Card, and a clean framework on its own): assume and confirm command, evaluate the situation, communications, deployment, strategy and the incident action plan, organization, review and revision, and continuing, transferring, or terminating command. Referenced in over 100 NIOSH line-of-duty-death reports.
Informal / kitchen-table command
Many smaller and volunteer departments run command informally, with no named certification. It is common and workable on small incidents — but it is the model most exposed when an incident escalates faster than the command structure does. Know whether this is your reality, and where its limits are.

None of these is "the answer" a board wants recited. What scores is knowing which system your department operates under, naming it, and explaining how you scale from a single-company response into a larger structure as the incident grows. NIMS is the backbone; Blue Card and the 8 Functions are the most widely adopted way to drill the everyday hazard zone; informal command is the honest reality across much of the fire service.

Size-Up — COAL WAS WEALTH

⏱ 14 min📋 Size-Up Framework

COAL WAS WEALTH is the comprehensive size-up framework used by the company officer to systematically assess an incident scene. Each letter represents a category of information that shapes the tactical approach. The officer who uses a systematic framework gathers more complete information than one relying on impressions.

C
Construction — What Type of Building?
Ordinary, legacy wood frame, lightweight truss, fire-resistive, non-combustible. Construction type determines structural behavior under fire load and potential failure timeline.
O
Occupancy — What Is the Building Used For?
Residential, commercial, industrial, assembly. Occupancy determines probable fuel load, occupant type and mobility, and hazardous materials probability.
A
Apparatus and Personnel — What Resources Do I Have?
On scene, responding, available. Resource inventory at time of size-up determines what tactical approach is achievable with available forces.
L
Life Safety — Are There Victims?
Known, probable, or unlikely? Location and mobility? Victim information is the primary driver of the offensive vs defensive decision.
W
Water Supply — Is There Adequate Water?
Hydrant availability, flow rate, distance. Without confirmed water supply, offensive operations have a hard stop built in.
A
Auxiliary Appliances — Suppression Systems?
Sprinklers, standpipes, FDC connections. Working suppression systems are the most valuable fire control asset on scene.
S
Street Conditions — Access and Egress?
Driveway access for apparatus, overhead obstructions, aerial positioning requirements, secondary egress routes.
W
Weather — How Does It Affect Operations?
Wind direction and speed (ventilation implications), temperature (decon and medical considerations), precipitation.
E
Exposures — What Is at Risk Beyond the Incident Structure?
Adjacent buildings, vehicles, utilities. Exposure protection may be a competing priority with offensive interior attack.
A
Area — How Big Is the Incident?
Fire area, building footprint, and square footage determine resource requirements and operational period planning.
L
Location and Extent of Fire — Where Is It?
Floor, area, compartment. Combined with construction type and occupancy, determines spread probability and tactical approach.
T
Time — How Long Has It Been Burning?
Elapsed time since ignition affects structural integrity, victim viability, and tactical options.
H
Hazardous Materials — What's in There?
Occupancy type, facility LEPC reports, visible placards, occupant information. Hazmat changes the tactical approach significantly.
COAL WAS WEALTH as a Training Tool

The systematic framework is most valuable as a training tool — a checklist that builds the habit of comprehensive size-up before committing to a tactical approach. Over time, the framework becomes internalized as part of the arriving officer's automatic assessment process.

COAL WAS WEALTH Is One System, Not the Only One

COAL WAS WEALTH is a complete, validated size-up framework — but not the only one. Other officers use WALLACE WAS HOT (a reordering of the same factors) or Brunacini's "critical factors" model. The point of any of them is identical: a systematic read instead of an impression. And size-up does not stop at the building — it includes reading the fire itself. Pair this framework with the smoke read (VVDC) and the modern fire-behavior indicators from Chapter 14 · Fire Behavior: a command decision is only as good as the size-up beneath it.

A Parallel System — The Blue Card / Brunacini Size-Up

COAL WAS WEALTH is one way to size up a fire — a thorough, thirteen-point recon checklist of what to look at. The most widely used command system in the American fire service organizes the same read differently. Blue Card, built on Alan Brunacini’s Fire Command, treats size-up as the front end of a decision: distill the scene into a handful of critical fireground factors, run them through a risk plan, and broadcast the result. Brunacini’s core insight is that no commander can act on seventy-five pieces of information — the human limit is five or six. The job is not to see everything; it is to identify the factors that actually drive the decision.

The Seven Critical Fireground Factors

Brunacini reduces the fireground to seven categories — the "tactical vital signs" the incident commander reads, gathered through three channels: visual (what you see on the 360), recon (reports from crews inside and around back), and preplan (what you knew before you arrived). Size-up never stops — the 360 and ongoing interior reports are continuous size-up.

Building
Size, height, age, construction type, condition, compartmentation — and how much is left to burn.
Fire
Size, stage, location, extent, and direction of travel: what is burning and where it is going.
Occupancy
Use and contents, the value at risk, and the life hazard the occupancy implies.
Life Safety
Occupants and firefighters — the factor that outranks all others and drives the strategy.
Arrangement
Access, layout, and exposures — what the fire can reach, and how you can reach it.
Resources
What is on scene, en route, and available — and honestly, what those resources are capable of.
Action
The effect of operations so far, and whether the strategy should be offensive or defensive.

An eighth bucket — special circumstances (weather, time of day, hazardous materials) — captures anything the seven categories miss.

The Standard Initial Radio Report

A size-up is worthless until it is communicated. Brunacini called the initial radio report "probably the most important communication we transmit on the entire incident," because it puts every incoming unit on the same page in one transmission. It follows a standard order:

1 · On scene
Unit designation and arrival — clear the alarm and announce you are on scene.
2 · Building
A quick building/area description: size, height, occupancy type.
3 · Conditions
The problem in standard terms: nothing showing, smoke showing, or a working fire.
4 · Action & mode
What you are doing — the initial action plan — and your command mode: investigating, fast-attacking, or command.
5 · Command
Assume and name command — "Engine 1 is Main Street Command" — so the incident has one accountable IC.
6 · Strategy
The declaration: offensive or defensive. Every unit now knows the operating posture.
Two Systems, One Read — How to Use Both at a Board

COAL WAS WEALTH and the Blue Card size-up are not competitors — they are the same fireground seen through two lenses. COAL WAS WEALTH is the exhaustive checklist of what to observe; the seven critical factors are how a commander distills that into a decision; the initial radio report is how you broadcast it. Brunacini’s strategic sequence ties them together: size up the critical factors, run them through the risk plan (risk a lot for a savable life, a little for savable property, nothing for what is already lost), declare the strategy, and build the action plan. The officer who can move fluidly between both systems — and name which one they are using — is exactly the candidate this chapter’s opening ribbon describes: the one who knows multiple systems is the most prepared in the room.

Rescue Priority Framework

⏱ 14 min🚨 Rescue Priorities · Search Tactics

Rescue — the removal of victims from immediate danger — is the highest priority in the fire service tactical hierarchy. The company officer at a structural fire makes rescue priority decisions based on victim location, victim viability, available resources, and structural conditions. These decisions are among the most consequential and most time-compressed judgments in fire service operations.

The Rescue Priority Framework

P1
Immediate Life Safety — Known Victims in Immediate Danger
Visible victims at windows, confirmed location from witnesses, immediate rescue prior to water application in some circumstances. P1 rescue may require bypassing standard entry sequence if the victim is accessible and rescue is achievable before conditions deteriorate.
P2
Primary Search — Systematic Search in Fire Area and Adjacent Spaces
Systematic search of the fire area and immediately adjacent spaces before fire control. Conducted simultaneously with or immediately following initial attack. Primary search seeks living victims who may be viable; it is not a recovery operation.
P3
Secondary Search — Complete Verification After Fire Control
Systematic search of the entire structure after the fire is controlled. The secondary search is the verification that the primary search did not miss anyone and that no victims are in spaces not reached initially.
P4
Recovery — Body Recovery Operations
Recovery of deceased victims after all viable rescue has been completed. Recovery operations typically involve different tactical approaches and may require law enforcement coordination for investigation purposes.
Vent-Enter-Search
Traditional vs Modern
Traditional VES — break window, enter, search — creates a flow path without water on the fire. Modern guidance: coordinate with attack team, water before or simultaneous with entry, or use transitional attack to stabilize the environment before entry.
Search Pattern Discipline
Systematic and Disciplined
Oriented search patterns (staying connected to the wall, maintaining orientation to the entry point) prevent disorientation and improve completeness. The officer who maintains orientation accountability for the search team is managing a critical safety function.
The Rescue-Safety Balance

The company officer who commits all resources to rescue without maintaining the two-in-two-out requirement, without confirming structural integrity, and without a crew bailout plan has made the rescue decision at the expense of crew safety. Rescues that result in additional firefighter casualties are organizational and human tragedies. The balance: aggressive rescue within a structured safety framework.

Naming the Risk Framework — Profile, Rules, and the Honest Critique

The lesson calls for "aggressive rescue within a structured safety framework." That framework has a name — the risk-management profile. Stating it explicitly at a board, and tying your tactical decision to it, is a 4/5 move:

Risk a lot
to save a lot — a known, savable life. Maximum acceptable risk, taken within two-in/two-out and a designated RIT.
Risk a little
to save a little — savable property. Reduce and minimize the inherent risk of routine activity.
Risk nothing
to save what is already lost. No risk to members is acceptable when there is nothing left to save.

The promotable officer also knows the modern critique, and showing that range is a 5/5 move. The maxim is increasingly challenged as dogma: it never defines what "a lot," "a little," "risk," or "save" actually mean, which is why the field is moving toward more formal, defined risk-analysis for company officers — borrowing the structured go/no-go decision tools wildland firefighting has used for decades. The IAFC Rules of Engagement for Structural Firefighting is one such named framework: an explicit set of go/no-go and risk-acceptance criteria for the crew and the IC.

Mitigate · Alter · Suspend — Controlling Risk Without Abandoning the Mission

Risk management is not a binary go/no-go. The officer has middle moves: alter the action (pull a company out of a collapse zone and flow the line from a safe vantage point), or suspend it (hold crews from advancing above the fire until the fire floor is controlled) — without abandoning the objective. Naming these, rather than only "risk a lot to save a lot," shows an officer who manages risk continuously, not once at the door.

The Search Decision — Survivability Profiling vs. Searchable Space

The hardest place to apply risk-versus-benefit is the search decision: do you commit a crew to search before the fire is controlled, based on whether anyone inside could still be alive? This is one of the most actively debated questions in the modern fire service — and a promotable officer should be able to speak to both sides, because a board may push on exactly this point.

The risk-based case
Survivability Profiling
Read the size-up cues — the fire’s location and severity relative to where victims would be, the conditions, and the elapsed time — and judge whether a viable victim could realistically still exist. It is presented as the logical extension of the risk-management profile: you do not risk firefighters to search a space where no one could have survived. Disciplined, conditions-driven, and honest about lost causes.
The mission-based case
Searchable Space
You cannot reliably judge survivability from the front yard. Critics argue “profiling” too easily becomes an excuse not to search — so the better frame is searchable space: the tenable, reachable pockets that exist even in a bad-looking structure, especially remote from the fire and behind closed doors. The mission is to save those who cannot save themselves; the default is to search.

Modern fire research has reshaped this debate, and largely toward the second view. UL’s Fire Safety Research Institute studied residential size-up and search, and the findings are pointed:

More Survivable Space Than Assumed
FSRI found there is more survivable space inside a burning home than the fire service long believed. Victims have been located alive — and rescued — in rooms venting heavy smoke and even in fully involved structures. “No survivability showing” called from the exterior is frequently wrong.
Closed Doors Create Tenable Pockets
Isolation by a single closed door dramatically extends survivability. A space’s tenability tracks two things — its proximity to the fire (thermal exposure) and its elevation (toxic gases and heat rise). A non-tenable front room says nothing about a rear bedroom with the door shut.
Time Is the Enemy
Exposure duration matters as much as dose — searchable space on arrival is not survivable space indefinitely. The research conclusion is not “search recklessly,” it is “search fast and search smart,” paired with coordinated fast-water suppression and isolation to expand tenable space for everyone inside.
The Defensible Board Position — Profiling Directs Search, It Does Not Cancel It

The mature answer holds both truths. The risk-management profile is real: you do not trade a firefighter for a body. But the research — and the line-of-duty data, where interior search is a small fraction of firefighter fatalities relative to cardiac and roadway events — does not support writing search off from the yard. So use survivability profiling to decide where to search first, not whether to search at all: prioritize the most likely-survivable, searchable spaces, isolate and ventilate to buy time, and put fast water on the fire to improve every space at once. Commit within the profile — risk a lot for a savable life — and pair this read with the smoke and fire-behavior cues from Chapter 14 · Fire Behavior. An officer who can name this tension, cite the research on both sides, and land on a coordinated, prioritized search is demonstrating exactly the judgment a board is scoring.

Resource Management at Incident Command

⏱ 14 min⚙️ Resource Management · Staging

The company officer as IC is not only managing the tactical operation — they are managing the resources required to execute it. Resource management at the company officer level includes requesting resources, staging them appropriately, assigning them to tactical objectives, and tracking their status throughout the incident.

Span of Control and Staging

ICS establishes a span of

ICS establishes a span of control guideline of 3–7 supervisory elements per supervisor, with 5 as optimal. The first-arriving IC who has four companies operating simultaneously without sector officers is exceeding a manageable span of control. The company officer recognizes when to request additional command resources — not just tactical resources — based on the growing complexity of the incident.

STG
Staging Discipline — Where Resources Wait
Level 1 staging: companies stage one block away and advise dispatch. Level 2 (large incidents): companies stage in a designated area. Resources that freelance to the scene without staging create congestion, accountability problems, and coordination failures.
ASN
Assignment Clarity — Every Resource Knows Its Job
Every company, team, and individual resource should have a clear assignment: where they are going, what they are doing, who they report to, and what their communications channel is. Companies that arrive without assignment and self-deploy create tactical chaos.
ACT
Accountability — Where Is Everyone?
The IC maintains accountability for every resource: assigned (working), staged (available), committed (deployed to a sector). Resources not tracked are resources that cannot be rapidly redeployed if tactical conditions change or a mayday is declared.
The Air Management Principle

Air management — managing the air supply in SCBA cylinders — is a resource management function for interior operating crews. The standard: crews should begin withdrawal from IDLH environments at 50% cylinder pressure, providing air for the exit under normal conditions plus a reserve for emergency. The company officer who does not track crew air status is managing a time-limited resource without a clock.

The Safety Systems With Names — Accountability, RIT, and the Mayday

Resource management at the company level includes the named safety systems a board will probe. Generic answers ("I would keep track of everyone") score lower than named systems applied correctly.

Accountability systems
A formal system tracks who is in the hazard zone and where — passport or tag systems, an accountability officer, and the PAR (Personnel Accountability Report), a roll call triggered at benchmarks (primary search complete, fire under control) or when conditions change suddenly. "Where is everyone?" is answered by a system, not a memory.
Two-in / two-out
The OSHA respiratory-protection rule (29 CFR 1910.134), reinforced by NFPA 1500: crews enter IDLH atmospheres in teams of at least two, with at least two staffed and equipped outside — the initial rapid-intervention crew. The recognized exception: a known, savable life may justify entry before the outside team is fully set — a deliberate risk-profile decision the officer owns.
RIT / RIC
The Rapid Intervention Team/Crew — a dedicated team staged and ready to rescue a downed or trapped firefighter. On a working fire it is a command priority, not an afterthought; the IC who has not designated one has accepted an unmanaged risk.
The Mayday
A firefighter in trouble transmits a Mayday in a standard format — LUNAR, broken out below. The officer trains the crew to declare it early and without hesitation, and the IC drops everything to manage it. A delayed Mayday is a recurring fatality theme.
L
Location
Where you are — floor, quadrant, side — as precisely as conditions allow.
U
Unit
Your unit and crew designation.
N
Name
Who is transmitting the Mayday.
A
Assignment
What you were doing when you got into trouble.
R
Resources
What you need to survive and exit — including your remaining air.

Special Tactical Situations

⏱ 14 min🔥 High-Rise · Commercial · Vehicle Fires

The company officer encounters structural fire situations beyond routine residential operations — commercial structures, high-rise buildings, vehicle fires, and wildland-urban interface conditions. Each presents unique tactical considerations that the officer should be prepared to address.

Commercial Structure Fires
Larger Volume, Higher Fuel Loads
Large volume commercial fires require earlier resource requests, higher water volumes, and more conservative offensive/defensive decisions. The same construction-type analysis applies, but at larger scale. Pre-incident plans are even more critical for commercial occupancies.
High-Rise Operations
Stairwell Attack, Elevator Protocols
High-rise fires require stairwell-based attack (typically two floors below the fire floor), elevator protocols (not to fire floor — to two floors below), and establishing a base, lobby control, and staging area. The company officer staging in a high-rise is managing a 3D resource coordination challenge.
Vehicle Fires
Hybrid/EV Hazards
Modern hybrid and electric vehicles present significant fire hazards: high-voltage battery fires that can reignite after apparent suppression, toxic off-gas from lithium-ion batteries, and structural features that complicate extrication. The company officer identifies hybrid/EV indicators and applies current guidance on thermal management and exposure protection.
Wildland-Urban Interface
Defensible Space and LCES
WUI fires require LCES — Lookouts, Communications, Escape Routes, Safety Zones — before any structure protection operations begin. The company officer who enters a WUI environment without confirming LCES has removed the survival system that WUI operations require.
The Pre-Incident Plan Advantage

The company officer who has pre-planned their commercial and high-occupancy buildings — with floor plans, utility shutoffs, hazardous materials locations, FDC connections, and structural features documented — arrives at those incidents with information advantage that dramatically improves their tactical decision-making speed and quality.

The Wildland-Urban Interface — An Emerging Threat Every Officer Must Own

The wildland-urban interface — where homes and infrastructure meet undeveloped wildland — is the fastest-growing threat in the American fire service, and it is no longer confined to the mountain West. Decades of development pushing into flammable landscapes, longer and hotter fire seasons, and dense fuels have produced a new failure mode: the urban conflagration, where a wildfire reaches a community and structures themselves become the fuel, spreading fire from building to building faster than any agency can contain it. Colorado’s Marshall Fire, the Lahaina disaster, and the 2025 Los Angeles fires all share that signature — and they are why national bodies now insist every officer, not just wildland crews, be prepared for it.

WUI operations are neither standard structural firefighting nor standard wildland firefighting. The fire arrives as a moving front and a storm of wind-driven embers that ignite structures far ahead of the flames; you face many threatened structures at once, with limited water, long access, and no realistic prospect of saving them all. The officer’s job shifts from "extinguish this fire" to "decide what is defensible, protect what can be protected safely, and never trade a firefighter for a building." Two disciplines govern that work: LCES and structure triage.

LCES — The Survival System, Set Before You Engage

LCES (Paul Gleason’s distillation of the 10 Standard Firefighting Orders and 18 Watch Out Situations) is the non-negotiable survival framework for any dynamic fire environment. All four elements must be in place — in order — before a crew commits to structure protection. If any one fails, the engagement stops.

L
Lookouts
Posted where they can see the fire and the crew, with communications and the authority to order immediate withdrawal. They watch the fire so working crews do not have to.
C
Communications
A clear, tested communication plan — who talks to whom, on what channel, with a defined trigger for emergency traffic — established before anyone engages.
E
Escape Routes
At least two, identified and time-tested, from every position. Re-evaluated continuously, because changing fire behavior changes the routes.
S
Safety Zones
Pre-identified areas large enough to survive a burnover without deploying a shelter — plus Temporary Refuge Areas en route. Know where they are and how long it takes to reach them.

Structure Triage — Deciding What to Defend

You cannot save every home, and trying to is how firefighters die. Structure triage matches limited resources to the structures that are both savable and safe to defend, and it is now a core company-officer skill — the subject of dedicated national training built on the recent conflagrations. Categorize every structure before committing to any.

Low priority
Defensible · Stand-Alone
Good defensible space and ignition-resistant construction; likely to survive with little or no help. Patrol, note it, and move resources to where they matter more.
Commit — if safe
Defensible · Prep & Defend
Savable with crews, and there is an adequate safety zone and escape routes for firefighters to stay and work. This is where active structure protection is justified.
Do not stay
Non-Defensible · Prep & Leave
Conditions are unsafe to commit. Do rapid prep only if time allows — close it up, move fuels — then leave. "Check and go," never "stay and hope."
No-go
Non-Defensible · Rescue Drive-By
Already lost, or no safe access and no safety zone. Bypass it. The only reason to slow down is a life-safety contact — not the structure.

The maneuvers that keep crews alive in the interface are all built on staying mobile: bump and run (defend briefly, then move with the fire front, constantly re-identifying escape routes and safety zones), anchor and hold, tactical patrol, and fire-front-following. Named engagement tools — PACE for the tactical engagement process, DRAW-D for levels of engagement — force the officer to pre-decide go/no-go before conditions force the decision. The constant is reassessment: fire behavior changes fast, and the tactic that was right ten minutes ago can become the one that gets a crew burned over.

The Risk Profile Holds — No Structure Is Worth a Firefighter

WUI is where the risk-management profile is tested hardest. Risk a lot for a savable life; risk a little, briefly, for a defensible structure with a safety zone and escape routes; risk nothing for a structure that is already lost or cannot be defended safely. The first and foremost intent of structure defense is that every firefighter goes home — the buildings are secondary. An officer who can say that plainly at a board, and back it with LCES and triage, is demonstrating exactly the judgment the assessors are scoring.

⏱ 12 min🎯 Scenarios

Company operations scenarios at Lieutenant level test whether the candidate can apply size-up, resource management, tactical decision-making, and crew safety in a coordinated response rather than as separate isolated skills.

Oral Board Prompt

"You are the first-arriving Lieutenant at a two-story wood frame residential structure with heavy smoke showing from multiple upper floor windows. Your crew consists of three firefighters. A neighbor tells you the family is home and may not have gotten out. Radio traffic indicates the next closest engine is 6 minutes out. Walk me through your initial actions."

3/5 Response
"I would size up the structure and deploy my crew for interior attack and search. I would establish command, request additional units, and initiate primary search of the upper floor while the engine crew advances the attack line."
5/5 Response
"My size-up in the first 60 seconds: two-story wood frame — lightweight truss construction possible given the neighborhood build date, I need to confirm. Multiple upper floor smoke — established fire, ventilation status unknown from this angle. Three-person crew — I have an attack line and a search team but not simultaneously with two-in-two-out for interior. I announce command on radio and immediately request a second alarm for the reported victim.

360 first — I need the back side before I commit my crew. This takes 30 seconds and tells me if victims are visible, if there's a fire escape, if the back is actively involved. While I'm doing that, my engineer is pulling a charged line to the front door and confirming water supply.

Interior entry decision: if the 360 shows tenable access and the smoke conditions support survivable interior conditions, I make entry with a two-person search team while my engineer maintains exterior with the charged line — two-in with one-out, requesting a fourth person for the second pair before entry if time allows. If the structure shows significant upper floor involvement and no viable victim window, I hold exterior and coordinate with the incoming engine for simultaneous entry-and-attack.

I am not sending my crew into a ventilation-controlled environment above an established fire without water coordinated for entry. Six minutes for the second engine changes the tactical calculus — the difference between waiting and entering may determine whether we maintain two-in-two-out. I make that decision based on visible victim probability, not urgency pressure."

The 5/5 Command Answer — Name the System and the Mode

On a company-operations scenario, assessors are listening for command literacy. A 3/5 says "I would take command and start an attack." A 5/5 names the system and the mode: "As the initial IC I am in Fast-Attack mode — portable radio, with my crew — because there is a reported savable life. I will transfer to a stationary command position when Engine 2's officer arrives. My risk decision is governed by the risk profile: I will risk a lot for a savable life, within two-in/two-out and a designated RIT."
