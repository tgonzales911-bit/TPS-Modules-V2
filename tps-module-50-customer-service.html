Customer Service
+ Citizen Complaints

The public cannot judge your tactics. They judge how your crew treated them, and as the company officer that is yours to own. This chapter covers the service standard and how to set it, the angry citizen at the station door, the formal complaint that has to go up the chain, and how to correct your own crew when the service was poor. It prepares you for the citizen role play and for the first real complaint that lands on your desk.

Chapter 45 · Lesson 1 of 5

Every Contact —
Is the Department

⏱ 9 min 🤝 Service as a Scored Behavior

The public does not grade your fire attack. They cannot tell a good one from a bad one. They grade how your crew treated them, and they remember it for twenty years. The late Chief Alan Brunacini built a career on that point: the customer's experience is part of the job, and most of it comes down to being nice when it would be easier not to be.

As a firefighter you were responsible for your own conduct. As a company officer you own the conduct of four people on every contact, including the ones you were not standing next to. When a citizen says "the fire department was rude to my mother," they mean your engine.

Assessors ask about customer service for two reasons. It shows how you represent the organization, and it shows whether you will correct your own crew when the service was poor.

Who the Customer Is

External
The person who called
The patient, the family, the business owner, the driver stuck behind your rig. Most of them are having one of the worst days of their year.
External
The person who did not call
Neighbors watching from the sidewalk, the passerby filming, the taxpayer who only ever sees you at the grocery store.
Internal
Other crews and divisions
The oncoming shift, the mechanic, dispatch, the prevention bureau. How you treat internal customers sets how your crew treats external ones.
Partner
Other agencies
Hospital staff, law enforcement, public works, the ambulance crew. Their opinion of your department is formed on scene with you.

The Three Standards

You do not need a customer service manual. You need three standards your crew can remember at three in the morning.

1
Solve the Problem They Called About
Competence first. Nothing else matters if the work was poor.
2
Treat Them the Way You Would Want Your Family Treated
Tone, patience, and explaining what you are doing. This is where most complaints start.
3
Leave It Better Than You Found It
Lock the door, move the furniture back, tell them what happens next, give them a number to call. Thirty seconds that people remember.

What Assessors Are Scoring

Scored Behavior 1
Represents the Organization
Speaks as "we." Does not blame policy, dispatch, or another shift in front of the citizen.
Scored Behavior 2
Listens Before Explaining
Lets the person finish. Explanations offered too early sound like excuses.
Scored Behavior 3
Takes Action or Routes It
Fixes what is within authority, and gets the rest to the right person with a name and a time.
Scored Behavior 4
Follows Up With the Crew
Addresses the behavior with the member involved. A candidate who soothes the citizen and never talks to the crew scores a 3.
The Core Idea of This Chapter

Service is a standard, the same as PPE or apparatus checks. You set it, you model it, and you correct it when it slips. The board wants to hear that you treat it that way.

The Angry Citizen —
L·A·S·T in Real Time

⏱ 11 min 🎙 Role Play & Real Life

A man walks up the apron and he is already yelling. Your engine blocked his driveway for forty minutes. Or the crew tracked mud through his mother's house. Or nobody told him which hospital his wife went to. This shows up in assessment centers as a citizen role play, and it shows up at your station door a few times a year.

The instinct is to explain. Do not start there. An upset person cannot hear an explanation until they believe they have been heard. Use a four-step service recovery sequence that is common across service professions.

L·A·S·T

L
Listen — All the Way to the End
Step toward them, not behind the rig. Let them finish without correcting facts. Take a note if you can; writing it down shows you are taking it seriously. Then say it back: "So the engine blocked you in and nobody came to tell you how long it would be."
A
Apologize — For the Experience
"I am sorry that happened, and I am sorry nobody talked to you." You are apologizing for their experience, which is real whether or not anyone did anything wrong. You are not admitting a policy violation or assigning fault to a member.
S
Solve — Or Route It
Fix what you have authority to fix right now. For everything else, tell them exactly who will handle it and when they will hear back. Then ask: "What would make this right for you?" Most people ask for less than you expect.
T
Thank — And Follow Up
"Thank you for coming to tell me directly. I would rather hear it from you." Then do what you said, and make the follow-up call yourself.

Staying Regulated

The CALM Model from Chapter 6 applies here exactly as it does with a subordinate. Lower your volume as theirs rises. Keep your hands visible and your stance open. If they are too escalated to talk, name it without judgment: "I want to hear all of this. Can we step over here out of the bay?" If there is any threat to safety, the conversation is over and you follow your department's procedure. Service does not require accepting abuse.

What It Sounds Like

Citizen Complaint at the Station · Lieutenant Level
Citizen
"Your truck sat across my driveway for forty minutes yesterday! I missed picking up my kid. Nobody said a word to me." (Loud, specific, legitimate)
Officer
"I want to hear this. I am Lieutenant Reyes, I was the officer on that engine. Tell me what happened from your side." (Identifies self, invites the full account)
Citizen
"I stood there waving at your guys and they just ignored me."
Officer
"So you were blocked in for forty minutes, you tried to get our attention, and nobody came over. I am sorry. That should not have happened and it is on me as the officer." (Says it back, apologizes for the experience, owns it)
Citizen
"Well... what was even going on over there?" (Volume drops)
Officer
"We had a patient in cardiac arrest two doors down and we needed the engine close. That explains where we parked. It does not explain why nobody talked to you, and that is the part I am going to fix with my crew. Is there anything from yesterday I can help with now?" (Explanation only after being heard, then Solve)
Assessor
Listened fully. Did not defend first. Owned the gap. Explained without excusing. Committed to follow up with the crew.
❌ Defends First — Loses Points
"Sir, we were on an emergency call. We park where we need to park. That is state law." Accurate, and the citizen leaves angrier and calls the chief's office.
✅ Hears First — Scores High
"You were stuck for forty minutes and nobody told you anything. I am sorry. Tell me the rest." Same facts will come out later, after the person is able to hear them.

The Formal Complaint —
Intake Without Investigation

⏱ 10 min 📝 Process & Protection

Some complaints are not fixed with a conversation. An allegation of misconduct, missing property, an inappropriate comment, rough treatment of a patient. The moment a complaint moves from "I was unhappy" to "your member did something wrong," your role changes. You are no longer the fixer. You are the intake point for a process that protects the citizen, the member and the department.

Company officers get this wrong in two directions. Some dismiss it at the door to protect their crew. Others launch their own investigation and compromise the real one. Both hurt the member they were trying to help.

The Intake Steps

1
Receive it respectfully
Same L·A·S·T opening. Thank them for bringing it forward. Never talk a person out of filing a complaint.
Every complaint is taken
2
Get the facts, not the verdict
Who, what, when, where, and how to reach them. Write down their words. Do not ask leading questions and do not offer your opinion of what happened.
Their account, in their words
3
Explain what happens next
Tell them the complaint goes to your Battalion Chief, that it will be reviewed under department procedure, and that someone will contact them. Do not promise an outcome.
Process, not promises
4
Notify up the same shift
Call your BC. Do not sit on it until morning and do not let the chief hear it from the citizen first.
Same shift, by phone
5
Document and preserve
Write a factual memo while it is fresh: what was reported, what you did, who you notified and when. Preserve anything relevant such as the run report.
Facts only, no conclusions

What You Do Not Do

Do Not
Investigate your own crew
Do not interview the member, gather statements or review their phone. Your department has a process and, in many places, members have procedural rights during an inquiry. Follow your policy and labor agreement.
Do Not
Pre-judge either way
"My guys would never do that" and "I knew he would do something like this" both end up in a deposition.
Do Not
Discuss it around the table
The accused member hears about it through the proper channel, not at dinner.
Do Not
Allow retaliation
No comments to or about the citizen, no change in service to that address. Say this out loud to the crew if you have to.

Taking Care of Your Member

Following the process is how you take care of your member. A fair, documented process is the thing that clears a firefighter who did nothing wrong. Tell the member what you can: that a complaint was received, that it is being handled under policy, that you expect professionalism while it runs, and that support is available. Check on them. An open complaint is one of the more stressful things a firefighter goes through.

Just Culture Connection

When the review is finished, the response depends on what the behavior was. An honest mistake is consoled and the system is fixed. A shortcut is coached. Conscious disregard is disciplined. Chapter 26 and Chapter 47 cover that sorting in detail. Your intake should never assume which one it is.

Service on Scene —
Coaching the Crew

⏱ 10 min 🚒 Daily Practice

Most complaints are prevented, not resolved. They are prevented on scene by small behaviors that take seconds, and by an officer who notices when those behaviors slip.

The Calls That Test It

Test 1
The low-acuity call at 0300
The stubbed toe, the third lift assist this week at the same address. The crew is tired and it shows in their faces before anyone speaks. This is where your standard is real or it is not.
Test 2
The frequent caller
Frustration is understandable. Contempt is not acceptable. Many departments have a referral path for frequent callers. Use it, and treat the person with dignity while you do.
Test 3
The family on the worst day
After a death or a serious fire, what the family remembers is whether someone explained what was happening and stayed an extra minute.
Test 4
The camera
Assume every scene is recorded. The standard is not "behave because you are being filmed." The standard is to behave so that it never matters.

Small Behaviors That Carry the Weight

Introduce yourself by name. Ask permission before moving through the house. Explain what you are doing as you do it. Tell the family which hospital and how to get there. Wipe your boots. Before you leave, ask whether there is anything else you can do. None of this slows the call down.

Setting the Standard

Say it on day one with a new crew, in plain words: "On every call we solve the problem, we treat people like family, and we leave it better than we found it. If I see something different, we will talk about it that shift." Then model it at 0300, when they are watching to see if you meant it.

Correcting It — The Same Day

When service slips, address it that shift with SBI: Situation, Behavior, Impact. Keep it about the behavior.

❌ Character Attack — Loses Points
"You were a jerk to that lady. You need to fix your attitude." Not specific, not behavioral, and the firefighter will argue the label instead of hearing the point.
✅ SBI Coaching — Scores High
"On the lift assist this morning, when Mrs. Alvarez asked if we were coming back tonight, you sighed and said 'probably.' She apologized to us three times after that. She is scared to call now, and that is the opposite of what we want. What was going on?" Specific, observable, tied to impact, and it ends with a question.

Then listen. Run your 3 U's. A tired firefighter on a fourth night call needs a different conversation than one who has decided that certain patients do not deserve courtesy. The first is a coaching moment and maybe a wellness check. The second is a standards conversation, and it gets documented.

Catch Them Doing It Right

Use SBI for good service too. "When you sat down next to that man's wife and explained the cardiac monitor, she stopped shaking. That is exactly what I want this crew known for." Reinforced behavior repeats.

L·E·A·D
Applied to Customer Service

⏱ 9 min ⚡ Full Integration

Customer service questions come in three forms: the philosophy question, the angry citizen scenario, and the crew behavior scenario. The structure is the same for all three.

L·E·A·D on a Service Question

L
Label
Usually Communication. A misconduct allegation is Ethics & Integrity. A crew member's behavior is a Personnel Issue. Label it before you answer, because the framework chain changes.
E
Engage
STAR-P for "tell us about a time." For "what would you do," walk L·A·S·T or the intake steps in order.
A
Apply
Show both halves every time: what you do for the citizen, and what you do with the crew afterward.
D
Deliver
Close on Service, Empathy or Trust, tied to the scenario.

The Complete Response

Model Response · "A citizen comes to the station angry about how your crew treated them"
Citizen first
"I would step outside or into the office with them so we are not in front of the crew. I introduce myself, ask them to tell me what happened, and I listen without interrupting. I say it back so they know I have it right, and I apologize for the experience they had."
Solve or route
"If it is something I can fix, I fix it then. If it is an allegation of misconduct, I tell them I am taking it to my Battalion Chief today, that it will be reviewed under our procedure, and that they will hear back. I get their contact information and I thank them for coming to me."
Chain
"I call my BC that shift so they hear it from me, and I write up what was reported and what I did."
Crew
"If it is a service issue and not a formal complaint, I talk to the member privately that same day. I describe the specific behavior and the impact, and I ask what was going on before I decide what it was. Then I restate the standard and follow up."
Principle
"Service is why this job exists. People call us on their worst day, and how we treat them is the one part of the call they are qualified to judge. As the officer, that standard is mine to set and mine to protect."
Assessor
Private setting. Listened first. Routed correctly. Notified up. Addressed the crew. Value close earned by the answer.

Common Scenario Types

Scenario
Driveway or parking complaint
L·A·S·T. Explain after listening. Look for the real gap, which is usually that nobody communicated.
Scenario
Rudeness on a medical call
L·A·S·T with the citizen, SBI and the 3 U's with the member.
Scenario
Property damage during operations
Listen, explain why it was necessary, and know your department's claims process so you can route it.
Scenario
Allegation of theft or misconduct
Intake, not investigation. Notify immediately. Protect both parties.
Scenario
Social media post about your crew
Do not respond online. Notify the chain and the PIO per Chapter 21. Address the underlying behavior if it was real.

Your Practice Target for This Chapter

Run the angry citizen scenario out loud twice: once in The Office with a live character, and once recorded on your own. In the recording, count the seconds before you start explaining. If it is under thirty, you are defending too early.
