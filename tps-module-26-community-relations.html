Chapter 21 · Lesson 1 of 6

Why Community Relations
Is Scored at Every Rank

⏱ 14 min 🏛️ Foundation + Context

Most candidates treat community relations as a box to check — name a program, mention Fire Prevention Week, close with a principle. Assessors know this pattern and it scores 3/5 reliably. The candidate who understands why community relations is assessed — what organizational function it serves and what leadership capacity it reveals — can speak about it with the depth that separates 4/5 from 5/5.

Why Assessors Score Community Relations

Community relations is not a public affairs add-on to the fire service mission. It is central to it. The department's ability to operate — its budget, its staffing, its public support for new stations and apparatus — depends entirely on the community's trust and understanding of what the fire service does and why it matters. The officer who builds and maintains that trust is protecting the organization's operational capacity as surely as any tactical decision.

Assessors score community relations because it reveals three specific leadership capacities that cannot be easily faked:

SRV
Service Orientation — Do You See the Community as Your Client?
The candidate who approaches community relations as an obligation or a performance is demonstrating something about how they see the public — as an audience to manage, not a community to serve. The candidate who speaks about it with genuine investment is demonstrating the Service element of T·C·H·O·E·D·S in its most visible form. Assessors score this distinction consistently.
SYS
Systems Thinking — Does the Candidate Connect Crew Behavior to Organizational Reputation?
Every interaction a crew has with a community member — whether at a routine call, during a public education event, or in casual conversation — either builds or erodes the department's relationship with that community. The Lieutenant who understands this is managing the department's most important long-term asset. That understanding is what assessors are testing when they ask community relations questions.
PRO
Proactive Leadership — Does the Candidate Wait for Problems or Create Conditions?
The reactive officer deals with community complaints after they arise. The proactive officer creates the relationships and programs that prevent complaints from arising in the first place — and that generate community support when difficult organizational decisions need public backing. This proactive vs reactive distinction maps directly to the locus of control framework from Module 24 and is consistently scored at every rank.

What NFPA 1021 Requires at the Company Officer Level

NFPA 1021 Standard for Fire Officer Professional Qualifications identifies specific community relations competencies at the Fire Officer I level. JPR 5.6 requires the Fire Officer I candidate to demonstrate the ability to initiate action on a community need by coordinating public information activities and participating in community and government relations functions.

This is not a vague expectation — it names three distinct competencies: public information, community relations, and government relations. Each has a specific behavioral expression at the Lieutenant level that assessors look for.

Public Information
Communicating the Department's Work
What the department does, how it does it, and why it matters — communicated clearly to community members, media, and elected officials. At the Lieutenant level, this includes managing public messaging during incidents, handling media at scene, and participating in department information activities.
Community Relations
Building the Relationship Before the Call
The ongoing relationship between the company and its service area — built through presence, familiarity, trust, and service. Community relations at the Lieutenant level means knowing your community, its vulnerabilities, its concerns, and its character well enough to serve it effectively and to advocate for it within the organization.
Government Relations
Understanding Organizational Context
At the FO I level, government relations competency means understanding how the department relates to its governing structure — city council, city management, boards and commissions — and being able to explain that relationship accurately to community members who ask about budget decisions, staffing levels, or service policies.
The Integration Point
T·C·H·O·E·D·S at the Community Level
Every element of T·C·H·O·E·D·S has a community relations expression. Transparency means honest communication during difficult incidents. Courage means delivering difficult public messages clearly. Humility means listening before responding. Service means prioritizing community need above convenience. Dedication means showing up for community programs consistently, not just during evaluation periods.
The Assessment Center Implication

When an assessor asks "How do you approach community relations at your station?" the 3/5 answer names programs and activities. The 4/5 answer names programs and connects them to organizational goals — trust-building, risk reduction, public support for the department's mission. The 5/5 answer does both of those things and names the leadership principle that drives the approach — Service, Transparency, or Dedication — and connects community relations work to the candidate's identity as a leader. That integration is what this module is designed to build.

Mapping Your Stakeholders —
Who Needs What from Your Station

⏱ 16 min 🗺️ Stakeholder Analysis

Effective community relations begins with knowing who you are in a relationship with. Most officers think of "the community" as a single undifferentiated audience — and then wonder why generic programs produce generic results. The Lieutenant who maps their stakeholders — who they are, what they need, how they interact with the fire service — delivers community relations work that actually connects.

The Company Officer's Stakeholder Map

🏠
Residents
Homeowners, renters, families. They want to know the department is present, capable, and on their side. Most interactions are during emergency response.
🏢
Businesses
Owners and managers. Pre-incident plans, inspections, and fire prevention are the primary touchpoints. Business relationships often generate the most durable community support.
🏫
Schools
Teachers, administrators, students. Fire prevention education and station tours. School relationships build community trust across generations.
🙏
Faith Communities
Churches, mosques, temples, synagogues. High-trust organizations with established community networks. Especially important for reaching vulnerable populations.
🏥
Healthcare Partners
Hospitals, clinics, dialysis centers, nursing facilities. Life safety and evacuation planning. EMS protocols and patient advocacy.
👥
Community Organizations
Neighborhood associations, civic groups, HOAs, nonprofits. Organized voices for specific communities. Key partners for CRR initiatives.

Vulnerable Populations — The Lieutenant's Responsibility

Every service area contains populations that face elevated risk and reduced capacity to protect themselves. NFPA 1300 and fire service research consistently identify these groups as deserving specific community relations attention — not out of charity, but because effective emergency services requires knowing and serving the full community.

ELD
Elderly Residents — Elevated Risk, Reduced Response Capacity
Older adults are disproportionately represented in fire fatalities. Mobility limitations, hearing/vision loss, and cognitive changes reduce escape time and increase injury severity. Specific programs: smoke alarm installation for those who cannot self-install, fire safety home visits, evacuation plan development. Relationships with senior centers and assisted living facilities are primary access points.
SES
Lower-Income Households — Resource Gaps and Housing Vulnerabilities
Lower-income households are more likely to have non-functioning smoke alarms, older electrical systems, space heaters as primary heat sources, and overcrowding — all significant fire risk factors. Community relations at the station level means knowing these risk concentrations in your service area and directing prevention resources toward them rather than toward more convenient or visible populations.
LNG
Language Access — Non-English Speakers Need Services Too
Emergency communications, fire safety education, and public information all require language access to be effective. This means knowing the primary non-English languages in your service area, having translated materials available, and connecting with community organizations that serve non-English speaking populations as trusted intermediaries for fire safety information.
DIS
Individuals with Disabilities — Special Needs Registry and Pre-Incident Planning
Many jurisdictions maintain special needs registries for residents with mobility, cognitive, or medical needs that affect emergency response. The Lieutenant who knows which residents in their service area are registered — and what their specific needs are — is in a fundamentally different response position than the one who discovers those needs at 3am during a working structure fire.

Building the Stakeholder Relationship — The Station's Role

Stakeholder relationships are not built through programs. They are built through consistent presence, reliable service, and genuine engagement over time. Programs are the mechanism — trust is the product. The Lieutenant who understands this invests in relationships continuously rather than activating them when inspection season or budget hearings approach.

The Pre-Incident Planning Connection

Community relations and pre-incident planning are not separate functions — they are the same function seen from different angles. The Lieutenant who has a strong relationship with a business owner gets walk-throughs, floor plan access, and honest information about storage and hazardous materials because that relationship exists before the business calls 911. The Lieutenant who shows up only when required gets what's legally required and nothing more. Which officer provides better emergency service to that business?

Assessment Center Application

Stakeholder mapping is one of the most powerful community relations tools in assessment center responses because it demonstrates systematic thinking rather than anecdote. "I would engage the community" scores 3/5. "I would identify the three highest-risk populations in this service area — elderly residents in the apartment complexes on 5th, the light manufacturing corridor, and the ESL-speaking neighborhood north of Main — and build specific outreach protocols for each" scores 4/5 and opens the door to a 5/5 integration with CRR and T·C·H·O·E·D·S.

Community Programs —
Building Relationships Through Consistent Presence

⏱ 16 min 📋 Programs + Partnerships

Community programs are the structured mechanisms through which the Lieutenant builds relationships, delivers fire safety education, and creates department presence in the service area. Knowing the programs is not enough — assessors consistently probe for why a program exists, what it accomplishes, how it connects to organizational goals, and how the Lieutenant manages it within the constraints of operational responsibility.

Core Community Programs — What the Lieutenant Manages

SPR
Smoke Alarm Programs — The Highest-ROI Prevention Tool
Smoke alarm installation programs — particularly those targeting elderly, lower-income, and vulnerable populations — consistently show the highest return on investment of any residential fire prevention intervention. NFPA data shows that homes with working smoke alarms have a 50% lower risk of fire death. At the station level, this means coordinating with community partners (faith organizations, senior centers, housing authorities) for access, managing equipment and installation logistics, and tracking outcomes. The Lieutenant's role is coordination, not just participation.
FPW
Fire Prevention Week — The Annual Platform
Held each October in the week that includes October 9th (anniversary of the Great Chicago Fire), Fire Prevention Week is the highest-profile annual community relations platform in the fire service. Station-level activities typically include school presentations, public open houses, media engagement, and smoke alarm distribution. The Lieutenant who treats Fire Prevention Week as an isolated annual event misses its relationship-building potential — it is most effective when it deepens relationships already built throughout the year, not initiates them.
CPST
Child Passenger Safety — A Broader Public Safety Role
Many fire departments run or participate in child passenger safety seat inspection programs — either through dedicated technicians or broader community events. These programs extend the department's community safety role beyond fire and EMS, build relationships with new parents and young families, and create sustained community touchpoints over time. The Lieutenant who oversees certified technicians on their crew needs to understand the program's operational requirements and community relationship function.
STR
Station Tours — Demystifying the Fire Service
Station tours for school groups, community organizations, and individuals build familiarity and trust in ways that no formal program can replicate. A child who has touched the apparatus, worn the gear, and spoken with the crew does not fear the firefighters who respond to their home. An adult who has met the Lieutenant personally does not assume malice or incompetence when something goes wrong. Tours are low-cost, high-impact community relations investments that require only organized commitment from the company.
CPR
Community CPR Training — Creating Lay Responders
CPR and basic life safety training programs — Hands-Only CPR events, Stop the Bleed instruction, Hands-Only CPR at community events — serve dual purposes: they build community preparedness (and therefore improve patient outcomes before EMS arrives) and they build department relationships with community members in a context of genuine mutual benefit. Participants often become advocates for the department in community discussions about funding and operations.

Managing Programs — The Lieutenant's Coordination Role

Community programs require management, not just participation. The Lieutenant who builds an effective community relations program addresses four operational questions:

Resource Allocation
Time, Personnel, Equipment
Community programs compete with training time, maintenance schedules, and rest requirements. The Lieutenant who treats community relations as extra work creates a crew that resents it. The one who integrates it as part of the station's professional identity creates a crew that sustains it.
Documentation
Tracking Outcomes and Justifying Investment
Programs that can be measured — smoke alarms installed, households reached, students educated, CPR certifications delivered — can be defended in budget discussions and scaled based on evidence. The Lieutenant who tracks outcomes is building the organizational case for continued investment.
Partnership Development
Leveraging Community Organizations
The most effective community programs are delivered through partnerships that extend the department's reach. A faith organization that co-sponsors smoke alarm installation events provides access to populations the department cannot reach alone. A school that integrates fire safety into curriculum provides sustained education. Partnerships multiply impact.
Crew Engagement
Building a Community-Serving Culture
The Lieutenant sets the tone. A crew that hears their officer talk about community relations as "soft" work develops a corresponding attitude. A crew that watches their officer treat every community interaction with professionalism and investment develops a professional identity that extends to every call, every conversation, every encounter.

Media Relations —
Speaking on Record, At Scene, and Online

⏱ 16 min 📰 Media · Social Media · PIO Protocol

Media interactions are among the highest-stakes community relations events a Lieutenant encounters — and among the least prepared for. A single poorly worded comment at a working fire can damage department credibility, compromise an investigation, or create legal exposure for the organization. A well-handled media interaction builds public confidence and demonstrates the professionalism assessors are looking for when they ask how you handle the media.

The PIO Relationship — Know Your Chain Before the Camera Finds You

Most departments have a Public Information Officer (PIO) — a designated spokesperson who manages media relations at significant incidents. The Lieutenant's primary media relations responsibility is knowing and respecting this chain of communication. Unauthorized statements — even accurate ones — can undermine coordinated messaging, contradict an ongoing investigation, or create liability the officer did not intend.

The Protocol Before Anything Else

Before any media interaction at a significant incident: notify your chain of command that media is present. Determine whether a PIO is en route or has been designated. Confirm what information has been authorized for release. The five minutes you spend getting clarity on this before speaking protects the organization and protects you. The Lieutenant who freelances with media at a working fire because "they seemed friendly" has created a problem their chief will be managing for the next three days.

What to Say — and What Never to Say — At Scene

Never Say at Scene
Cause speculation: "It looks like it started in the kitchen" — investigators have not confirmed cause.

Injury/fatality details: "We found one victim" — before family notification is complete.

Dollar estimates: "The house is a total loss, probably $400,000" — not your assessment to make.

Criticism of others: "If they'd had working smoke alarms..." — blaming victims is never appropriate.

Operational criticism: "We could have used two more units on this one" — resource discussions happen internally.
Appropriate at Scene
Confirmed facts only: "We responded to a report of a structure fire at this address at [time]."

Current status: "The fire is under control / we are still working the scene."

PIO referral: "For additional information, our Public Information Officer will be available to speak with you at [location/time]."

Safety messaging: "We want to remind residents that working smoke alarms save lives."

Honest uncertainty: "I'm not able to confirm that at this time" is always appropriate.

Social Media — The Lieutenant's New Media Responsibility

Social media has fundamentally changed the media relations landscape for company officers. The bystander with a smartphone is now a real-time broadcaster. The crew member who posts incident photos without authorization creates liability. The officer who comments on department policy on a personal account creates organizational problems. And the department's own social media presence is increasingly a primary community relations channel that the Lieutenant may be asked to contribute to.

POL
Know Your Department's Social Media Policy
Most departments have specific policies about what officers and crew members may post about department operations, incidents, and personnel. Not knowing this policy is not a defense. The Lieutenant is responsible for knowing it, following it, and ensuring their crew knows and follows it. Social media violations can result in disciplinary action, compromise investigations, and generate significant media attention of an unwanted kind.
INC
Incident Photography and Video — Chain of Authorization
Incident photos and video — even taken for training or documentation purposes — may capture patient information, structural vulnerabilities, or tactical details that should not be publicly shared. The authorization chain for incident photography typically runs through the PIO or department administration. Crew members who share incident photos on personal accounts "just to show what we do" have likely violated both department policy and potentially HIPAA.
PER
Personal Accounts and Professional Identity
Officers who comment on department policy, local politics, or community issues on personal social media accounts do not separate their personal and professional identities in the reader's mind. "Lt. Johnson from Station 7 says the department is underfunded" reads as a department position statement, not a personal opinion. The Courage element of T·C·H·O·E·D·S applies here: if the message is worth sharing publicly, share it through appropriate organizational channels.

The On-Camera Fundamentals

When authorized to speak on camera — at community events, during planned media interactions, or in appropriate incident circumstances — the Lieutenant applies the same communication principles that produce 4/5 oral board scores:

  • One message, repeated clearly. Identify the single most important thing you want the community to know and say it directly, not buried in context.
  • Specific and factual. Concrete details are more credible than general statements. "We installed 47 smoke alarms in this neighborhood last month" is more compelling than "we've been doing a lot of outreach."
  • Calm and unhurried. Officers who speak quickly under media pressure appear anxious. The professional pause before answering is as appropriate on camera as it is in an oral board.
  • Redirect when appropriate. "I'm not able to confirm that, but what I can tell you is..." returns the conversation to your message without appearing evasive.

The Media-Relations Phrase Bank — On the Record
On record, every word is the department’s word. The same situation, spoken two ways, either protects trust or creates a liability. The weak column is how reputations get damaged at a microphone; the strong column stays accurate, stays in your lane, and protects the people involved.
Off the rails
“It looks like the fire started from a space heater they left running.”
On the record, in your lane
“The cause is under investigation. I won’t speculate — the fire marshal will release findings once they’re confirmed.”
Why: Speculating on cause on record is how departments get corrected — or sued. Stay with what is confirmed.
Off the rails
“There were two victims, an older couple — I think the husband didn’t make it.”
On the record, in your lane
“I’m not able to release victim information. Identification and notification go through the proper channels.”
Why: Victim details are protected and belong to investigators and family notification — never the scene.
Off the rails
“No comment.” (and walk away)
On the record, in your lane
“Here’s what I can confirm right now: [facts in your lane]. For anything beyond that, our PIO will follow up.”
Why: A flat ‘no comment’ reads as evasive. Give the confirmable facts; refer the rest.
Off the rails
“We got here as fast as we could — dispatch was slow getting us the address.”
On the record, in your lane
“Our crews responded and operated under the conditions on scene. I’ll let the after-action review speak to the timeline.”
Why: Never put another unit, dispatch, or agency on blast on camera. The department speaks with one voice.
Off the rails
“Ma’am, you need to get back — we’re busy here.”
On the record, in your lane
“I understand this is your home — I need you with me at the command post so I can keep you safe and informed.”
Why: Even under pressure, dignity is a trust deposit. The same instruction, delivered with respect, builds instead of burns.
Off the rails
Posting a personal opinion about the call from your own account.
On the record, in your lane
Routing anything public through the PIO; keeping personal accounts out of department incidents entirely.
Why: Your personal account is never the department’s channel. Online is on record — permanently.

Community Risk Reduction
at the Lieutenant Level

⏱ 18 min 🌍 NFPA 1300 · Five E's · Data-Driven Prevention

Community Risk Reduction is the framework that connects community relations to the department's strategic mission. The distinction between an officer who does community outreach and an officer who practices CRR is the same as the distinction between an officer who responds to fires and an officer who commands incidents: the same activities, organized by a strategic framework, produce fundamentally different results.

CRR at the Company Level — What NFPA 1300 Expects

NFPA 1300 (Standard on Community Risk Assessment and Community Risk Reduction Plan Development, 2020) establishes the framework for systematic risk reduction at the organizational level. At the company officer level, CRR practice means applying this framework within the station's service area — identifying the highest-risk populations and conditions, deploying the most effective interventions, and measuring results.

The Five E's — The CRR Intervention Framework

CRR interventions fall into five categories — the Five E's — that represent different mechanisms for reducing community risk. Module 12 covers the Five E's in depth in the context of strategic planning. At the Lieutenant level, the relevant application is knowing which E's are within company officer authority and how they connect to specific risk profiles in the service area.

E1
Education — Building Community Knowledge and Behavior
Fire safety education — school presentations, CPR training, smoke alarm demonstrations, cooking fire prevention — is the most common E at the company level. Education changes knowledge and, over time, behavior. Assessors score whether candidates understand education's limitations (knowledge does not always produce behavior change) and strengths (cost-effective, relationship-building, scalable through partners).
E2
Engineering — Changing the Physical Environment
Engineering interventions change the environment to reduce risk regardless of human behavior — sprinkler systems, smoke alarms, fire-resistant construction, automatic door closers. At the company officer level, engineering engagement happens primarily through pre-incident planning (noting the presence or absence of suppression systems), fire inspections (identifying code violations), and advocacy for local ordinances requiring engineering solutions in high-risk occupancies.
E3
Enforcement — Using Code and Regulation
Enforcement interventions use the legal system to compel risk reduction — fire codes, building codes, occupancy regulations, citation and penalty structures. Company officers are often involved in enforcement through fire inspections and code compliance activities. The Lieutenant who approaches enforcement as a community service (helping the occupant understand and correct violations) rather than purely as a punitive function builds better long-term relationships and better compliance.
E4
Economic Incentives — Making Risk Reduction Financially Attractive
Insurance discounts for sprinkler installation, grant programs for smoke alarm distribution, tax incentives for fire-resistant renovation — economic incentives make risk-reducing behavior more financially attractive. At the company level, this primarily means knowing what programs exist in your jurisdiction and connecting residents and businesses to them. The Lieutenant who knows the local home fire safety grant program is adding tangible value to every community interaction.
E5
Emergency Response — The Last Line of Defense
Emergency response is the intervention that occurs after all other E's have failed to prevent the incident. It is the most expensive and the most visible E — and the one the traditional fire service has over-invested in relative to the others. The assessment-center-ready Lieutenant frames emergency response as part of a CRR continuum, not as the department's primary product. "We protect lives and property through prevention as much as through response" is a 4/5 framing. Naming all five E's and explaining their integration is a 5/5 framing.

Data-Driven Risk Reduction — The Lt's Analytical Role

Effective CRR at the company level is not random. It is directed by data — incident history in the service area, demographic risk profiles, housing stock characteristics, code compliance history, and national research on high-risk populations and behaviors. The Lieutenant who can say "our incident data shows a concentration of cooking fires in the multi-family corridor on the east side of our service area — so we are deploying specific cooking fire prevention education and outreach to those buildings" is demonstrating the analytical capacity assessors associate with Captain-level readiness.

The Strategic Connection

CRR at the company level is the operational expression of the organizational strategy covered in Module 12 (Strategic Thinking + Planning). The department-level CRR plan sets direction; the company-level Lieutenant executes it in their service area. The Lieutenant who can connect their station's CRR activities to the department's strategic goals is demonstrating the vertical alignment that assessors score as organizational leadership — a competency explicitly associated with promotion to Captain and beyond.

The Five E’s, Applied — A Worked CRR Example
Community Risk Reduction is grounded in NFPA 1300: assess the risk through a Community Risk Assessment, then attack it with the Five E’s. No single E works alone — the strongest plans combine several. Here is how a Lieutenant applies all five to one identified risk.
Identified risk (from the CRA): a cluster of unattended-cooking fires in the Maple Street apartments, concentrated in the evening hours.
Education
Targeted cooking-safety outreach at the complex — door-to-door and in the community room, in the residents’ primary languages. Education changes behavior, but rarely on its own.
Engineering
Work with the property owner on engineered solutions: stovetop fire-suppression devices, temperature-limiting burners, and a working smoke alarm in every unit.
Enforcement
Coordinate code inspections of units and common areas — verify alarms and egress meet code, and follow up on any violations.
Economic Incentive
Pursue a Fire Prevention & Safety grant or a cost-share with the owner and insurer for the retrofits — and name the insurance and premium benefits that reward compliance.
Emergency Response
Pre-plan the complex — hydrants, access, and unit layout — so when a fire does occur, the response is fast and structured.
One risk, five levers, combined — that is a CRR plan, not a flyer. Tie it to Service and you have a 5/5 CRR answer.
The EMS Arm of CRR — CARES & Community Paramedicine

Community Risk Reduction has an EMS delivery arm: CARES (Community Assistance, Referral, and Education Services) — also called Mobile Integrated Health or Community Paramedicine. These programs target the small group of “super-utilizers” who drive a large share of 911 volume, connecting them to primary care, mental health, and social services so the right resource meets the need — and repeat calls and avoidable ER visits fall.

Designing, funding, and partnering for one of these programs is a Captain- and Chief-level task — covered in depth in the EMS Leadership and CRR Strategic chapters. At the Lieutenant level your role is upstream and just as important: recognize the pattern on a call — the frequent caller, the unmet social need, the lift-assist regular — and make the referral into the program. Spotting it and routing it is the company officer’s contribution to CRR.

Community relations scenarios appear in multiple assessment center formats — oral boards, in-baskets, written exercises, and role plays. How Community Relations Is Scored by Dimension

Judgment & Decision-Making
Decisions That Affect Community Relationship
When supervisory judgment scenarios involve community impact — a crew member who made an inappropriate comment to a resident, a team that declined a community event, a media incident at a call — community relations knowledge informs the response. The 4/5 answer addresses the immediate supervisory issue AND names the community relations principle being protected.
Interpersonal Sensitivity
Community Member Interactions as Role Plays
Community member role plays — an angry resident at a scene, a concerned parent at a school presentation, a business owner upset about an inspection finding — test whether the candidate can apply interpersonal communication skills in a community context. The dialogue template from Module 5 applies directly; the community relations layer is the organizational relationship being protected.
Leadership Philosophy
Community Relations as Identity
Leadership philosophy questions that touch community relations test whether the candidate has an integrated view of the department's public service role. The 5/5 answer connects community relations to Service and Transparency from T·C·H·O·E·D·S, to CRR's Five E's, and to the identity statement: "I am the kind of Lieutenant who treats every community interaction as a reflection of the department's commitment to the people we serve."
Administration / Management
Program Planning and Resource Deployment
Administrative scenarios about community relations test planning and resource management capacity. In-basket items about community program scheduling, budget requests for prevention materials, or media relations protocols require the candidate to demonstrate organized, systems-level thinking — not just good intentions.

Close

Scenario 2 — New to Station (Oral Board)

Prompt

"You have just been promoted to Lieutenant and assigned to a new station. How would you approach building community relations in your service area in your first 90 days?"

3/5 Response
"I would get out in the community and introduce myself. We'd do some station tours and participate in Fire Prevention Week. I'd make sure we had smoke alarms available for residents who need them and that we were visible at community events."
4/5–5/5 Response
"My first step would be assessment — reviewing incident data for the service area to identify risk concentrations, meeting with local community organizations to understand what they know about the neighborhood that we don't, and identifying the vulnerable populations that require targeted outreach. I'd also establish the media protocol with my crew from day one — who speaks at scenes, what we confirm, and who the PIO contact is. The leadership principle driving all of this: Service. My station's relationship with this community is one of the most important things I manage."

A 5/5 Answer — When It Goes Public
Scenario: A firefighter on your crew made a dismissive comment to a resident during an EMS call. The resident posted it online and it is gaining traction. As the company officer, how do you respond?
Reactive · 3/5
“I’d talk to the firefighter and tell them to be more careful. The social media side is above my pay grade, so I’d let the PIO handle that. Hopefully it blows over — people post things all the time.”
Why it caps: No framework, no diagnosis, no stakeholder awareness, and it defers the whole thing upward while hoping it “blows over” — external locus. It never addresses the resident, the crew, or the trust that was damaged.
Integrated · 5/5
“This hits three things at once — personnel, community trust, and media — so I diagnose before I act. Personnel: I run the 3 U’s. If the firefighter didn’t realize how the comment landed, that’s a coaching conversation; if it was a choice, that’s accountability — either way I own it as his officer. Community: the resident is the primary stakeholder, and repair starts with them — appropriate follow-up and, within policy, a genuine acknowledgment, because trust is rebuilt at the individual level before the public one. Media: I stay in my lane — notify my chain and the PIO immediately, post nothing, speculate on nothing, and give the PIO accurate facts so the department speaks with one voice. The throughline is Service: we exist to serve this community, and a single interaction either honors that or erodes it. Owning that repair instead of deflecting it is the standard I hold as a company officer.”
Why it scores: Diagnoses with a named framework (3 U’s), identifies the stakeholder (the resident), holds the media discipline (chain/PIO, no speculation), owns it at rank, and closes on a value anchor (Service). Personnel + community + media, integrated — that integration is the 5/5 move.
