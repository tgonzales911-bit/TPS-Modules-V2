The Core Truth
The oral board is not a test of what you know. It is a performance under observation. The candidates who score highest are not the ones who have the most knowledge — they are the ones who can deliver what they know clearly, confidently, and within a structure that assessors can score.

The Oral Board —
What Assessors Score

⏱ 18 min📊 Oral Communication
Fast Track
Frameworks + OB cards only

The oral board is the assessment exercise that carries the most weight in the most departments — and the one where candidates most consistently leave points on the table. Not because they give wrong answers, but because they deliver correct answers in a way that is difficult to score. Assessors need structure. Your job is to give it to them.

What Assessors Are Evaluating

◆ TPS FRAMEWORK
The Four Assessment Dimensions — What Every Assessor Scores
Every exercise. Every scenario. Every rank. The same eight dimensions — every time.
01
Supervisory Judgment
Diagnose before acting. 3 U's → Skill/Will → named principle close. Most heavily weighted.
Key: Name the U before any action word.
02
Interpersonal Skills
Bias awareness. Glasl phase identification. Positions vs interests. De-escalation with framework.
Key: Name the bias check explicitly.
03
Oral Communication
STAR-P structure. 50% of time in Action. Named TPS framework as closing principle.
Key: Stay in Action until you hit 50%.
04
Written Communication
Five-Paragraph Formula. Purpose statement in ¶1. Analysis depth in ¶3. Specific decisions in ¶4.
Key: Purpose statement first — always.

Content accuracy. Do your answers reflect an accurate understanding of leadership principles, supervisory judgment, and the issues the question raises? This is necessary but not sufficient.

Structural clarity. Is your answer organized in a way the assessor can follow and score? A response that covers all the right content in a disorganized way scores lower than a response that covers the most important content in a clear structure.

Key Application

Delivery. Is your pace controlled? Is your eye contact appropriate? Do you appear composed and confident — not rehearsed and robotic? Assessors are evaluating whether they would trust this person in a supervisory role. Delivery is part of that evaluation.

Completion. Did you address the question that was asked? Many candidates answer a related question — the one they prepared for — rather than the one they were given. Assessors notice this immediately.

  • Spending too long on the situation and not enough on the action and result
  • Using filler language ("um," "you know," "basically") that undermines credibility
  • Failing to close — the answer trails off rather than landing with a clear conclusion
  • Becoming visibly rattled by a supplemental question

Applying the Framework

The Frame Shift

Stop thinking of the oral board as an interview. Think of it as a leadership demonstration. You are not being asked what you would do — you are being asked to demonstrate, in five minutes, that you already think like a company officer. That frame shift changes how you prepare, how you deliver, and how you recover when something goes wrong.

The STAR-P
Method

⏱ 22 min📊 Oral Structure Framework
Fast Track
Frameworks + OB cards only

STAR-P is the TPS adaptation of the standard STAR behavioral interview method, modified for the specific demands of fire and EMS promotional assessment centers. The addition of the P — Principles — is what separates TPS-trained candidates from candidates who have learned generic interview structure.

S
Situation
Set the context briefly. Who was involved? What was the setting? What made this situation worth discussing? Keep this concise — 15 to 20 seconds maximum. Assessors do not need a narrative. They need context.
T
Task
What was your specific responsibility in this situation? What were you trying to accomplish? This is where you establish your role — not the team's role, your role. Assessors are scoring you, not your crew.
A
Action
What did you specifically do? This is the longest and most important component. Walk through your decision-making process. Name the frameworks you applied. Explain your reasoning. This is where your supervisory thinking becomes visible — and where most candidates underinvest.
R
Result
What happened? What was the outcome? If it was not a clean success — and real leadership stories rarely are — what did you learn? Assessors respect honest results more than manufactured wins. The reflection on outcome demonstrates supervisory maturity.
P
Principle
What leadership principle does this story illustrate? This is the TPS addition — and the one that most differentiates TPS-trained responses. Connecting your story to a named principle ("This experience reinforced my belief that a leader's job is to develop others, not just to solve problems") signals genuine leadership philosophy and scores in the Behavioral Consistency dimension simultaneously.
Time Allocation

A five-minute oral board response should distribute roughly as follows: S (15%), T (10%), A (50%), R (15%), P (10%). If you find yourself spending more than 20% of your time on Situation, you are spending it in the wrong place. The Action component is where assessors do their scoring.

Coach's Note

Record every STAR-P practice session. The most common error you will hear in playback is spending too long on Situation and rushing through Action. You will not hear this in the moment — you will only hear it on the recording. This is why recording yourself is non-negotiable.

The Opening and
Closing Sentence

⏱ 20 min📊 Response Architecture
Fast Track
Frameworks + OB cards only

Most candidates spend their preparation time on the content of their oral board responses. The candidates who score highest spend equal time on the architecture — specifically on how they open and close each response. The opening sentence orients the assessor. The closing sentence lands the response. Both are deliberate, practiced, and non-negotiable.

The Opening Sentence

Your opening sentence should accomplish three things in one sentence: it should name the core principle your response will illustrate, signal the structure you will use, and demonstrate leadership confidence. You have approximately eight seconds before assessors form an initial impression. The opening sentence is those eight seconds.

A strong opening sounds like this: "This question gets at something I think is fundamental to supervisory leadership — how you respond when you need to address a performance gap while maintaining the relationship and motivation of the person involved."

A weak opening sounds like this: "Um, so I was working on Engine 7, and there was this situation where one of my crew members was having some issues..."

The strong opening signals a leader. The weak opening signals a storyteller. Assessors are not scoring your stories — they are scoring your leadership framework.

The Closing Sentence

Your closing sentence should land with confidence and signal completion. It should connect your story back to the question or the principle you named at the opening, and it should leave no ambiguity about whether your answer is finished.

A strong close: "That experience shaped how I think about development — it is the officer's job to build capacity, not just to fill gaps. That is the kind of leader I am committed to being."

A weak close: "So yeah, that's basically what happened, and I think it worked out pretty well..."

Applying the Framework

The Bookend Principle

Think of your opening sentence and your closing sentence as bookends. The opening establishes the frame. The closing reinforces it. Everything between them is content. When assessors review their notes after your session, the bookends are what they remember most clearly. Invest accordingly.

Handling
Supplemental Questions

⏱ 18 min📊 Pressure Management
Fast Track
Frameworks + OB cards only

Supplemental questions are the questions assessors ask after your initial response — either to probe deeper on something you said, to challenge an assumption, or to assess how you perform when you cannot rely on a prepared response. They are not traps. They are opportunities. Candidates who handle them well frequently outscore candidates who gave better initial responses.

The Four Types of Supplemental Questions

The Depth Probe

"Can you tell me more about what you did in that situation?" This is an invitation to go deeper into your Action component. Respond by expanding on your reasoning — what were you thinking, what options did you consider, what made you choose the approach you took?

The Challenge

"Some would argue that your approach was too lenient — what would you say to that?" This is a test of your confidence and your ability to defend a position without becoming defensive. Respond by acknowledging the perspective, then explaining the reasoning behind your decision. "That's a fair observation. Here's what I was weighing..."

The Hypothetical Extension

"What would you have done if the situation had escalated further?" This is a test of your ability to think under pressure with incomplete information. Apply the same STAR-P thinking you used in your prepared response — even without a prepared story.

The Clarification Request

"What did you mean when you said...?" This is usually a gift — the assessor is giving you a chance to be clearer on something important. Respond by restating your point more precisely, not by apologizing or walking it back.

The Pause Permission

You are allowed to pause before answering a supplemental question. A brief pause — two to three seconds — is not a sign of weakness. It is a sign of someone who thinks before they speak. Take the pause. Then answer.

Coach's Note

The most dangerous supplemental question response is rushing. Candidates who rush through supplemental questions to fill silence produce disorganized answers that undermine the structured response they gave before it. Slow down. The assessors have time. Use it.

All Ranks
"An assessor asks: What would you do differently if the firefighter in your scenario filed a grievance?"
This is a Type 2 supplemental (stress test). Do not change your answer. Acknowledge the grievance process, explain how your approach is defensible because it followed the framework, and reaffirm your decision. The assessor is testing whether you fold under pressure.
Lesson 4 — Key Takeaways
Four types of supplementals: clarification (they want more detail), stress test (they challenge your answer), extension (what happens next), and pivot (entirely new angle).
Stress test supplementals are not attacks — they are opportunities. Standing firm on a well-reasoned answer scores higher than changing your answer to please the assessor.
For every supplemental: pause, identify the type, respond. Never immediately change your answer — assess whether the question requires new information or new reasoning.

Recording Yourself —
The #1 Practice Tool

⏱ 14 min📊 Practice Method
Fast Track
Frameworks + OB cards only

Of all the practice methods available to oral board candidates, recording yourself is the most effective and the most consistently avoided. Candidates avoid it because watching themselves on video is uncomfortable. That discomfort is the point — and it is the source of the information you most need before your assessment.

What You Will Hear and See

Filler words. "Um," "uh," "you know," "basically," "like" — these are invisible to you in the moment and immediately visible to assessors. You cannot eliminate what you cannot hear. The recording makes them audible.

Time distribution errors. You will almost certainly discover that you spend too long on the Situation and rush through the Action. You cannot feel this in the moment. You can see it on a recording with a stopwatch.

Closing problems. Most candidates trail off rather than close. The recording reveals whether your final sentence lands with conviction or fades into uncertainty.

Pace and composure. Speaking too fast under pressure is the most common delivery error. A recording lets you hear whether your pace is controlled or whether stress is accelerating it.

The Recording Protocol

  • Record every practice session — not just the ones that feel ready
  • Watch each recording immediately after, without waiting
  • Count filler words per response and track the number over time
  • Time each STAR-P component and check the distribution
  • Watch without sound first — evaluate posture, eye contact, and composure separately from content
The Compound Effect

Candidates who record themselves consistently improve faster than candidates who practice the same number of reps without recording. The feedback loop is the variable. Recording closes the loop. Twenty recorded reps will outperform one hundred unrecorded reps every time.
