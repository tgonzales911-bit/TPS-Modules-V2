The Captain's HR Role —
What's in Scope and What Isn't

⏱ 14 min👥 HR Scope · Authority + Limits

One of the most common errors company officers make in both actual practice and in assessment centers is either overstating their personnel authority — acting as if they can make HR decisions that belong to HR — or understating it — treating all people issues as someone else's problem. Getting the scope right is foundational to everything else in personnel management.

What the Captain Owns

Daily Performance Management
Observation, Coaching, and Standards
Setting clear performance expectations, observing whether they are being met, providing specific feedback — positive and corrective — and coaching toward improvement. This is the primary and ongoing personnel management function of the company officer. It cannot be delegated to HR.
Formal Evaluations
Completing and Delivering Written Assessments
The periodic formal assessment of member performance against established criteria. The Captain completes the evaluation, discusses it with the member, documents it, and signs it. This is an organizational responsibility — not optional, not delegable, and not properly performed if it is dishonest.
Documentation
Creating and Maintaining Performance Records
Documenting significant performance events — positive and negative — as they occur. Counseling memos, commendation documentation, incident reports involving personnel conduct, training completion records. This documentation is the foundation of both formal evaluations and any disciplinary process that may follow.
Initial Response to Issues
First-Line Problem Identification and Intervention
Identifying performance problems, behavioral issues, or attendance patterns early, and initiating the appropriate response — which may be a coaching conversation, an EAP referral, a supervisory documentation memo, or an escalation to the chain of command depending on the nature and severity of the issue.

What Belongs to HR, Legal, or the Chain of Command

DIS
Formal Discipline Beyond Verbal/Written Counseling
Suspensions, demotions, terminations, and formal disciplinary actions beyond counseling are not company officer decisions in most jurisdictions — they belong to the chain of command and HR, and often to civil service or labor relations processes. The Captain who attempts to impose formal discipline unilaterally has exceeded their authority. The one who recognizes when an issue has escalated beyond their scope and routes it appropriately is doing their job correctly.
LEG
Legal Determinations — Harassment, Discrimination, ADA, FMLA
When a personnel issue involves potential legal exposure — allegations of harassment or discrimination, medical accommodations under the ADA, leave entitlements under FMLA — the company officer's role is to identify and route, not to adjudicate. Making substantive decisions in these areas without HR involvement creates organizational liability and often harms the affected individuals. Route immediately, document the routing, and ensure HR is engaged.
EAP
Clinical Assessment and Treatment — EAP and Mental Health
When a member's performance or behavioral issues suggest an underlying mental health, substance use, or personal crisis, the company officer's role is to refer — not to diagnose or treat. The Employee Assistance Program (EAP) exists specifically for this function. The Captain who attempts to personally manage what is clinically a mental health crisis both exceeds their competence and prevents the member from accessing the support they actually need.
The Scope Principle

The company officer who acts within scope protects the organization, protects the individual member, and maintains their own credibility with HR and legal. The one who exceeds scope — acting on legal matters without HR, imposing informal discipline that bypasses the contract, or personally managing clinical issues — creates liability, undermines legitimate processes, and often makes the situation worse for the member they're trying to help. Know your scope. Route outside it clearly and promptly.

Performance Evaluations —
The Honest Assessment Your People Deserve

⏱ 16 min📋 Evaluations · Feedback · Bias

Performance evaluations are among the most important things a company officer does — and among the most frequently done poorly. The evaluation is an organizational accountability mechanism, a development tool, and a legal document. When it is inflated, vague, or dishonest, it fails all three of those functions and creates specific organizational problems that ultimately trace back to the supervisor who chose comfort over responsibility.

The Four Evaluation Failures — And Why They Happen

Inflation
Rating everyone "Exceeds Expectations" to avoid difficult conversations or maintain crew relationships.
Evaluate against the actual standard, not relative to the least capable person on the crew. An "Meets Expectations" rating that is accurate is a better gift to the member than an inflated one that prevents them from seeing where growth is needed.
Vagueness
"Performs well. Good attitude. Recommended for advancement." No specifics. No behavioral evidence. No developmental direction.
Name specific behaviors, specific incidents, specific demonstrated competencies. "Demonstrated strong incident command skills at the Maple Street MVA — organized scene, clear communication with crew, thorough documentation." Specific, behavioral, evidence-based.
Recency Bias
Rating a member based primarily on their most recent performance — either positively if they had a strong month before evaluation, or negatively if there was a recent incident.
Evaluate the full evaluation period. Maintain ongoing documentation throughout the year so the evaluation reflects 12 months of performance, not the last 30 days. This is why documentation throughout the year matters.
Surprise
Delivering a below-standard evaluation that the member did not see coming — because the Captain never provided honest feedback during the year.
The annual evaluation should contain no surprises. Every significant performance issue should have been addressed in real-time through coaching conversations. If a member is receiving a below-standard rating, they should have known the standard was not being met for months before the evaluation.

Delivering the Evaluation Conversation

The evaluation form is the written record. The conversation is the development tool. Both matter, and neither replaces the other. An evaluation handed to a member without a meaningful conversation is an administrative form, not a leadership act. An evaluation conversation without a written record has no organizational value.

PRE
Prepare — Review the Full Year, Not Just the Form
Before the evaluation conversation, review all documentation from the evaluation period — training records, incident reports, counseling memos, commendations. The Captain who arrives at the evaluation conversation having reviewed the full year's record is prepared to have a specific, credible conversation. The one who relies on memory is about to have a vague one.
SEQ
Structure — Strengths, Gaps, Goals
A productive evaluation conversation has three components: specific recognition of what the member did well (with behavioral examples), honest identification of one or two areas for growth (with specific, behavioral feedback), and a forward-looking conversation about development goals and what support the Captain will provide. Not a list of complaints. Not a cheerleading session. A balanced, specific, development-focused conversation.
LIS
Listen — The Member's Self-Assessment Matters
The evaluation conversation is a dialogue, not a monologue. Ask the member how they assess their own performance before delivering your assessment. Two things happen: you often learn something that changes or adds nuance to your assessment; and the member who has done honest self-reflection is far more receptive to feedback than the one who hears the evaluation as something done to them.
The Courage Requirement

Honest evaluation requires the Courage element of T·C·H·O·E·D·S more consistently than almost any other leadership responsibility. The evaluation that is inflated because the Captain didn't want the difficult conversation has failed the member — who needed honest feedback to grow — and has failed the organization — which needs accurate performance data to make decisions about development, assignment, and promotion. The evaluation that is honest, specific, and delivered with genuine care for the member's development is one of the most valuable things a company officer provides. It requires courage every time.

Behavioral Issues + EAP —
Performance Problems with a Human Core

⏱ 16 min🧠 EAP · Behavioral Health · Intervention

Many performance problems in the fire service have a behavioral health component beneath the surface — a member whose attendance pattern reflects an emerging substance use problem, a previously high-performer whose work quality has declined in the context of a personal crisis, a crew member whose interpersonal behavior has become erratic in ways that suggest something is wrong. The company officer who recognizes these patterns and responds effectively — within scope — can intervene in a career trajectory, and sometimes in a life.

Recognizing the Pattern — What to Look For

The Captain is not a clinician. But the Captain sees their crew every shift, and behavioral health problems have observable operational signatures. Recognizing these patterns is part of the personnel management responsibility:

  • Attendance changes: Increased use of sick leave, particularly on specific days (Mondays, days after known events), unexplained absences, pattern that differs from the member's historical baseline.
  • Performance decline: A previously competent member whose work quality, attention to detail, or judgment has declined without an obvious operational explanation.
  • Interpersonal changes: Withdrawal from normal crew interactions, increased irritability or volatility, conflict with crew members who had previously positive relationships with the member.
  • Appearance and physical changes: Changes in grooming, apparent fatigue, physical changes that suggest health impact.
  • Response to normal supervision: Defensiveness or emotional volatility in response to routine performance feedback that previously would not have generated that reaction.
The Critical Boundary

The Captain who observes these patterns does not diagnose them. "I think Martinez has a drinking problem" is a clinical conclusion the Captain is not qualified to reach. "Martinez has missed three Mondays in the last six weeks after meeting his attendance standards for two years, and his performance on the last two calls has been significantly below his normal level — I'm concerned and want to make sure he has access to support" is an observable behavioral description that appropriately activates the EAP referral process. The distinction is critical — both for the Captain's legal exposure and for the member's dignity.

The EAP Referral — Two Types

Voluntary Referral
Making EAP Available Without Mandating It
The Captain who has established a relationship of genuine care with their crew creates conditions where members who are struggling will approach them for help. When a member does this — or when the Captain observes patterns that suggest need — the voluntary referral makes EAP available: "I want to make sure you know about the EAP. These conversations are confidential, the support is real, and there's no obligation. Here's how to access it." The Captain facilitates; the member chooses.
Mandatory Referral
Performance-Based EAP Requirement
When a member's performance has declined to the point that formal action is required, many departments use EAP as a component of the corrective action plan — the member is required to engage with EAP as a condition of continued employment or return from leave. Mandatory referrals involve HR and the chain of command. The company officer's role is to document the performance issues that justify the referral, not to design or deliver the referral process independently.

Maintaining the Professional Relationship During the Process

The company officer who initiates an EAP referral or begins a formal performance management process is not ending their relationship with the member — they are reshaping it into one that can actually help. The distinction between caring about someone and covering for them is important: the supervisor who covered the performance problems for years was not helping — they were preventing the member from accessing support they needed.

What Genuine Care Actually Looks Like

The Captain who says "I've covered for you because I care about you and the crew" is describing friendship, not leadership. Genuine care at the company officer level means being honest about what you're observing, getting the member connected to the right support, holding the standard that allows the member to remain employed and safe, and maintaining respect throughout the process. That is harder and more valuable than covering.

Hiring Involvement —
Interview Panels and Selection Integrity

⏱ 14 min🎯 Interview Panels · Selection · Legal

Captains and above are frequently asked to serve on hiring panels — for entry-level firefighter recruitment, lateral hire processes, or promotional assessments. This is both an organizational responsibility and an opportunity to shape who joins and leads the organization. It also carries legal obligations that the uninformed panelist can inadvertently violate.

The Legal Framework — What You Cannot Ask

Interview questions must be job-related

Interview questions must be job-related and must not solicit information that could be used as the basis for a discriminatory hiring decision. The categories protected by federal law (and in many cases extended by state law) include:

  • Race, color, national origin, ancestry
  • Sex, gender, gender identity, sexual orientation
  • Religion or religious practices
  • Age (for applicants over 40, under the ADEA)
  • Disability status (ADA — focus on ability to perform the job, not the disability)
  • Pregnancy or family planning
  • Marital status, family structure
  • Military service (beyond what's relevant to the position)
The Casual Question Problem

Most illegal interview questions are not asked with discriminatory intent — they are asked because a panelist is trying to be friendly, make conversation, or learn more about the candidate. "So where are you originally from?" "Do you have kids? The shift schedule can be challenging for families." "What church do you attend? I notice you listed some volunteer work there." All illegal, all often asked casually and without malice. The company officer who serves on a panel is responsible for knowing what not to ask — and for redirecting or noting when another panelist asks something they shouldn't.

Behavioral Interview Questions — The Gold Standard

Behavioral interview questions ask candidates to describe specific past behaviors rather than hypothetical responses. Research consistently demonstrates that past behavior is the strongest predictor of future behavior — and behavioral questions are both more legally defensible and more predictively valid than hypothetical or opinion-based questions.

SIT
The STAR Framework — How to Evaluate Behavioral Responses
Strong behavioral interview responses contain four elements: the Situation (context for what occurred), the Task (what the candidate was responsible for), the Action they took (specifically what they did, not what "we" did), and the Result (what happened as a consequence of their action). Panelists who evaluate against STAR criteria are applying a consistent, defensible standard. The candidate who gives vague, general, or "we"-heavy responses without specific individual action is not demonstrating behavioral competence — they're describing a team.

Panel Integrity and Consistency

The interview panel's credibility — and legal defensibility — depends on consistency: asking the same questions of every candidate, using the same scoring criteria, and documenting scores independently before discussion. The panelist who departs from the structured questions, who is influenced by non-job-related factors, or who reaches consensus with other panelists before independent scoring has compromised the process.

Consistent Questions
Same Questions, Every Candidate
Departing from the question set for some candidates but not others — asking easier follow-up questions, providing more prompting, or asking different questions entirely — creates a disparate interview experience that undermines validity and legal defensibility. Follow the structured protocol.
Independent Scoring
Score Before Discussion
Each panelist scores independently before any group discussion. The panelist who waits to see what others think before committing to a score is not providing independent assessment — they are being influenced. Independent scoring, then discussion of significant disagreements, then final scoring is the appropriate process sequence.

Documentation —
The Record That Protects Everyone

⏱ 14 min📄 Personnel Records · Counseling Memos · Legal

Personnel documentation is the organizational memory that makes fair, consistent, and legally defensible personnel management possible. The Captain who documents thoroughly — positive and negative, throughout the year — is protecting the member (whose full performance record is captured accurately), the organization (whose personnel decisions can be defended), and themselves (whose supervisory actions are on record). The one who documents selectively or retroactively creates all three of these vulnerabilities simultaneously.

What to Document and When

NOW
Document Contemporaneously — Not Retroactively
The most common documentation failure: something significant happens, the Captain tells themselves they'll document it later, and it's never documented — or it's documented weeks afterward in a way that looks like it was created to support a subsequent action. Contemporaneous documentation (created at or near the time of the event) is far more credible legally and organizationally than documentation created after the fact to justify a decision already made.
SPE
Specific, Behavioral, and Factual — Not Interpretive
Documentation records what happened, not what it means. "Member arrived 8 minutes late to shift briefing on [date] without prior notification. This is the third late arrival in the past 30 days" is factual and specific. "Member appears to have a problem with punctuality and doesn't seem to care about the job" is interpretive and subjective — and in many jurisdictions, legally problematic. Document behavior, not conclusions.
POS
Document Positive Performance Too — Not Just Problems
Personnel files that contain only corrective documentation present a distorted picture of a member's performance. The Captain who documents commendable performance — strong incident command, exceptional mentoring of a probationary member, initiative on a community program — creates a complete record that serves the member's career and provides balanced context for any performance management conversations that follow.
KNOW
Know What's in the Personnel File vs What Isn't
In most jurisdictions, the official personnel file is maintained by HR and contains formal evaluations, disciplinary records, and approved documentation. The supervisor's working notes — informal records of conversations, observations, and coaching sessions — are typically not part of the official file. Know your department's records management policy: what must be filed with HR, what can be maintained as supervisory notes, what must be provided to the member, and what retention requirements apply.

The Counseling Memo — What It Is and How to Write It

A counseling memo is a written record of a supervisory conversation about a performance issue. It is not a disciplinary action — it is documentation that the issue was identified, discussed, and that expectations were clarified. The counseling memo typically contains: a factual description of the performance issue, a statement of the applicable standard, a summary of the conversation that occurred, and the expectation going forward. The member typically signs to acknowledge receipt (not necessarily agreement).

Documentation as Care

Documentation is sometimes framed as adversarial — as building a case against a member. The better framing: documentation protects the member's right to a fair, consistent, and accurate assessment of their performance. The member who has a complete performance record — positive and negative, specific and contemporaneous — is in a far better position if they ever need to contest a personnel action than the one whose file contains nothing but a retroactive summary written after the decision was made. Documentation done right is a form of respect for the member's dignity and rights.

Assessment Center Application —
Personnel Management Scenarios

⏱ 14 min🎯 Scoring + Practice Scenarios

Personnel management scenarios are among the most common and most varied in Captain-level assessments — they test judgment, communication, legal awareness, and genuine care for people simultaneously. The 5/5 response demonstrates scope awareness (what belongs to the Captain vs HR), behavioral specificity, an appropriate referral or escalation path, and the human dimension that separates technically correct responses from leadership responses.

Practice Scenario — Performance Decline with Possible EAP Need

Prompt

"You have a six-year member on your crew, previously one of your highest performers. Over the past three months, you have observed four unplanned sick day absences (all on Mondays), two instances of below-standard performance at incidents, and noticeable withdrawal from normal crew interaction. No violation of any specific policy has occurred. A colleague tells you informally that the member is going through a divorce. How do you respond?"

3/5 Response
"I would meet with the member privately and express my concern about the performance issues I've observed. I would document the conversation and let them know that their performance needs to improve. I would also let them know about the EAP program available to them."
5/5 Response
"My first step is to separate what I know from what I've been told informally. The divorce information is secondhand and is personal — it plays no role in this conversation, and I don't acknowledge it. What I have is documented, observable performance data: four Monday absences in three months against a two-year attendance baseline, two below-standard incident responses, and behavioral withdrawal from crew interaction. That is what I address.

I would meet privately with the member, open with genuine care — not criticism — and specifically describe what I've observed: 'Over the past three months I've noticed some changes in your attendance pattern, your performance at two incidents, and how you've been engaging with the crew. This is a departure from the person I've watched work for six years. I'm concerned, and I want to make sure I'm doing right by you.'

I would listen before I direct. I'm not diagnosing anything — I'm giving them an opportunity to tell me what's happening if they choose to. Then I would make EAP available clearly and without stigma: 'Regardless of what's driving this, I want you to know about the EAP. It's confidential, it's real support, and there's no obligation. Here's the information.'

I would also be clear about the performance standard: 'I also need to be honest with you — the Monday pattern and the performance at the last two calls need to change. I want to help you get there. If there's something I can do operationally to support that, I'm open to it. But the standard applies regardless of what's happening personally — not because I don't care, but because I do.'

I would document the conversation — specifically and contemporaneously. I would not document the divorce information. And I would schedule a follow-up in 30 days to assess whether the pattern has shifted. If it hasn't, I have documented the conversation, the standard, and the EAP referral — and I can engage HR and my chain of command with a clean, behavioral record."
