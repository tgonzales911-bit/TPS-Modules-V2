The Tactical Exercise —
What Assessors Are Scoring

⏱ 15 min 🔥 Emergency Scene Command

The tactical exercise is the assessment center component most candidates underestimate. They spend weeks on oral board responses and written exercises — and walk into the tactical exercise thinking their fireground experience will carry them. It does not. Not because their experience is irrelevant, but because the tactical exercise is not testing their firefighting skill. It is testing their command judgment.

The difference is significant. Firefighting skill is about knowing what to do. Command judgment is about knowing what to do, in what order, at what authority level, communicated to whom, documented how — and then being able to articulate the leadership principle behind every decision you made. That last part is what produces 4/5 scores instead of 3/5 scores.

What the Tactical Exercise Looks Like

Tactical exercises appear in several formats depending on the department and assessment center design. The three most common formats you will encounter at the Lieutenant level:

Format 1
Verbal / Radio Simulation
You respond to a scenario description read aloud or presented on paper. You verbalize your size-up, initial commands, and resource decisions as if communicating over radio. The most common format at Lieutenant level.
Format 2
Static Photo Simulation
You are shown a photograph of an incident scene and asked to manage it. You verbalize what you observe, your size-up conclusions, and your tactical decisions based on visual information.
Format 3
Dynamic Role-Play Simulation
Role players respond to your commands as if they are units on the incident. The scenario evolves based on your decisions. Most common at Captain and Battalion Chief level — less common but possible at Lieutenant.

The Five Dimensions Assessors Score in the Tactical Exercise

The same eight assessment dimensions apply in the tactical exercise as in every other exercise. But in tactical scenarios, two additional behavioral dimensions appear on most scoring rubrics specifically:

01
Command Presence
Do you take command immediately and clearly? Hesitation, vague language, or failing to formally establish command signals to assessors that you are operating as a senior firefighter — not a Lieutenant.
Scored from your first sentence
02
Size-Up Quality
Do you verbalize a complete, organized size-up before committing resources? Assessors specifically listen for the elements of a complete size-up. Missing elements cost points systematically.
Every element missed = a scoring opportunity lost
03
Resource Management
Do you assign resources to specific tasks with clear objectives? "Take a line inside" is a 3/5 assignment. "Engine 62 — advance a 1¾-inch line to Division 1 for primary search and fire attack" is a 4/5 assignment.
Scored on every resource assignment
04
Accountability
Do you know where your people are at all times? Establishing accountability — who is where, doing what, for how long — is a scored Lieutenant behavior. Not establishing it is a common 3/5 error.
Scored as an ongoing command function
05
Principle Close
Do you close with a named leadership principle? This is the element that separates Lieutenant candidates from experienced firefighters. Your tactical decisions reflect your leadership philosophy. Name the connection explicitly.
The move from 3/5 to 4/5 — no competitor teaches this

The Most Common Errors in the Tactical Exercise

These five errors appear repeatedly across assessment center candidates at the Lieutenant level. Knowing them before your first rep is the fastest way to eliminate them from your performance.

The Five Tactical Exercise Errors

Error 1 — Acting before size-up: Assigning resources before verbalizing a complete size-up. Assessors catch this immediately.

Error 2 — Vague resource assignments: "Get a line in there" instead of named unit, specific location, specific task, specific objective.

Error 3 — Failing to establish command: Jumping into tactical decisions without formally announcing command establishment. A Lieutenant does not skip this even in a familiar scenario.

Error 4 — No accountability: Moving through tactical decisions without establishing who is inside, what they are doing, and on what timeline.

Error 5 — No principle close: Ending the tactical response when the last resource is assigned. A 4/5 response closes with a named leadership principle connecting the tactical decisions to the Lieutenant's leadership philosophy.

The Core Insight

Assessors are not watching you fight the fire. They are watching you command the incident. The fireground is the context. Leadership under pressure is the content. Every decision you make — from the first size-up word to the final principle close — is a data point about whether you already think like a Lieutenant.

ICS/NIMS
Essentials for the Assessment Center

⏱ 18 min 📋 Command Frameworks

The Incident Command System is not something you learn for an assessment center — it is something you already know from your career. What this lesson does is give you the specific ICS vocabulary and structural concepts that assessors listen for in tactical exercises, so that what you already know becomes visible and scoreable.

The ICS/NIMS essentials for assessment center purposes focus on five core concepts. Not the entire ICS structure — the five elements that appear most frequently in Lieutenant-level tactical scenarios and that assessors specifically score for.

The ICS/NIMS Essentials — Five Concepts the Board Scores

IC
Incident Command — Unified Command
Command must be formally established, named, and communicated. In a single-company response: "Engine 61 is on scene. I am establishing Thornton Command." In a multi-agency response: "Unified Command is established with [agencies]." Assessors listen for the formal command establishment phrase. Without it — you have not established command in their scoring.
CC
Chain of Command — Span of Control
Every resource reports to one supervisor. Span of control at the Lieutenant level is 1:5 — one supervisor for every five personnel. In your tactical response: every crew you assign reports to a named supervisor. You do not bypass the chain. You do not assign resources to tasks without a named responsible party. Assessors score whether your assignments maintain organizational structure.
IAP
Incident Action Plan
The IAP is your stated incident strategy — what you are trying to accomplish and how. At the Lieutenant level the IAP does not need to be written. It needs to be verbalized clearly: "Our incident objectives are: (1) life safety — primary search and rescue, (2) incident stabilization — fire attack, (3) property conservation — limit extension." State your objectives before assigning resources. This is the organizational thinking assessors look for.
RM
Resource Management — Accountability
Know what resources you have, what they are doing, and where they are at all times. In a Lieutenant-level scenario this means: named unit assignments, specific locations (Division/Group designations), specific tasks with objectives, and a stated accountability checkpoint. "All units: accountability check — report location and assignment" is a scored behavior.
TC
Transfer of Command
When a higher-ranking officer arrives on scene, command is formally transferred — not assumed. "Command is being transferred to Battalion 1. Briefing follows: we have a two-story residential, working fire second floor, two lines in operation, primary search in progress, all units accounted for." A complete transfer brief demonstrates Lieutenant-level organizational thinking. Missing it when required costs points.

Why These Five — Not All of ICS

The full ICS structure covers dozens of positions, concepts, and protocols. For assessment center purposes, the five concepts above are the ones that appear in scoring rubrics at the Lieutenant level. They are the concepts that separate a candidate who knows ICS theoretically from a candidate who applies it as command language under pressure.

The test is not whether you can recite ICS structure — every candidate can do that. The test is whether these concepts are embedded in your actual command language. Whether you establish command by name. Whether you state your incident objectives before assigning resources. Whether you maintain accountability as an ongoing command function — not a one-time checkbox.

ICS Language vs Fireground Language

One of the most common scoring drops in the tactical exercise happens when candidates default to informal fireground language instead of ICS command language. The content may be correct — but the language signals to assessors which role the candidate is operating from.

❌ Fireground Language — 3/5
"Get a line to the second floor. Have someone start searching. Watch the roof — it looks bad. Tell the next engine to stage outside." Correct instincts. Informal language. No command structure visible. Scores Meets Standard.
✅ Command Language — 4/5
"Engine 61 establishing Command. Incident objectives: life safety and stabilization. Engine 62 — advance a line to Division 2 for fire attack. Ladder 1 — primary search Division 2. Engine 63 — stage and stand by. Accountability check in five minutes." Same scene. Command language visible. Scores Above Standard.
The ICS/NIMS Principle Close

In Leadership Philosophy and tactical scenarios, you can close with a value anchor: "Every decision I made in this scenario reflects the ICS principle of unified command — one clear chain of authority, accountable to the mission and to the safety of every person on that scene. That is not procedure. That is leadership." This close signals to assessors that you have internalized ICS as a leadership philosophy — not just a radio protocol.

A Note on Department Communication Protocols

This module uses standard NFPA ICS/NIMS terminology. If your department uses a specific communication model — such as Blue Card Command or another hybrid system — apply the same five concepts through that protocol's language. The principles of unified command, span of control, IAP, resource accountability, and transfer of command are universal. The vocabulary your department uses to express them is equally valid in an assessment center.

The Verbal
Size-Up Protocol

⏱ 20 min 🔥 Core Tactical Skill

The verbal size-up is the single most scored behavior in the tactical exercise. It happens before any resource is assigned, before any tactical decision is made, before your first command. It is the thirty seconds that tells assessors whether you have arrived as a senior firefighter or as a Lieutenant.

Most candidates understand size-up conceptually. Fewer candidates deliver a complete, organized, verbalized size-up under assessment conditions. The pressure of observation — assessors watching, time running — compresses the size-up. Candidates skip elements. They rush to action. They assign resources before they have told assessors what they are dealing with. Every skipped element is a missed scoring opportunity.

A Blue Card Size-Up — Model Example

Here is an example of an accepted size-up, delivered in the Blue Card format — one widely used model, not the only one. Give your size-up in the structure your own department runs. Study the language, the sequence, and the specificity.

Blue Card Size-Up — Residential Structure Fire · Lieutenant Level
Initial Radio Report
“Engine 61 on scene — two-story, single-family residential, approximately 1,800 square feet. Working fire, heavy smoke showing from Division 2, Charlie side.”
Strategy + Command
“Engine 61 is going offensive for fire attack and primary search. Engine 61 has Maple Command.”
360 + Follow-Up
“Maple Command, follow-up report — 360 complete, no basement, fire in Division 2 extending toward the attic. One occupant reported, location unknown. No change to the offensive strategy.”
Fire Attack · TLO
“Engine 62 — task: fire attack; location: Division 2, Charlie side; objective: locate and control the fire.”
Primary Search · TLO
“Ladder 1 — task: primary search; location: Division 2; objective: all occupants located and reported.”
Resources + Accountability
“Command requesting a second alarm — Battalion 1, respond to assume Command. All units, accountability check and PAR at the ten-minute mark.”

The Thirty-Second Rule

A complete verbal size-up at this level takes thirty to forty-five seconds. Many candidates feel this is too long — that they should be assigning resources faster. The opposite is true. Assessors write notes during your size-up. The more complete and organized your size-up, the more scoring opportunities you give them before you have assigned a single resource.

The candidate who assigns resources in fifteen seconds has given assessors two data points. The candidate who delivers a complete thirty-second size-up before the first assignment has given assessors twelve data points. Every element of the size-up is a scored behavior. Rushing eliminates scoring opportunities.

Practice Target

In your Practice Engine — before submitting any tactical scenario response, write your size-up first completely and in order. Do not write a single resource assignment until your size-up is complete. This discipline in practice becomes automatic behavior in the assessment center.

The Fireground Command Frameworks
These are the size-up and command frameworks a board expects a company officer to know cold. They are not interchangeable — each does a different job. Know what each is for, and be ready to defend the one your department runs.
COAL WAS WEALTH
The systematic 13-factor size-up. What you assess before committing to a tactic.
Construction · Occupancy · Apparatus and personnel · Life hazard · Water supply · Auxiliary appliances · Street conditions · Weather · Exposures · Area · Location and extent · Time · Hazards (height and hazmat)
When it applies: The 360 and the size-up. Life Hazard is the primary driver — state it first; it overrides every other factor.
RECEO-VS
The classic tactical priority order. Rescue is always first.
Rescue · Exposures · Confinement · Extinguishment · Overhaul — then Ventilation · Salvage (V and S support throughout, not only at the end)
When it applies: Sequencing tactics once size-up is done. Life safety (Rescue) is unconditional and first — developed in the 1980s and still the fireground default.
SLICERS
Updates engine-company operations for flow path and cooling. Complements RECEO-VS — it does not replace it.
Size-up · Locate the fire · Identify and control the flow path · Cool the space from a safe location · Extinguish — then Rescue · Salvage (actions of opportunity, any time)
When it applies: First-arriving engine company. Built on UL/NIST fire-dynamics research: cool the space from the safest location before committing to the interior.
Blue Card · TLO
Brunacini’s hazard-zone command standard for NIMS Type 4/5 incidents — the structure for the radio.
Initial Radio Report (building and conditions → problem → initial action plan → strategy → resources → command) → 360 → assume and name Command → assign every company by TLO: Task, Location, Objective. Built on the 8 Functions of Command.
When it applies: Establishing and running command. Every hazard-zone assignment names a Task, a Location, and an Objective — TLO, TLO, TLO. Example: “Engine 1 on scene, two-story residential, smoke showing Division 2 — offensive — Engine 1 has Command.”

Lieutenant
Command Language

⏱ 16 min 🎙 Language That Scores

Command language is the vocabulary assessors hear as evidence of rank-appropriate thinking. It is not jargon. It is not memorized scripts. It is a set of specific phrases and structural patterns that, when delivered naturally, signal to every assessor in the room that you already operate at the Lieutenant level.

The difference between fireground language and command language is not volume or confidence. It is specificity, organizational structure, and accountability. A firefighter says "get a line in there." A Lieutenant says "Engine 62 — advance a 1¾-inch attack line to Division 1, primary objective fire attack on the Bravo wall. Report when water is flowing." Same instruction. Completely different score.

The Resource Assignment Formula

Every resource assignment in a tactical exercise should follow this four-part formula. It takes ten additional seconds to complete a full assignment. It moves your score from 3/5 to 4/5 on resource management every time.

W
Who — Named Unit
"Engine 62..." Not "the next engine" or "send someone." Named unit. Always.
W
Where — ICS Location
"...to Division 2..." Use ICS division, group, or sector designations. Not "upstairs" or "around back." ICS location language.
W
What — Specific Task
"...advance a 1¾-inch line for fire attack..." Specific. Observable. Assessors can visualize what the crew is doing.
W
Why — Objective
"...primary objective is protecting the exposure on the Charlie side." Connecting the task to the incident objective shows strategic thinking — not just tactical execution.

The Command Language Library

These are the specific phrases that signal Lieutenant-level command language to assessors. Read them out loud until they sound natural — not recited.

Command Establishment
Arrival
"Engine 61 is on scene at [address]. I am establishing [location] Command."
Size-Up Complete
"[Location] Command to all units — I have completed my size-up. Incident objectives are as follows..."
Resource Assignments
Attack Assignment
"Engine 62 — advance a 1¾-inch attack line to Division 1. Primary objective: fire attack, Bravo side. Report when in position and water is flowing."
Search Assignment
"Ladder 1 — conduct primary search Division 2. Report all-clear or victim location immediately. You have one PAR cycle — ten minutes."
Staging
"Engine 63 — stage at [location], stand by for second assignment. I anticipate needing you for exposure protection on the Delta side."
Accountability + Progress
Accountability Check
"Command to all units — accountability check. All crews report location and current assignment."
Progress Report
"Command to dispatch — progress report at five minutes. Working fire, two-story residential. Offensive operations. Two lines in operation. Primary search in progress. All units accounted for. Awaiting Battalion 1."
Tactical Changes + Transfer
Going Defensive
"Command to all units — emergency traffic. We are transitioning to defensive operations. All units evacuate the structure immediately. Establish exterior positions. Report when clear."
Transfer Brief
"Battalion 1, Command briefing: two-story residential, working fire second floor, offensive operations. Two attack lines, primary search complete, one civilian located and removed, all units accounted for. Requesting your assumption of command."
Your Department's Communication Protocol

The command scripts in this lesson use standard ICS/NIMS terminology. If your department uses Blue Card Command, a hybrid system, or its own radio protocol — use that language instead. The four-part assignment formula (who, where, what, why) and the structural principles apply universally. The specific phrases are the delivery vehicle — use the one your assessors will recognize as your department's standard.

The Language Test

Read your practice responses and apply this test to every resource assignment: does it contain a named unit, an ICS location, a specific task, and an objective? If any of the four are missing — rewrite the assignment before submitting. Do this on every rep until the four-part formula is automatic.

The Command-Language Bank — Tentative to Declarative
Command presence is audible. The same decision, spoken two ways, scores two ways. Tentative language caps the tactical exercise; declarative, structured language at the rank you are testing for is what the assessor is listening for. Notice how the strong column uses Blue Card’s TLO — Task, Location, Objective — to assign with zero ambiguity.
Tentative — caps the exercise
“I guess I’ll take command for now.”
Declarative — command presence
Engine 1 is on scene, two-story residential, smoke showing Division 2 — Engine 1 has Command.
Why: A declarative on-scene report with command assumed and named. No hedging, no “for now.”
Tentative — caps the exercise
“We’ll probably go inside and see what we’ve got.”
Declarative — command presence
We are in the offensive strategy, conducting interior fire attack.
Why: A declared strategy tells the board you have decided — not that you are hoping it works out.
Tentative — caps the exercise
“Truck 1, go help out upstairs.”
Declarative — command presence
Truck 1 — task: primary search; location: Division 2; objective: all occupants located and reported.
Why: Task, Location, Objective. Every hazard-zone assignment names all three — that is Blue Card command, with zero ambiguity.
Tentative — caps the exercise
“Send everything you’ve got, this looks bad.”
Declarative — command presence
Command is requesting a second alarm for an offensive fire in a two-story residential with a confirmed life hazard.
Why: A resource request tied to conditions and strategy reads as command judgment, not alarm.
Tentative — caps the exercise
“Be careful in there, guys.”
Declarative — command presence
Crews are operating in the hazard zone — I want a PAR at ten minutes and a RIT in place now.
Why: Specific, accountable risk management beats a vague caution every time.
Tentative — caps the exercise
“Okay Chief, it’s all yours.”
Declarative — command presence
Battalion 1, face-to-face transfer: offensive strategy, two lines in service Division 2, primary search underway, all crews PAR — you have Command.
Why: A structured transfer — conditions, actions, accountability — is the mark of an officer who owns the incident.

Common Tactical Scenarios
and Framework Application

⏱ 22 min 🎯 Scenario Application

The tactical exercise at the Lieutenant level draws from a predictable set of incident types. Knowing the five most common scenario types — and the specific ICS and command-framework application for each — means you walk into the exercise with a practiced response architecture rather than improvising under observation pressure.

This lesson covers the five most common Lieutenant-level tactical scenarios, the specific challenges each presents, and the framework you apply to close each one with a named principle.

The Five Most Common Lieutenant-Level Tactical Scenarios

Scenario Type 1 — Residential Structure Fire

The most common tactical scenario at every rank. The Lieutenant-level version focuses on single-company operations with limited resources and a life safety unknown — typically a reported missing occupant.

What Assessors Test
Life Safety Priority
Can the candidate simultaneously manage fire attack and primary search with limited resources? Do they prioritize correctly and communicate the prioritization?
Common Trap
Delaying Search
Candidates who commit all resources to fire attack without establishing a search assignment when a missing occupant is reported score below candidates who address life safety first.

Principle close for residential fire: "Every decision in this response reflects a Dedication value anchor — specifically the commitment to never accept a known life safety risk when resources allow me to address it simultaneously with fire attack. That is not tactics. That is what it means to be accountable for every person on and around that scene."

Scenario Type 2 — Multi-Vehicle Accident with Entrapment

Tests resource coordination, scene control, and the Lieutenant's ability to manage a multi-agency response at the company level. Often includes a request for additional resources decision.

What Assessors Test
Multi-Agency Coordination
Can the candidate establish unified command with law enforcement and EMS, assign resources to multiple simultaneous tasks, and maintain accountability across agencies?
Common Trap
Scene Control Failure
Candidates who treat the MVC as a fire service-only incident — failing to coordinate with law enforcement on scene control and EMS on patient care — miss the multi-agency ICS application that assessors score.

Principle close for MVC: "This scenario demonstrates a Humility value anchor in command — specifically recognizing that effective Lieutenant-level command at a multi-agency scene means coordinating assets I do not control, deferring on decisions outside my authority, and communicating clearly enough that every agency on that scene knows their role and my intentions."

Scenario Type 3 — Commercial Structure with Reported Occupants

Scales the residential structure fire to a larger building with more complex occupancy, unknown life safety status, and resource management complexity. Tests span of control and the additional alarm decision.

The Specific Test
The Additional Alarm Decision
Assessors specifically evaluate whether the candidate requests additional resources before they need them — not when they are overwhelmed. The Lieutenant who calls for a second alarm proactively demonstrates organizational thinking. The one who waits demonstrates reactive firefighter thinking.

Scenario Type 4 — Hazardous Materials Incident

Tests awareness-level decision-making at the Lieutenant level. The correct response is isolation, identification, and notification — not entry. Most candidates with operational experience over-respond to HazMat scenarios. Assessors score the candidate who correctly identifies the boundaries of Lieutenant-level authority in a HazMat incident.

The HazMat Trap

Candidates who direct crews to approach, investigate, or mitigate a suspected HazMat incident without HazMat team activation and proper PPE score below standard — even if their tactical instincts are otherwise strong. The Lieutenant-level HazMat response is: isolate, deny entry, identify, notify, and request appropriate resources. That is the complete correct response at this rank.

Scenario Type 5 — Active Personnel Problem During an Incident

The hybrid scenario — a tactical emergency complicated by a personnel issue occurring simultaneously. A crew member becomes injured, refuses an order, or exhibits dangerous behavior during an active incident. This scenario tests whether the candidate can manage the tactical incident AND the personnel issue simultaneously without either deteriorating.

The framework: ICS/NIMS for the tactical component, 3 U's Diagnostic for the personnel component. Name both frameworks explicitly. Assessors are specifically watching whether you maintain command presence through the personnel disruption.

Principle close for hybrid scenario: "This scenario tested two things simultaneously — my tactical judgment and my supervisory judgment. The Ownership value anchor applies to both. I own the safety of that incident and I own the performance environment of every crew member on it. Neither responsibility pauses because the other one is difficult."

The Scenario Recognition Habit

Before your first word in any tactical scenario — L·E·A·D. Label the scenario type. Engage your verbal size-up structure. Apply at Lieutenant scope — every decision belongs to the authority of a company officer. Deliver your close with a value anchor connected specifically to this incident. Ten seconds. Every tactical scenario. Every rep.

A 5/5 Answer — The First-Due Size-Up
Scenario: You arrive first-due, Engine 1, on a two-story single-family residential, working fire, smoke showing from the Bravo side, Division 2. Give your size-up and initial actions.
Reactive · No Structure · 3/5
“Okay, so we’ve got a house fire, looks like it’s upstairs. I’d probably pull a line and send the crew in to find the fire and put it out. I’d get a search going for anyone inside and call for more units if it looks bad. Safety is the priority.”
Why it caps: No on-scene report, no declared strategy, no command established, no structured size-up, and assignments with no Task, Location, or Objective. It is a reaction, not command — it reads as a firefighter, not a company officer.
Structured Command · 5/5
“Engine 1 is on scene of a two-story, single-family residential, smoke showing from the Bravo side, Division 2 — we are in the offensive strategy. Engine 1 is stretching a 1¾ for fire attack, Division 2, Bravo side, objective locate and control the fire. I am completing a 360 and assuming Command — Engine 1 has Command.

On the follow-up: 360 complete, no basement, primary search of Division 2 needed. Truck 1 — task: primary search; location: Division 2; objective: all occupants located and reported. Life safety drives this: Rescue is first, and per the flow-path research I want the space cooled from the safest location before we push in. That is the Dedication to crew and civilian safety I would bring as a company officer.”
Why it scores: On-scene report with building and conditions, a declared strategy, command assumed and named, assignments by TLO (Task, Location, Objective), life-safety-first sequencing (Rescue / RECEO-VS), flow-path awareness (SLICERS cooling), and a value-anchor close at rank. Report → strategy → command → TLO assignments is the 5/5 command signature.

L·E·A·D
Applied to Tactical

⏱ 14 min ⚡ Integration

Every framework in this module connects to a single four-step sequence. You have seen L·E·A·D in Module 1. You have applied it to oral board scenarios in Module 22. This lesson shows you exactly how L·E·A·D maps to the tactical exercise — so that the same ten-second pre-response discipline that governs your oral board responses governs your command decisions in every tactical scenario.

L·E·A·D in the Tactical Exercise

L
Label — Scenario Type + Primary Framework
Before your first word: What type of tactical scenario is this? Residential fire? MVC? HazMat? Hybrid? Name it internally. What is the primary framework? ICS/NIMS for pure tactical. 3 U's + ICS for hybrid. HazMat awareness protocol for hazmat. Ten seconds. Before you speak.
E
Engage — Six-Element Size-Up Protocol
Your structure in the tactical exercise is a complete, organized size-up. Commit to it before your first word. Establish command. Life safety. Building and conditions. Resources. IAP with objectives. Accountability. In order. Every time. Not as a checklist — as command thinking expressed out loud.
A
Apply — Lieutenant Scope + Command Language
Every decision, every assignment, every escalation request must belong to Lieutenant-level authority. You are commanding a single company — not a battalion. Your assignments use the four-part formula (who, where, what, why) in ICS command language. You do not make battalion-level decisions. You do not skip command language to sound more natural. Scope discipline is scored.
D
Deliver — Value-Anchor Close
After your last resource assignment — pause. Do not end the response. Close with a named principle connecting your tactical decisions to your leadership philosophy. "Every decision I made in this scenario reflects a clear value anchor — specifically [how it applies to this incident]. That is not procedure. That is who I am as a Lieutenant." This close is what separates your score from every other candidate who knew the same tactics.

E — IAP
"Incident objectives: first — life safety, primary search. Second — stabilization, fire attack. Engine 61 crew — advance attack line to Division 1, Bravo-Charlie corner. Specifically the commitment to never accept a known life safety risk when I have the resources to address it. That is not procedure. That is the lens through which I command every incident."
