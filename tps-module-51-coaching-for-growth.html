Coaching
for Growth

Counseling fixes what is below the standard. Coaching raises people who are already meeting it, and it is the conversation most officers never have. This chapter gives you the GROW conversation, feedback that reinforces what you want repeated, and a one-page development plan built on real experience instead of a list of classes. It answers the board question most candidates fumble: how do you develop the people who are not a problem?

Coaching —
The Conversation Nobody Has

⏱ 9 min 🌱 Development

Most officers have exactly two conversations with their people: the correction when something goes wrong, and the annual evaluation. Everything in between is silence. The firefighter who shows up, does solid work and causes no problems can go a full year without one real conversation about where they are headed.

That silence is expensive. Your strongest people are the ones most likely to stall, coast or transfer, because nobody is investing in them. And your next officer is sitting at your kitchen table right now, waiting to find out whether you noticed.

Coaching is the conversation that fills the gap. It is planned, it looks forward, and the member does most of the talking.

Four Different Conversations

Training
Building a skill
You know how, they do not yet. You demonstrate, they practice, you correct. Chapter 14.
Counseling
Correcting a gap
Performance or conduct is below standard. Diagnose with the 3 U's, deliver SBI, set the expectation. Chapter 40.
Mentoring
Sharing your path
Longer term, often outside the chain of command. You share experience and open doors.
Coaching
Drawing out their growth
Performance is acceptable or better. You ask questions that help the member set a goal and build their own plan to reach it.

The most common mistake is using the counseling tone for a coaching conversation. If a good firefighter gets called into the office and braces for bad news, you have already told them something about how often you talk to them.

Where Coaching Fits on the Skill/Will Matrix

You know the four quadrants from Chapter 22. Coaching is not one quadrant. It is how you raise people from one to the next.

High Will · Low Skill
Guide
Eager and new. Coaching here is structured: clear goals, frequent check-ins, lots of reinforcement.
High Skill · Low Will
Re-engage
Capable and coasting. Coaching here is about finding what they still care about. Ask before you assume.
High Skill · High Will
Stretch
Your best people. Coaching here means bigger assignments and real authority. This is the quadrant that gets ignored.
Low Skill · Low Will
Counsel first
This is a performance conversation before it is a coaching one. Start in Chapter 40.

What Assessors Are Scoring

Scored Behavior 1
Develops Everyone
Describes developing solid and high performers, not only fixing problems.
Scored Behavior 2
Asks Before Telling
Uses questions to find the member's goals instead of assigning the officer's goals to them.
Scored Behavior 3
Makes It Specific
A named assignment, a date, a follow-up. Not "I would encourage them."
Scored Behavior 4
Builds the Bench
Connects individual development to succession: who can ride the seat when you are gone.
The Core Idea of This Chapter

Your job as an officer is to work yourself out of a job. The measure is not how well the crew runs when you are there. It is how well it runs when you are not, and how many of your people promote.

G·R·O·W —
The Coaching Conversation

⏱ 11 min 🗣 Core Coaching Tool

GROW is the most widely used coaching structure in the world, popularized by Sir John Whitmore. It is four questions in order. Its strength is that it keeps you from doing the thing officers do by reflex, which is hearing a problem and handing over the answer.

When you hand someone the answer, you get compliance and they learn that thinking is your job. When they build the answer, they own it.

The Four Stages

G
Goal — What Do You Want?
"Where do you want to be in two years?" "What would you like to be better at by the end of this quarter?" Get one specific goal in their words. If they say "I don't know," ask what part of the job they would do more of if they could.
R
Reality — Where Are You Now?
"What have you done toward that so far?" "What is in the way?" "On a scale of one to ten, where are you today?" Listen for the gap between the goal and the present. Do not fill it for them.
O
Options — What Could You Do?
"What are three ways you could close that gap?" "What else?" Ask "what else" at least twice. Offer an idea only after they have run out, and offer it as one option, not the answer.
W
Will — What Will You Do?
"Which one are you going to do first, and by when?" "What do you need from me?" "When do we talk again?" A coaching conversation without a date at the end was just a chat.

What It Sounds Like

GROW Conversation · Lieutenant with a Solid Five-Year Firefighter
Officer
"You have been doing good work for a long time and I have not asked you this: where do you want to be in a couple of years?" (Goal)
Firefighter
"Honestly? I have thought about engineer. I just have not done anything about it."
Officer
"What would you need to be ready for the engineer process?" (Reality)
Firefighter
"Pump time, mostly. I know the theory. I have maybe driven the engine ten times."
Officer
"What are some ways you could get that time?" (Options)
Firefighter
"I could ask Ortiz to let me pump at drills. Maybe take the driver-operator class in the spring."
Officer
"What else?" (Options — the second ask)
Firefighter
"I guess I could drive on the non-emergent returns if you were good with it."
Officer
"I am good with it. Which of those do you want to start with, and when?" (Will)
Firefighter
"Returns starting next shift. I will talk to Ortiz today about drills."
Officer
"Good. I will set up the class request with you. Let us sit down again the first shift of next month and see where your hours are." (Support and a date)

Count the officer's statements. Nearly all of them are questions. The plan belongs to the firefighter, and the officer committed to two things: permission and a follow-up date.

❌ Advice Dump — Loses Points
"You should go for engineer. Take the driver-operator class, get your pump hours, and study the hydraulics book. I will sign you up." Good advice, zero ownership. The officer set the goal and built the plan.
✅ GROW — Scores High
"Where do you want to be in two years? What is in the way? What are some ways to close that? Which one first, and by when?" Four questions. The member leaves with a plan they wrote.
When to Tell Instead of Ask

Coaching is for development, not emergencies. On scene, and for safety-critical skills where there is one right way, you direct. Asking "what do you think we should do?" with fire through the roof is not coaching.

Feedback
That Builds

⏱ 9 min 📣 Reinforcement

You learned SBI as a correction tool in Chapter 40: Situation, Behavior, Impact. The same three steps are the best recognition tool you have, and most officers never use them that way.

"Good job today" is pleasant and useless. The firefighter does not know what was good, so they cannot repeat it. Specific positive feedback tells a person exactly which behavior to keep doing.

SBI for Reinforcement

❌ Generic Praise — Forgotten by Dinner
"Nice work on that call, everybody." No behavior named. Nobody learns anything, and the person who carried the call hears the same thing as the person who carried the clipboard.
✅ SBI Reinforcement — Repeats
"On the rollover this morning, before I asked, you had the second line pulled and the battery disconnected. That gave me one less thing to think about while I was talking to the medics. That is what thinking a step ahead looks like." Situation, Behavior, Impact. Fifteen seconds.

Three Habits

Habit 1
Say it the same day
Feedback loses value by the hour. The tailboard after the call beats the evaluation in December.
Habit 2
More reinforcing than correcting
If the only time you describe someone's behavior is when it is wrong, they stop listening when you start a sentence with their name. Look for what is going right and say it.
Habit 3
Ask for it yourself
"What is one thing I could have done better as the officer on that call?" The first time you will get silence. Ask again next shift. A crew that gives the officer feedback is a crew that speaks up on the fireground.

Feedforward — For the Next Rep

When performance was acceptable and could be better, skip the autopsy and aim at the next attempt. "Next time you give a size-up, try naming the occupancy type first. See if it tightens the rest of the report." It is one change, stated as an experiment, with no judgment of the last attempt. People who get defensive about feedback usually take feedforward well.

Developmental Feedback With a High Performer

Strong performers are often the hardest to give growth feedback to, because everything they do is above the line. Raise the line. "You are the best firefighter on this crew, and I am not going to coach you as a firefighter anymore. I am going to coach you as a future officer. On that last call, what would you have done from my seat?" That question changes what they pay attention to on every call afterward.

Public and Private

Reinforce in front of the crew when the person is comfortable with it, because it tells everyone what the standard is. Correct in private. Coach for growth wherever the conversation happens naturally: the rig, the bay, the kitchen at 2200.

The Development Plan —
One Page, One Stretch

⏱ 10 min 🗺 70-20-10 Applied

A coaching conversation produces a goal. A development plan makes it happen. It does not need a form. It needs one page the member writes and you both keep.

70-20-10 Tells You Where to Put the Effort

You met the 70-20-10 Development Model in Chapter 22. Roughly seventy percent of development comes from challenging experience, twenty from other people, and ten from formal courses. Most development plans in the fire service are a list of classes, which means they are built entirely out of the ten.

70 · Experience
A real stretch
Acting officer time. Running the drill. Leading a station project from Chapter 44. Writing the pre-plan. Doing the size-up on the next fire with you coaching from the passenger seat.
20 · People
Exposure and feedback
A ride-along with the BC. A sit-down with the training captain. A mentor outside your station. Your own regular coaching conversation.
10 · Courses
Formal learning
Officer classes, certifications, a degree, a book you both read and talk about. Necessary, and the smallest slice.

The One-Page Plan

1
The goal, in their words
One sentence and a target date. "Ready to test for engineer in the spring process."
From the G in GROW
2
One stretch assignment
One real piece of work slightly beyond their current level, with a deadline.
The 70
3
One person to learn from
A name, and what they will ask that person.
The 20
4
One course or book
Scheduled, not "sometime."
The 10
5
The check-in rhythm
A recurring date. Monthly is enough.
Without this it is a wish

Giving a Stretch Assignment Safely

A stretch has to be real to work, which means it can go badly. Your job is to pick one where failure is survivable and to stay close enough to catch it. Brief the outcome, not the method. Agree on a midpoint check. Let them run it their way even when you would do it differently. Debrief with questions before you offer your view.

❌ Dumping — Loses Points
"I need the monthly training calendar done. You do it from now on." No reason given, no connection to their goals, no support. That is offloading work.
✅ Developing — Scores High
"You told me you want to be an officer. Officers plan training. I would like you to build next month's drill calendar and run the first drill. Show me a draft on the 20th and we will go over it together. I picked you for this because of how you taught the new guy forcible entry." Tied to their goal, clear outcome, midpoint check, and a reason.

Building the Bench

Ask yourself one question about your crew: if I were off for three months, who rides the seat? If the answer is nobody, that is your development priority. Assessors at the Captain level listen for succession. They want to hear that you are producing your own replacement on purpose.

Acting Time Is Coaching Time

When a firefighter rides up as acting officer, do not just hand over the seat. Before the shift: "What is one thing you want to work on today?" After the shift: "What surprised you? What would you do differently?" Ten minutes turns a staffing move into development.

The Hard Cases —
and L·E·A·D Applied

⏱ 10 min ⚡ Full Integration

Coaching the eager probationary firefighter is easy. These are the ones that test you.

Four Hard Coaching Cases

Case 1
The plateaued senior member
Twenty years on, excellent at the job, no interest in anything new. Do not assume the will is gone. Ask what they are proud of and what they would want to pass on before they leave. Many of them come alive when asked to teach.
Case 2
The fast riser
Talented, ambitious, and a step ahead of their own judgment. They need bigger assignments and honest feedback about how they land with the crew. Coach the humility along with the skill.
Case 3
The one who does not want to promote
That is a legitimate career. Growth does not have to mean rank. Ask what mastery looks like for them: a specialty team, instructor credentials, being the firefighter every officer wants.
Case 4
The peer you now supervise
You were friends last month. Say it directly: "This is new for both of us. I am still me. I am also responsible for your development now, and I am going to take that seriously." Then ask the GROW goal question like you would for anyone.

L·E·A·D on a Development Question

L
Label
Development & Mentoring. If the member is below standard, it is a Personnel Issue and you start with the 3 U's instead.
E
Engage
STAR-P. Development questions almost always reward a real example with a named person and a measurable result.
A
Apply
Skill/Will to diagnose, GROW for the conversation, 70-20-10 for the plan. Show that the member did the choosing.
D
Deliver
Close with Service or Humility: their success is the result, and it belongs to them.

The Complete Response

Model Response · STAR-P · "Tell us about a time you developed someone"
Situation
"Two years ago I had a firefighter on my crew, eight years on, very capable, who had never tested for anything."
Task
"As the acting officer on that crew, I felt it was my responsibility to find out whether that was a choice or whether nobody had asked."
Action
"I sat down with him and asked where he wanted to be in a few years. He said he had thought about engineer but did not think he was ready. I asked what ready would look like, and he said pump time. I asked how he could get it, and he came up with three ways. He picked two. I arranged for him to drive on returns, and I asked our engineer to coach him at drills. I also asked him to teach hydraulics to our probationary firefighter, because teaching it was the fastest way for him to own it. We checked in the first shift of every month."
Result
"He logged forty hours on the pump panel in six months, tested in the next process, and finished second. He was promoted that fall."
Principle
"I did not do much in that story except ask and follow up. That is what Service means to me as an officer. My job is to see more in people than they see in themselves yet, and then get out of the way."
Assessor
Real example. Asked before telling. Member built the plan. Experience-weighted development. Measurable result. Value close with humility.

Your Practice Target for This Chapter

Before your next shift ends, have one GROW conversation with someone on your crew who is doing fine. Just the four questions. Then bank it in your Experience Inventory, because you now have a recent, relevant development example for the board.

On the Job

Put a recurring reminder on your calendar: one coaching conversation per crew member per quarter. Four people, four conversations, fifteen minutes each. That is one hour a quarter to be the officer people remember.
