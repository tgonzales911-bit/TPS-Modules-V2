What Assessors Are Watching PA + JU

⏱ 16 minDimension: PA + JU
Fast Track
Frameworks + OB cards only

Supervisory and Administrative Judgment is one of the eight core assessment dimensions — and statistically, it is where the most candidates lose ground. Not because they lack knowledge, but because they underestimate how differently a company officer thinks compared to a senior firefighter.

The distinction is not about experience. It is about frame. A firefighter's frame is operational: what do we do right now? A company officer's frame is supervisory: what are the people, process, and organizational considerations that should shape what we do right now? Assessors are watching for which frame you operate from — and they can tell within the first sixty seconds of a scenario.

In the canonical eight-dimension model, the thinking this chapter builds maps to two distinct scored dimensions: Problem Analysis (diagnosing the real issue) and Judgment & Decision-Making (choosing and owning the response).

The Four Elements of Supervisory Judgment

Personnel Management

How you handle performance gaps, behavioral issues, interpersonal conflict, and personnel development. This is the most common subject of in-basket items and role-play scenarios because it is where most new officers get into trouble fastest.

Administrative Competence

How you manage documentation, policy compliance, reporting requirements, and the administrative load of a company officer position. Candidates underweight this because it feels mundane — but assessors score it because it is where real officers fail most often.

Judgment Under Ambiguity

How you make decisions when you do not have all the information you want. The scenarios that reveal judgment most clearly are the ones where reasonable people could disagree — and where your reasoning process matters more than your conclusion.

Organizational Awareness

The degree to which your decisions reflect an understanding of the institution you serve — its chain of command, its policies, its community relationships, and its values. A decision that would be appropriate on the fireground may be completely wrong in an officer context when organizational factors are in play.

The Dimension Signal

Every supervisory judgment scenario is asking the same underlying question: do you understand that a company officer's job is to diagnose people and process, decide with a named principle, and act within the authority of the rank — not simply to solve the operational problem in front of you?

Dimension Focus — Problem Analysis + Judgment & Decision-Making

This chapter develops two scored dimensions. Diagnosing (the 3 U's, Skill/Will, reading the situation) is Problem Analysis. Choosing the response, naming the principle, and owning the call is Judgment & Decision-Making. The strongest candidates make both visible.

"A firefighter cuts a corner on a routine call — no one is hurt, but policy wasn't followed. As a new company officer, walk us through how you think about this differently than you would have as a senior firefighter."
This tests frame. A firefighter's frame is operational ("what do we do right now?"); a company officer's is supervisory ("what people, process, and organizational considerations shape what we do?"). Name the frame shift explicitly — assessors read it in the first 60 seconds. Diagnose before you decide (Problem Analysis), then make the supervisory call (Judgment & Decision-Making). Deliver at rank, close on the frame that separates officer from firefighter.

The 3 U's Diagnostic Framework PA

⏱ 20 minDimension: PA
Fast Track
Frameworks + OB cards only

Before you can respond to a supervisory situation appropriately, you need to diagnose it correctly. The 3 U's framework is a diagnostic tool — not a response script — that helps you identify why a performance or behavior problem exists so you can respond with the right intervention.

Applying the wrong response to a correctly identified problem is ineffective. Applying a response before you have correctly diagnosed the problem is worse. The 3 U's framework prevents both errors by forcing a diagnostic step before the response step.

The Three Diagnoses

U
Unaware
The person does not know that a problem exists, that their performance is below standard, or that their behavior is not acceptable. They cannot fix what they do not know is broken. The response is information and clear communication of expectations — not discipline.
U
Unable
The person knows the expectation but lacks the skill, knowledge, or resources to meet it. They want to perform — they simply cannot yet. The response is training, coaching, mentoring, or resource provision — not discipline.
U
Unwilling
The person knows the expectation, has the capability to meet it, and is choosing not to. This is the only situation where a disciplinary response is appropriate — and even then, the response must be documented, proportionate, and consistent with department policy.

How to Apply the 3 U's in a Scenario

When a supervisory scenario presents a problem — a firefighter repeatedly arriving late, a crew member whose documentation is consistently incomplete, a subordinate whose tone toward the public is inappropriate — the 3 U's give you a diagnostic sequence to follow before you decide how to respond:

  1. Does this person know a problem exists? If not, the response is a clear, private, documented conversation establishing the expectation.
  2. Does this person have the capability to meet the expectation? If not, the response is support, training, or coaching.
  3. Is this a choice? If yes, the response escalates accordingly — but always within your authority level and in alignment with department policy.

The Assessment Application

In oral board scenarios involving personnel issues, do not rush to a response. State your diagnostic process explicitly: "Before I decide how to respond, I want to understand whether this person is Unaware, Unable, or Unwilling — because each situation calls for a different approach." Assessors reward the diagnostic instinct, not just the correct response.

Common Misapplications

Treating an Unaware situation as Unwilling produces unnecessary disciplinary escalation and damages trust. Treating an Unwilling situation as Unable produces ineffective coaching the person has no motivation to accept. Failing to distinguish between the three produces generic responses that assessors immediately recognize as low-level supervisory thinking.

Dimension Focus — Problem Analysis

This entire lesson lives in the Problem Analysis dimension: separating symptom from cause, and naming why the problem exists, before any action word leaves your mouth.

Oral Board · Diagnose Before You RespondLieutenant
"A normally reliable firefighter has been late three times in two weeks. Before you decide what to do, how do you diagnose what's actually going on?"
This is pure Problem Analysis. Use the 3 U's as a diagnostic step — don't jump to a response. Separate the real cause from the surface symptom; the right response to a wrongly-diagnosed problem still fails. Name that you're diagnosing before deciding. Deliver at rank, close on diagnosis as the precondition for the right intervention.

The Skill/Will Matrix JU + LD

⏱ 22 minDimension: JU + LD
Fast Track
Frameworks + OB cards only

The Skill/Will Matrix is the companion tool to the 3 U's. Where the 3 U's help you diagnose the problem, the Skill/Will Matrix helps you select the right leadership style for the individual and the situation.

A company officer who leads every person the same way — regardless of their skill level or motivation — is not leading. They are applying a default that is correct sometimes and wrong most of the time.

The Four Quadrants

High Skill · High Will
Delegate
Has both capability and motivation. Micromanaging them damages motivation and wastes your capacity. Give clear outcomes, provide resources, and get out of the way. Check in — but do not hover.
Low Skill · High Will
Coach / Develop
Wants to perform but lacks the skill. Needs structured guidance, demonstration, and patient feedback — not discipline. Investing in this quadrant produces long-term organizational strength.
High Skill · Low Will
Motivate / Direct
Your most difficult scenario. The person can do the job but is choosing not to. Investigate the cause — temporary disengagement, misalignment, or something else? The response depends on the root cause. Do not let it drift.
Low Skill · Low Will
Structure / Supervise
Needs close direction and clear expectations. Do not delegate to this quadrant. Provide explicit guidance, document your conversations, and monitor closely. If motivation does not improve with support, escalate appropriately.

Using the Matrix in an Assessment Scenario

Assessment scenarios will present you with a person — a firefighter, a crew member, a new hire — and a situation. Your job is to identify their quadrant before selecting your response. You do this by asking two diagnostic questions: Does this person have the skill to perform as expected? Does this person have the motivation to perform as expected?

Name the quadrant explicitly in your response: "Based on what I know, this firefighter has the skill — they've done this correctly before — but something has changed in their motivation. I'm going to treat this as a High Skill / Low Will situation and find out what's driving the change before I decide on a response." Assessors score the diagnostic thinking, not just the outcome.

Coach's Note — The Most Common Error

The most common assessment error with the Skill/Will Matrix is jumping to the Low Skill / Low Will quadrant because the scenario makes the person sound difficult. Before you apply the most restrictive response, confirm both dimensions. A person who is Low Skill / Low Will requires a different intervention than one who is Low Skill / High Will. Getting this wrong signals low-level supervisory thinking to assessors.

Personnel Items — The Trap Scenarios PA → JU

⏱ 24 minDimension: PA → JU
Fast Track
Frameworks + OB cards only

Assessment centers are engineered to reveal how candidates perform under pressure in situations that have no clean answer. The trap scenarios in the supervisory judgment area are the ones where the most natural instinct — the response that feels right in the moment — is also the one assessors score lowest. Understanding the pattern of these traps is as important as knowing the frameworks.

The Five Common Trap Patterns

Trap 1 — Acting Before Gathering

A scenario presents a complaint, a conflict, or a performance issue. The trap is to apply a solution immediately — to decide what to do before you have gathered the information you would need to decide correctly. The correct response is always to gather first: meet with the involved parties separately, get the facts, review relevant documentation, and then decide.

Trap 2 — Overpromising Resolution

A scenario involves a subordinate or community member who wants a definitive answer — "will this be fixed?" or "will this person be disciplined?" The trap is to promise an outcome you cannot guarantee or that requires approval above your rank. The correct response is to commit to a process: "I will investigate, document, and take appropriate action consistent with department policy. I cannot promise you a specific outcome, but I can promise I will handle this seriously."

Trap 3 — Disciplining the Wrong Dimension

A scenario involves a performance gap that stems from lack of training, unclear expectations, or inadequate resources — but is framed to sound like a conduct issue. The trap is to apply discipline when the correct response is development. Applying the 3 U's diagnostic before responding prevents this error.

Trap 4 — Avoiding the Difficult Conversation

A scenario involves interpersonal conflict, conduct concerns, or a crew member whose behavior is affecting others. The trap is to document without confronting, or to confront in a way that is vague and non-specific. The correct response is a direct, private, documented conversation that names the specific behavior, explains the impact, and establishes a clear expectation — followed by documentation of that conversation.

Trap 5 — Acting Outside Your Authority

A scenario presents a problem that would be satisfying to resolve — but the resolution requires approval, resources, or authority above your rank. The trap is to commit to a resolution you do not have the authority to provide. The correct response is to name your authority level, commit to what you can do, and identify the escalation path for what falls outside your rank.

The Five Traps That Sink Candidates
The Meta-Pattern

Every trap scenario tests the same underlying competency: can you manage the discomfort of uncertainty without collapsing into premature action? The candidates who score highest gather, diagnose, and act within their authority — even when the scenario is engineered to make immediate action feel necessary.

Dimension Focus — PA → JU Bridge

Disciplined Problem Analysis (gather, diagnose) is what makes your Judgment & Decision-Making defensible. Every trap above is a failure to diagnose before deciding, or to decide within the bounds of the rank.

Oral Board · The "Act Before Gathering" TrapLieutenant
"A firefighter comes to you furious, accusing a crewmate of unsafe behavior on the last call. Your instinct is to act. What do you actually do?"
This is the #1 trap — acting before gathering. The natural, in-the-moment response (decide immediately) is what assessors score lowest. Gather first: meet parties separately, get facts, review documentation — then decide. This is the PA→JU bridge: diagnosis disciplines the decision. Deliver at rank, close on gather-then-act.

Documentation Language That Scores WC + JU

⏱ 18 minDimension: WC + JU
Fast Track
Frameworks + OB cards only

How you write in an assessment center is as important as how you speak. Documentation exercises — in-basket responses, written summaries, memo drafts — reveal the same supervisory thinking that oral board responses do. The language you use signals whether you think like a firefighter or like a company officer.

The Four Principles of Documentation Language

1. Specific and Behavioral

Document observable behavior, not interpretation. "Firefighter Jones arrived at 0815 on Tuesday and Wednesday this week, both times 15 minutes after the 0800 shift briefing" is documentation. "Firefighter Jones has been having attitude problems and doesn't seem motivated" is opinion. Assessors score specificity because it holds up in a grievance process and demonstrates supervisory maturity.

2. Objective and Professional

Remove adjectives that imply judgment about character and replace them with descriptions of conduct. "Unprofessional behavior" becomes "raised voice during crew briefing in front of three other crew members." "Poor performance" becomes "missed three documentation deadlines in the past 30 days."

3. Impact-Aware

Connect the documented behavior to its organizational impact. Why does this matter? How does it affect the crew, the mission, the community, or the department? Documentation that establishes impact demonstrates that the officer understands the supervisory context — not just the isolated incident.

4. Action-Oriented

Documentation should always end with a next step: what will you do, when will you follow up, what is the expectation going forward? A document that records an incident without establishing a path forward is incomplete. Assessors look for the follow-through signal.

The Written Exercise Connection

In written exercise scenarios, you will often be asked to respond to a memo, draft a communication, or summarize a situation. Apply these same four principles: be specific, be behavioral, acknowledge impact, and include a clear next action — and structure the response itself using the Five-Paragraph Formula you will master in Chapter 8 (Written Communication). The written exercise is a documentation task wearing a different costume: the four principles govern your language, the Five-Paragraph Formula governs your structure.

Coach's Note

Read your documentation aloud before finalizing it in a practice session. If it sounds like you are describing a person's character rather than their conduct, rewrite it. Assessors have seen thousands of documents. They know the difference between supervisory writing and complaint writing — and they score accordingly.
