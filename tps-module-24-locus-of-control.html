Internal vs External Locus — The Ownership Mindset

What Locus of Control Is

Internal locus does not mean believing you control everything. It means your first question is always: "What can I do differently?" — not "Why did this happen to me?"

In 1954, psychologist Julian Rotter introduced the concept of locus of control — the degree to which people believe they have control over the outcomes of their lives. The word locus is Latin for location or place. The central question: where do you locate the cause of what happens to you?

Internal Locus of Control
The belief that your outcomes are primarily determined by your own actions, decisions, effort, and preparation. When something goes wrong, you look first at what you could have done differently. When something goes right, you connect it to the specific choices that produced it. In fire service: "I didn't score as well as I wanted. I need to go back and identify the specific gaps in my preparation and address them directly."
External Locus of Control
The belief that your outcomes are primarily determined by external forces — luck, the system, other people's decisions, circumstances beyond your control. When something goes wrong, the explanation is outside yourself. When something goes right, it tends to be attributed to favorable external conditions. In fire service: "I didn't score as well as I wanted. The assessors were biased. The process is political. The questions weren't fair."
The Critical Distinction

Internal locus of control does not mean believing you control everything — that is delusion, not leadership. It means that when you face an outcome you do not want, your first question is "what can I do differently?" rather than "why did this happen to me?" The internal locus leader acknowledges external factors and then immediately focuses on what is within their agency to change.

Why Locus of Control Predicts Leadership Effectiveness

Decades of research since Rotter's original work has consistently found that internal locus of control correlates with higher leadership effectiveness, greater resilience under adversity, better performance under stress, higher levels of initiative and persistence, and stronger developmental growth over time.

The mechanism is straightforward: if you believe your actions determine your outcomes, you invest in improving your actions. If you believe external forces determine your outcomes, you invest in managing, avoiding, or complaining about those forces — none of which produces development.

The Fire Service Context

Fire service promotional culture has a specific external locus vulnerability that coaching must address directly. The promotional process in many departments is genuinely imperfect — there are political dynamics, inconsistent assessment quality, and structural inequities that real candidates experience and name accurately. This makes external locus attribution feel not just available but justified.

The coaching challenge is not to deny these external realities — they exist. The coaching challenge is to help candidates understand that spending energy on what they cannot change produces no development, while spending energy on what they can control produces promotion.

The Trap

A candidate who has tested three times and placed just outside the promotion band each time has accumulated three rounds of evidence for external attribution. "The process is unfair." "I keep getting passed over." "The system is broken." Every failed test is another data point supporting the external narrative — and every round of external attribution is a round of development that does not happen. The internal locus intervention is not "the process is fair." It is "regardless of whether the process is fair, the only variable you control is your own performance. Let's focus there."

Locus of Control and T·C·H·O·E·D·S

The connection to your existing TPS framework is direct and powerful:

  • Ownership — "I for failures, WE for achievements" is the behavioral expression of internal locus. The leader who owns failures operates from internal locus. The leader who explains failures with external factors operates from external locus.
  • Courage — It takes courage to look inward when external attribution is easier and more socially acceptable. The internal locus candidate who says "I need to develop my written communication" in a culture where "the process is rigged" is the dominant narrative is demonstrating Courage.
  • Humility — Internal locus requires the humility to acknowledge that your outcomes are a reflection of your development level — not of the system's unfairness. This is uncomfortable. Humility enables that discomfort.
The Assessment Center Identity Statement

"I am the kind of [rank] who looks inward first — who asks what I can do differently before asking what the system could do differently. That orientation is what makes development possible and what ultimately produces promotion."

Locus of Control Is Not Fixed

The most important coaching insight in this lesson: locus of control is not a personality trait you are born with. It is a pattern of attribution that can be identified, challenged, and shifted through deliberate coaching intervention. Candidates who present with strongly external locus patterns can and do develop internal locus orientation — particularly when coaching creates the safety to explore those patterns without judgment.

The six-week coaching engagement is exactly the right window for this work. The internal locus shift often produces more performance improvement than any framework coaching because it changes the candidate's fundamental orientation toward their own development.

Covey's Three Circles — Where Your Energy Goes Is Where Your Influence Goes

The Three Circles

◆ TPS Framework · Chapter 37 · Covey's Three Circles Your Focus Is a Gravitational Force

The Paradox That Changes Everything

Here is the counterintuitive truth that Covey's research and decades of subsequent organizational psychology has validated:

The Energy Paradox

Leaders who focus their energy on the Circle of Concern — worrying about, analyzing, and complaining about what they cannot control — experience a shrinking Circle of Influence over time. Their energy is consumed by what they cannot change, leaving less available for what they can. Leaders who focus their energy on their Circle of Control and Circle of Influence experience a growing Circle of Influence over time. As they develop and perform, more of what was previously "concern" becomes "influence."

Why This Happens in Fire Service Specifically

Fire service culture has a specific pattern of Circle of Concern focus that coaching must recognize and address: the crew room conversation. The debrief that becomes a department criticism session. The shift that starts with a complaint about the new policy, the chief's decision, or the promotional process. These conversations feel like leadership engagement — but they are Circle of Concern energy that produces nothing except the shared comfort of collective grievance.

The leader who redirects — not by dismissing legitimate concerns but by consistently steering toward what the team can actually influence — is building a Circle of Influence culture that over time produces more organizational change than any amount of concern-focused conversation.

The Military Version — The Three Rings

The military operational version of Covey's model, used extensively in officer development, divides the landscape into three categories that translate directly to fire service leadership:

What I Control Directly
My actions, my decisions, my preparation, my response to adversity, my communication choices, my effort level, my leadership behavior in this moment. No leader has more agency than in this ring — and most leaders systematically underinvest here because the other rings are louder.
What I Influence But Don't Control
My crew's behavior and development, my station's culture, my peers' decisions, my organization's direction through my advocacy and example. I can affect these — but the outcome is not mine alone to determine. Investment here produces results at a longer time horizon than direct control, but the results are more organizationally durable.
What I Must Accept and Adapt To
The budget allocation, the promotional process design, the city management decision, the department's history, the union contract, other candidates' preparation. These are real. They affect outcomes. And spending more than minimal energy on them produces zero return. The only productive energy investment here is acknowledging the constraint and then asking: given this constraint, what is the best available action within my control?

Application at Each Rank

RankCommon Circle of Concern TrapCircle of Control Redirect

The Assessment Center Application

This is where the framework becomes immediately scoreable. In any assessment center scenario — especially at BC and above — there are elements outside the candidate's control (budget, staffing levels, city decisions) and elements within it (how they communicate, what they prioritize, what they develop, how they lead). The candidate who structures their response around their Circle of Control scores higher than the candidate who spends their response on the constraints in their Circle of Concern.

Live Example — The Circle of Concern Response (3/5)
BC-level strategic scenario
"This is a difficult situation because the department is understaffed, the budget has been cut, and the city manager doesn't understand our operational needs. The union contract makes it hard to implement changes and the previous leadership left a lot of culture problems. I would try to work within these constraints as best I could."
This response is almost entirely Circle of Concern. It names real constraints accurately — and then stops there. Assessors write: "Candidate acknowledged constraints but demonstrated no proactive leadership thinking. Appears to be waiting for better conditions before acting."
Live Example — The Circle of Control Response (4/5)
Same BC-level strategic scenario
"The budget constraint and staffing challenge are real — I'm not going to pretend otherwise. But before I address what I cannot change, I want to identify what is within my direct control and influence. Within my Circle of Control: how I prioritize my Captains' development, how I use the budget I do have, how I communicate upward about operational needs. Within my Circle of Influence: the battalion's culture, my Captains' buy-in, the relationships I build with city management that position future budget requests. My response focuses there — because energy spent on the constraint itself produces nothing."
Assessors write: "Candidate acknowledged constraints and immediately redirected to proactive leadership. Named analytical framework. Demonstrates organizational thinking appropriate for BC level."

Locus of Control in the Assessment Center

The Scoring Reality

Locus of control is not explicitly named on most assessment center rubrics. But it is implicitly scored in every dimension, every exercise, every scenario. Here is why:

The behavioral indicators assessors are trained to look for — proactive thinking, taking ownership of outcomes, proposing solutions rather than naming obstacles, connecting decisions to personal leadership principles — are all behavioral expressions of internal locus of control. The behavioral indicators that lower scores — blame shifting, constraint-focused responses, reactive thinking, passive framing — are all behavioral expressions of external locus.

When an assessor writes "candidate demonstrated strong Supervisory Judgment" they are often observing internal locus behavior without naming it as such. When they write "candidate appeared to be waiting for the problem to resolve itself" they are observing external locus behavior.

The Three Assessment Center Manifestations

Manifestation 1 — Constraint-First Responses

The external locus candidate begins responses by naming constraints. "This is a difficult situation because..." "Given the staffing limitations..." "With the budget we have..." These openers immediately signal to assessors that the candidate's primary orientation is toward what they cannot control. Even when the constraint is real and relevant, leading with it signals the wrong analytical frame.

Internal locus reframe: Acknowledge constraints briefly and pivot immediately to action. "I recognize the staffing constraint — and within that constraint, here is my approach." One sentence on the concern, the rest of the response on the control.

Manifestation 2 — Passive Action Language

External locus responses use passive or qualified action language. "I would try to..." "I would attempt to..." "I would hope that..." "I would see if..." These constructions signal uncertainty about whether action will produce outcomes — which is external locus in linguistic form.

Internal locus reframe: Declarative action language. "I will..." "My first action is..." "I am responsible for..." The confidence of the language signals the confidence of the leadership orientation.

Manifestation 3 — Outcome Attribution Errors

In leadership philosophy and development scenarios, external locus candidates attribute team and individual outcomes to external factors rather than to their own leadership. "FF Jones has been like this his whole career." "This station has always had culture problems." "The previous captain left this mess."

Internal locus reframe: Own the environment you inherited. "I inherited this situation — and what I choose to do with it from today forward is entirely within my control. My approach begins with..." This is the Extreme Ownership principle — the no-bad-teams insight — expressed in assessment center language.

The 5/5 Integration — Locus + Frameworks

A 5/5 response does not just demonstrate internal locus — it names the principle. The candidate who says "my approach here is grounded in the Ownership element of T·C·H·O·E·D·S — I own everything within my circle of control, and I focus my energy there" is doing three things simultaneously: demonstrating internal locus behavior, naming the TPS framework, and closing with a leadership principle. That integration is what assessors score at the highest level.

Practice Scenario — All Ranks
Bucket: Leadership Philosophy · Bloom's Level: Evaluate (L5)
"Describe a time when you faced a significant leadership challenge that was partially or largely outside your control. How did you approach it and what did you learn?"
This scenario is designed specifically to test locus of control orientation. The 3/5 candidate describes the external factors in detail and their response as "doing the best they could." The 4/5 candidate explicitly identifies what was within their circle of control, describes the actions they took in that space, and connects the outcome to those specific actions. The 5/5 candidate names the Ownership element of T·C·H·O·E·D·S, articulates the circle of control / circle of concern distinction explicitly, and closes with an identity statement: "What this experience taught me is that the internal locus orientation — focusing on what I can control — is not just a tactical choice. It is the foundational mindset that makes leadership development possible."

Coaching Out of External Locus Patterns

Recognizing the Pattern

External locus patterns in coaching clients present in predictable ways. Learning to recognize them quickly allows you to address the root cause rather than the surface symptom:

Signal 1 — The Testing History Narrative
The candidate's account of previous assessments focuses heavily on external factors — the process, the assessors, the competition, the timing, the department's culture. They can describe in detail why the assessment was unfair or disadvantageous. They have less to say about what they would do differently in their preparation or performance.
Signal 2 — Homework Externalizing
When homework is incomplete, the explanation is always external — shift schedule, family obligations, department demands. Some of these are genuine. The pattern to watch for: the candidate who consistently has an external explanation, never examines what they could have controlled about their time management, and never takes ownership of the non-completion.
Signal 3 — Practice Rep Debriefing
After a practice rep that doesn't go well, the external locus candidate explains the performance with scenario factors: "That wasn't a realistic scenario." "That question was unusual." "I was distracted today." The internal locus candidate says: "I lost my structure in the Action section. What do I need to do differently next rep?"
Signal 4 — The Qualified Commitment
"I'll try to complete this homework." "I'll do my best with the Practice Engine." "I'll see if I can make time." The linguistic qualification signals the candidate is already locating the outcome in external factors (whether time permits, whether circumstances allow) rather than in their own agency.

The Coaching Intervention Sequence

Shifting a candidate from external to internal locus orientation is identity work — which means it follows the same principles as all identity coaching. It cannot be lectured. It must be experienced. Here is the sequence:

Step 1 — Create Awareness Without Judgment

Name the pattern without evaluating it. "I want to share an observation. In the last three practice reps, your debrief has focused primarily on the scenario factors — what made the question unusual or difficult. I want to explore that pattern with you." The candidate who feels judged for their locus of control orientation will become defensive. The candidate who feels curious about it will engage.

Step 2 — The Ownership Audit

Ask the candidate to complete this exercise verbally in session:

The Ownership Audit Questions
1. "Thinking about your most recent assessment center — what percentage of the outcome was within your control?"
2. "Of the things within your control — your preparation, your practice frequency, your framework application — which ones did you fully optimize?"
3. "If you had spent zero energy on the things outside your control and redirected all of it to the things within your control — what would you have done differently?"
4. "What would your score have been if you had done those things?"

The Ownership Audit typically produces a shift in the candidate's own thinking — they arrive at the internal locus insight themselves rather than receiving it as an instruction. That self-generated insight is significantly more durable than any coaching directive.

Step 3 — The Identity Reframe

Once the candidate has identified the external locus pattern in their own thinking, connect it to the identity work:

"The identity you described earlier — 'I am the kind of [rank] who develops people and builds high-performing teams' — requires an internal locus orientation to sustain under pressure. A leader with that identity doesn't explain their team's underperformance with external factors. They look inward first. How does this pattern we're observing connect to the identity you want to build?"

Step 4 — The Language Contract

In sessions going forward, establish a coaching contract around language: any time the candidate uses external locus language in a practice rep — "I would try to," "given the constraints," "this has always been a problem" — you name it in real time. Not to correct, but to create awareness. "Did you notice that construction? What would an internal locus version of that sentence sound like?"

Over several sessions, candidates begin self-monitoring and self-correcting. The language shift is both the symptom and the mechanism of the identity shift — changing how you talk about leadership changes how you think about it.

The Hard Case — The Justified Externalizer

The most challenging coaching scenario: the candidate whose external attributions are largely accurate. The department genuinely is political. The process genuinely has been unfair to them. Their external attributions are not distorted — they are observations of real organizational dynamics.

The coaching is more delicate here but the principle is unchanged: the accuracy of the external observation is separate from its utility as a developmental orientation. A candidate can simultaneously say "this process has real inequities" AND "regardless of those inequities, here is what I control and what I am choosing to invest in." Both can be true. The coaching question is not whether the external attribution is accurate — it is whether it is useful.

The One Sentence That Reframes Everything

"You may be completely right about the external factors. And none of that changes what is available to you within your circle of control. Let's focus there — because that is where promotion lives."

Using This in Session Notes

When you observe external locus patterns in a coaching session, note them specifically in your session notes. This creates the longitudinal record that allows you to track the shift over the six-week engagement. A candidate who enters session one with strong external attribution and exits session six with consistent internal language has made an identity-level change — and that change will show up in their assessment center performance in ways that framework coaching alone cannot produce.

From Reactive to Proactive Leadership

Reactive vs Proactive — The Covey Distinction

Covey uses the term proactive deliberately and specifically. It does not mean enthusiastic or fast-moving. It means taking responsibility for your own life and choices — recognizing that between stimulus and response, there is a space. And in that space lies your freedom and your power to choose your response.

Reactive leadership is stimulus-response: something happens, you respond based on instinct, habit, emotion, or circumstance. Proactive leadership is stimulus-space-response: something happens, you recognize the space between, you choose your response based on values and principles rather than circumstance and emotion.

This distinction — and the specific language around it — is one of the most powerful leadership concepts you can embed in a candidate's assessment center vocabulary, because it directly connects to what assessors are looking for in Supervisory Judgment and Leadership Philosophy scenarios at every rank.

Viktor Frankl's Insight — The Source of Covey's Framework

Covey credits Viktor Frankl — the Austrian psychiatrist and Holocaust survivor who wrote Man's Search for Meaning — as the source of this insight. Frankl observed, during his years in Nazi concentration camps, that the one freedom that could never be taken from him was his freedom to choose his response to any circumstance. The space between stimulus and response is where human freedom lives. Proactive leadership is the deliberate cultivation and use of that space — in any circumstance, including the most adverse ones.

The Stimulus-Response Model in Fire Service

Fire service creates specific stimulus-response traps that proactive leadership must navigate:

The Rank-Authority Stimulus

A crew member challenges the Lieutenant's decision publicly. The reactive response: defensiveness, discipline, or backing down. The proactive response: recognize the stimulus, pause in the space, and choose a response based on the leadership principle that best serves the team and the individual. The Courage element of T·C·H·O·E·D·S — delivered calmly, privately, and developmentally — rather than the reactive emotion-driven response.

The Inherited Problem Stimulus

A newly promoted Captain inherits a dysfunctional station culture. The reactive response: "I didn't create this" (external locus) or "I need to fix everything immediately" (reactive urgency). The proactive response: acknowledge the inheritance, identify what is within the Circle of Control, and begin the systematic trust-building work that precedes any cultural change.

The Failed Assessment Stimulus

The candidate receives a placement that doesn't result in promotion. The reactive response: external attribution, discouragement, or giving up. The proactive response: use the space between the result and the next action to choose a development-focused orientation — "What specifically can I improve? What is within my circle of control to change before the next opportunity?"
