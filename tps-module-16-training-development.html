The Company Officer as Lead Trainer —
Why Training Ownership Matters

⏱ 12 min🎓 Adult Learning · Training Culture

Training is the company officer's most important ongoing leadership function — because it is the only function that directly determines whether the crew can perform safely and effectively when the stakes are highest. The Lieutenant who treats training as an administrative burden delegates the most critical officer responsibility they hold.

The NFPA 1021 Training Requirement

NFPA 1021 FO I requires the company officer to design and conduct training programs for their crew. This is not a suggestion or a supplemental activity — it is a core competency of the rank. The company officer who cannot design, conduct, and evaluate training is not meeting the NFPA standard for their position.

Why Adults Learn Differently
Andragogy, Not Pedagogy
Malcolm Knowles' adult learning theory identifies key differences between adult and child learners: adults are self-directed, draw on existing experience, learn best when they see relevance to immediate problems, and prefer problem-centered over subject-centered learning. The company officer who designs training around real operational scenarios rather than abstract concepts is applying andragogy correctly.
The Training Culture Signal
What the Officer Models
The officer who trains with the crew — who is visibly invested in skill development, who takes the training as seriously as they expect the crew to, and who treats every drill as preparation for performance — creates a training culture. The one who delegates training to the newest member and checks their phone during drills creates a different culture.

Training Is Bigger Than the Drill Ground — 70-20-10

Most candidates equate "training" with scheduled drills. Research on how leaders actually develop says the drill is the smallest slice. The 70-20-10 model holds that roughly 70 percent of capability is built through challenging on-the-job experience, about 20 percent through developmental relationships, and only about 10 percent through formal coursework. Owning your crew’s development means deliberately engineering the 70 and the 20 — the stretch assignment, the acting-officer rep, the honest coaching conversation — not just filling the 10. You go deeper on this in The Developing Leader (Chapter 22).

Training Needs Assessment —
What Does My Crew Actually Need?

⏱ 14 min📊 Needs Assessment · Gap Analysis

Effective training begins with an honest assessment of what the crew needs to learn or improve — not what was scheduled last year, not what is easiest to deliver, but what the data says about actual performance gaps. The company officer who trains based on needs analysis produces better outcomes than the one who trains based on calendar.

OBS
Observation — What Do I See During Operations?
The most accessible needs assessment data is direct observation: how does the crew perform during training exercises, during routine calls, and during significant incidents? The officer who observes systematically — noting specific performance gaps rather than general impressions — builds the data that drives targeted training.
AFT
After-Action Review Data — What Did the AAR Reveal?
After-action reviews of significant incidents (covered in Module 21) produce specific, actionable training needs. "The attack crew lost their orientation to the entry door during the second-floor search" is a specific training need. "We need to work on teamwork" is not.
REC
Records Analysis — What Do Certification and Training Records Show?
Training records that show certification gaps, recertification approaching, or skills not drilled in 6+ months are a needs assessment data source. The officer who reviews records before planning the monthly training calendar is doing data-driven training.
BEN
Benchmark Standards — What Does the NFPA Standard Require?
NFPA standards, department SOGs, and certification requirements define the minimum performance standards. The gap between current demonstrated performance and required standard is the training need.
INC
Incident Data — What Patterns Appear in the Department's Incident History?
Near-miss reports, LODD investigations, and incident reviews from other departments are valuable training needs data. The training that prevents the incident that happened to another company is the training with the highest return.
The Learning Objectives Standard

Every training session should begin with clear learning objectives — specific, behavioral, and measurable. "Participants will be able to perform a complete building survey using COAL WAS WEALTH within 15 minutes with 90% information accuracy" is a learning objective. "We'll practice size-up today" is a topic. Learning objectives allow the trainer to evaluate whether learning occurred.

Diagnose Before You Design — Is It a Training Problem at All?

A needs assessment is a diagnosis, and the most expensive officer error is prescribing a drill for a problem training cannot fix. Before you build a single evolution, run the same diagnostic frameworks you would use on any personnel issue — the 3 U’s and the Skill/Will Matrix from Supervisory Judgment (Chapter 5), and Heifetz’s technical-versus-adaptive test from Change Management (Chapter 27).

Only when the diagnosis reads "Unable, technical, and here is the competence level" have you confirmed this is a training problem — and what kind of training it actually needs. That single sentence in a board response separates a supervisor who schedules drills from an officer who develops people.

Lesson Design and Delivery —
The Four-Phase Approach

⏱ 14 min📐 Lesson Plan · Delivery · Evaluation

A well-designed training session follows a predictable structure that research shows maximizes adult learning and retention. The company officer who uses this structure produces training that sticks rather than training that fills time.

MOT
Motivation — Why Should They Care?
Every effective training session begins with establishing relevance: why does this skill or knowledge matter to this crew, in their specific operational context? The motivation phase connects the training content to actual operational performance, to crew safety, or to outcomes the crew cares about. Adults who understand why they're learning something retain it better.
CON
Concept/Content — What Are We Learning?
The content delivery phase: the specific knowledge, skill, or process being trained. Uses demonstration, lecture, visual aids, case studies, or readings — selected based on the type of learning required. Motor skills require demonstration and practice. Knowledge requires explanation and application. Attitude change requires reflection and discussion.
APP
Application — Let Them Do It
Adult learners retain most effectively when they apply what they've learned in a practice context that is as realistic as possible. The training session that ends with demonstration without application is a training session that produced 20% retention rather than 60–70%.
EVL
Evaluation — Did Learning Occur?
Evaluation confirms whether the learning objective was met. Observation of skill performance, verbal quiz, written assessment, or demonstration under simulated conditions — all appropriate depending on the learning objective. Evaluation without feedback to the learner is incomplete: they need to know not just that they were evaluated but what the evaluation showed.
The Tabletop vs Functional vs Full-Scale Spectrum

Not every training need requires a full-scale drill. Tabletop exercises — verbal walk-throughs of scenarios — are effective for decision-making and knowledge-based objectives. Functional drills — executing specific functions with equipment — build skill for specific tasks. Full-scale exercises are reserved for complex, multi-company, or multi-agency scenarios where the full coordination is the training objective. Matching the exercise type to the learning objective saves resources and improves training effectiveness.

Design the Way the Science Says — SPARR and Making It Stick

The four-phase structure above is sound scaffolding. To build training that actually changes fireground behavior, run each skill through SPARR — the deliberate-practice method this entire program is built on (SPARR, Chapter 3) — and design in the most validated findings in all of learning science.

S
Spot
Target the one specific weakness, not the whole skill. Precision beats volume — name the exact gap the rep is built to close.
P
Picture
Have the learner mentally rehearse what excellence looks like before they move. A clear mental model of the standard precedes the attempt.
A
Actively Practice
Effortful, hands-on reps — performing aloud or in motion, not watching a demonstration. Thinking through a skill is not practicing it.
R
Run It Live
Observed, timed, under realistic pressure. Practice comfort does not transfer to performance under stress — the arousal of being watched is the mechanism, not a side effect.
R
Repeat
One correction, then run it again. The feedback-refinement loop is where skill is actually built — a single rep teaches almost nothing.
How Learning Actually Sticks — Build These Into Every Session

Two findings dominate the science of learning, and both belong in your lesson design. Retrieval practice: make the crew recall and perform, don’t re-show — the act of testing is itself the learning event, not a measurement of it. Spaced repetition: revisit a skill across multiple shifts rather than massing it into one session; spacing dramatically outperforms cramming for durable retention. And interleave — mix related skills rather than drilling one to exhaustion; it feels harder in the moment and transfers far better to the unpredictable fireground. The knowledge checks in this program are retrieval practice and the way chapters resurface is spacing — you have been the experiment.

Documentation + Training Records

⏱ 12 min📄 Records · NFPA 1401 · Liability

Training documentation is simultaneously a learning reinforcement tool, a personnel record, a certification maintenance record, and an organizational liability protection mechanism. The company officer who maintains accurate, current training records is protecting their crew, themselves, and the organization.

What to Document
Every Training Event
Date, location, duration, topic, learning objectives, attendees, and evaluation results for every training event — formal and informal. The drill that isn't documented may as well not have happened from a liability and certification perspective.
Certification Tracking
The Officer's Currency Matrix
Each crew member's certifications — Firefighter I/II, Hazmat Awareness/Operations, EMS certifications, technical rescue, driver-operator — with expiration dates. The officer who finds out a certification expired by receiving notice of a training failure has been managing records reactively. Proactive tracking prevents gaps.
NFPA 1401
Recommended Practice for Fire Service Training Reports and Records
NFPA 1401 provides guidance on what training records should contain, how they should be maintained, and what retention periods are appropriate. Following this standard protects the department in litigation and ensures records are useful for evaluation and improvement.
The Liability Connection
Documentation as Legal Protection
In litigation following a firefighter injury or death, one of the first questions is: what training was provided? The department that can demonstrate a consistent, documented training program meeting professional standards is in a fundamentally different legal position than the one that cannot. Training records are organizational protection.
The Informal Training Record

The brief company drill, the tailgate training, the in-station skill review — these informal training events are often the most frequent and most practically impactful training the crew receives. They also frequently go undocumented. The company officer who documents even brief informal training events builds a comprehensive record that reflects actual training investment.

Training Safety and Live-Fire Protocol

⏱ 12 min🔥 NFPA 1403 · Live Fire Safety

Training exercises — particularly live-fire training — carry their own risk profiles. Multiple firefighter fatalities and injuries have occurred during training evolutions. The company officer who manages training safety with the same rigor applied to operational incident safety is managing a real risk, not a theoretical one.

1403
NFPA 1403 — Standard on Live Fire Training Evolutions
NFPA 1403 establishes the minimum safety requirements for live fire training: safety officer assignment, building inspection and preparation, accountability system, medical standby, hose line requirements, instructor-to-student ratios, and prohibited scenarios. Compliance with NFPA 1403 is not optional at live fire training — it is the documented standard against which training fatalities are measured.
ISO
Training Safety Officer — Required, Not Optional
A dedicated training safety officer (TSO) — separate from the instructors — is required for live fire training. The TSO has the authority to stop any evolution that presents an unsafe condition, without the need to consult instructors or the officer in charge. The TSO who cannot exercise stop authority is not providing real safety oversight.
STD
Stopping Standard — When to Stop
Every training session should have a pre-established stopping criterion: the specific unsafe condition that will cause the evolution to stop immediately. Making this explicit before the training begins — rather than leaving it to judgment in the moment — removes the pressure to continue despite emerging safety concerns.
The Training Complacency Problem

Training fatalities often share a contributing factor: the training environment is treated as lower-risk than operational incidents because "it's just training." Complacency about PPE compliance, accountability, and safety protocols in training creates exactly the conditions that lead to training injuries and deaths. The company officer who enforces the same safety standards in training that they enforce at incidents is not being overly cautious — they are being professionally consistent.

Assessment Center Application —
Training and Development Scenarios

⏱ 10 min🎯 Scoring

Training and development questions at Lieutenant level most often appear in supervisory judgment or administrative scenarios. The 5/5 response demonstrates systematic approach and genuine investment in crew development rather than treating training as an obligation.

Oral Board Prompt

"You have just taken over a company that has had the same officer for eight years. The crew is experienced but the training records show that no systematic training program has been conducted in the past two years — training has been ad hoc and largely undocumented. How do you approach training in your first 90 days?"

3/5 Response
"I would assess the crew's skills and identify areas for improvement. I would create a training schedule and ensure we are meeting all certification requirements. I would document all training going forward."
5/5 Response
"My first step is a needs assessment, not a training schedule. I want to know: what do the certification records show for gaps and approaching expiration dates? What does the incident history show — any near-misses, any performance patterns I should know about? I'll also observe the crew in the first few weeks before drawing conclusions about what they need.

I'd then sit down with the crew and be direct about what I found: 'We have a gap in documented training over the past two years. Part of my job is making sure we build that back up — both because it's required and because it protects all of us operationally and legally.' I'm framing it as shared interest, not criticism of the previous officer or the crew.

Ninety-day plan: first month, certification audit and emergency backfill for anyone with expired credentials. Months 2 and 3, systematic needs-based training — one documented drill per shift, with learning objectives written before the drill and evaluation notes after. I'll use the NFPA 1403 standard for any live fire components and ensure I have a designated training safety officer for every live evolution.

Longer term: monthly training calendar built from needs assessment data, not from what's convenient. Records documented in real time. And I train with them — I'm not setting up a drill and observing. I'm in it."

Putting It All Together — You Already Know How to Train

Here is the part most candidates miss: everything in this chapter is something this program has been doing to you. The way you develop your crew is the way TPS develops you — the science is identical. It is five moves, and every one of them is a framework you already know:

Move 1
Diagnose
3 U’s · Skill/Will · Heifetz · Ch 5
Before you train, find the real gap. Is the crew member Unaware, Unable, or Unwilling? A drill fixes Unable — it never fixes Unwilling. And ask whether the problem is technical (trainable) or adaptive (a culture or belief that training alone will not move).
Move 2
Design
SPARR · deliberate practice · Ch 3
Build effortful, repeatable reps instead of demonstrations: Spot the weakness, Picture excellence, Actively Practice aloud, Run It Live under realistic pressure, then Repeat with one correction. This is the deliberate-practice method the whole program runs on.
Move 3
Coach
CLEAR · SBI · Ch 4
Deliver feedback that develops rather than just corrects: Commend specifically, name the one Limitation, show the Example of what excellence looks like, prescribe the Adjustment for the next rep, and anchor it to the Rank standard.
Move 4
Reinforce
Retrieval practice · spaced repetition
Make it stick with the two most validated tools in learning science: test to learn (retrieval beats re-reading), and revisit at spaced intervals rather than cramming it into one shift. The knowledge checks in every chapter are this principle, applied to you.
Move 5
Reflect
After-Action Review · Kolb · Ch 19
Turn every call and every drill into the next lesson. The After-Action Review is the experiential learning cycle institutionalized — concrete experience, honest reflection, a principle drawn out, and a change tested on the next run.
The 5/5 Development & Mentoring Answer

A company officer who can name this chain at a board is demonstrating Managing-Officer thinking: “I don’t just run drills — I run a development system. I diagnose the gap with the 3 U’s (Ch 5) before I spend a minute training, I design deliberate practice with SPARR (Ch 3), I coach with CLEAR (Ch 4) instead of just correcting, I reinforce with retrieval and spacing so the skill survives past the drill, and I close the loop with an After-Action Review (Ch 19).” That is the difference between a supervisor who fills training hours and a leader who builds capability — and a board hears it instantly.
