The Third Identity Shift —
From Managing to Executive

⏱ 15 min📘 Organizational Leadership · Executive RoleNEMSMA Pillar 5 — Leadership · Pillar 7 — EMS & Society
Fast Track
Frameworks + OB cards only

Every significant promotion in EMS leadership demands more than a title change — it demands a fundamental reorientation of identity. The paramedic learns to follow. The Lieutenant learns to lead people. The Captain learns to lead programs and systems. The Chief, Director, or Executive Officer must learn something categorically harder: to lead the organization itself — across time horizons that extend beyond any single incident, budget cycle, or personnel decision.

This is the third identity shift. It is not an expansion of the Managing Officer role. It is a change in kind, not just degree.

Level Key — Executive Track Context
S Supervising EMS Officer — Operates within the organization's mission. Executes as directed. Understands the executive role in order to communicate organizational decisions intelligibly to crews and to escalate appropriately. M Managing EMS Officer — Implements executive strategy within their functional area. Provides accurate upward information that executive leaders depend on. Understands the executive layer well enough to prepare for it and to communicate decisions from it. E Executive EMS Officer ◆ Primary Level This Chapter — Sets organizational direction, manages governance frameworks, represents the agency externally, and is accountable to the public and governing authority for everything that occurs within the organization.
The executive officer content in this chapter is the primary focus. Supervising and Managing Officers benefit from understanding the executive perspective — both to prepare for advancement and to contextualize decisions that come down through the chain of command.

The Three Identity Shifts in EMS Leadership

Understanding where the executive shift begins requires understanding the full arc of the three shifts that define EMS leadership development. Each shift demands the officer let go of the identity that made them successful at the previous level — one of the hardest things any leader is asked to do.

◆ THE THREE IDENTITY SHIFTS — EMS LEADERSHIP PROGRESSION
What Changes at Each Level — Identity, Time Horizon, and Authority
Each shift is categorical, not incremental. The officer who tries to perform the next level while holding onto the previous identity will struggle at both. The shift must be made deliberately.
Shift One · Phase 1
Supervising Officer
Leads people through daily operations. Identity centered on crew and call. Time horizon is shift-to-shift. Authority is positional and immediate — derived from rank and presence. The work is visible, physical, and feedback arrives quickly. The shift: from provider who does the work to supervisor who ensures the work gets done well.
Shift Two · Phase 2
Managing Officer
Leads programs and systems. Identity centered on process and performance. Time horizon is quarterly to annual. Authority is functional and institutional — derived from scope and accountability. The work is less visible, often administrative, and feedback arrives slowly. The shift: from supervisor of people to manager of systems that produce outcomes.
Shift Three · Phase 3
Executive Officer
Leads the organization and its external environment. Identity centered on mission and legacy. Time horizon is multi-year to generational. Authority is relational and political — derived from trust built over time. The work is abstract, strategic, and feedback may not arrive for years. The shift: from manager of systems to steward of the organization's future.
The executive officer's most common failure mode is not incompetence — it is attachment. Officers promoted to executive rank who remain operationally focused, who still want to run calls, manage grievances personally, or be the smartest clinical voice in the room, have not made the shift. The work they cling to is real and comfortable. The work they need to do is abstract and uncertain. The shift requires deliberately releasing the former to claim the latter.

What Changes: Managing Officer vs. Executive Officer

◆ THE EXECUTIVE SHIFT — WHAT CHANGES AND WHAT DOESN'T
Managing Officer Mindset vs. Executive Officer Mindset
Both levels require intelligence, discipline, and commitment to the mission. What changes is the direction of attention — inward and downward at the managing level, outward and forward at the executive level.

The Executive Officer's Stakeholder Universe

At the supervising level, the primary stakeholder relationship is with the crew. At the managing level, it expands to include the workforce and a handful of institutional partners. At the executive level, the stakeholder universe expands dramatically — and every relationship in it requires cultivation, because every one of them will matter at a moment the executive officer cannot predict.

◆ EXECUTIVE STAKEHOLDER UNIVERSE — FOUR CATEGORIES
Every Relationship Is a Future Asset or a Future Vulnerability
The executive officer who only manages internal relationships will always be surprised by external ones. The one who only manages external relationships will lose their organization from the inside. Both dimensions must be actively managed.
Internal
The Organization Itself
Operations staff, supervisors, administrative team, QA/QI personnel, union representatives, training division. The executive officer's visibility here should be intentional and cultural — not micromanagement, but presence that reinforces values and communicates direction. Internal trust is the foundation everything else is built on.
Governmental
The Authority Structure
City or County Manager, elected officials and their staff, Budget and Finance office, state EMS regulatory body (CDPHE in Colorado), NHTSA and HRSA at the federal level. These relationships determine the organization's resource base, regulatory environment, and political vulnerability. Neglecting them is not neutral — it creates a vacuum others will fill.
Clinical & Partner
The Healthcare Ecosystem
Medical Director, hospital systems and ED leadership, fire and law enforcement leadership, public health departments, mutual aid partners. These relationships define clinical quality, system coordination, and emergency response effectiveness. They also generate letters of support, joint grant applications, and coalition partners when the executive needs them.
Community & Media
The Public Trust Environment
Local news media, community organizations, faith communities, patient advocacy groups, neighborhood associations, school systems, business community. These relationships constitute the agency's public trust reserve. An executive who has invested in community relationships enters every crisis with more credibility than one who appears only in adverse coverage.
The executive officer's primary currency is trust — not the trust of their crew in their clinical competence, but the trust of elected officials, governing authorities, partner organizations, and the community that EMS exists to serve. Trust is built slowly, through demonstrated integrity, transparent communication, and consistent follow-through over years. It can be destroyed in a single press conference managed poorly.

Applying the Framework

The Identity Shift in Plain Language

The Supervising Officer asks: Did my crew perform well today? The Managing Officer asks: Is this system producing the outcomes we need? The Executive Officer asks: Is this organization positioned to fulfill its mission — not just this year, but in ten years — and am I building the relationships and conditions that make that possible?

Contract Management &
Service Delivery Agreements

⏱ 16 min📘 Organizational Administration · Legal FrameworksNEMSMA Pillar 6 — EMS Administration · Bucket 3 — Organizational Culture
Fast Track
Frameworks + OB cards only

At the executive level, EMS organizations do not simply provide service — they negotiate, execute, and manage formal agreements that define the terms of that service. Whether your agency operates under a municipal ordinance, a county franchise agreement, a third-service contract, or an interlocal agreement, the executive officer must understand how these agreements work, what they protect, and where they create vulnerability.

The Managing Officer implements contract terms. The Executive Officer owns them — negotiates them, governs compliance, and manages the relationships within which they operate.

Why This Matters Below the Chief Rank

Captains and Operations Chiefs are routinely responsible for implementing contract terms in daily operations — managing response time compliance, tracking performance metrics, and briefing front-line personnel. Understanding contract structure ensures you enforce correctly, escalate before violations compound, and never make unilateral decisions that inadvertently expose your agency to legal liability or penalty triggers.

Types of EMS Service Delivery Agreements

◆ EMS SERVICE DELIVERY AGREEMENTS — FIVE TYPES
How EMS Systems Are Authorized, Funded, and Governed
No two EMS agencies operate under identical legal frameworks. The executive officer must know which type of agreement governs their agency — and what each type's specific vulnerabilities are.
Type 1
Municipal SDA
City or County Direct Operation
Government directly funds and directs EMS operations. Performance metrics, staffing levels, and service area are defined in the annual budget ordinance or a dedicated service agreement approved by City Council. The chief reports to a city manager or department director. Changes require budget amendment or council action.
Executive rule: Build your relationships with the City Manager's office before budget season begins. The executive who explains their needs at budget time is already too late.
Type 2
Franchise Agreement
Exclusive Franchise Grant
A governing authority (county, regional district) grants an exclusive franchise to operate EMS within a defined service area. The agreement specifies response time requirements, equipment standards, reporting obligations, and often financial penalties for non-compliance. Most common in high-performance EMS systems. The franchise can be lost — and has been, in multiple U.S. systems — for sustained non-compliance.
Executive rule: Know your penalty trigger thresholds cold. Track compliance daily, not quarterly. The first month you learn about a penalty should never be from a cure notice.
Type 3
Interlocal Agreement
Mutual Aid / ILA
Interlocal Agreements govern the terms under which jurisdictions provide resources to one another — response protocols, cost recovery methodology, liability allocation, and command authority during shared operations. ILAs must be reviewed by legal counsel whenever operational practices change. An ILA written for 2010 call volumes and system configurations will contain incorrect assumptions by 2025.
Executive rule: Schedule an ILA review every three to five years minimum. Operational changes that outrun the written agreement create liability exposure for both parties.
Type 4
Medical Direction Agreement
Physician Medical Direction
The formal agreement between the EMS agency and its Medical Director defining scope of practice authority, protocol oversight obligations, QI participation requirements, and the Medical Director's right to restrict or revoke provider certification. This is among the most legally sensitive documents the executive officer manages. The Medical Director's authority over clinical matters is statutory in most states — not merely contractual.
Executive rule: The Medical Director is not a subordinate — they are an authority within their defined domain. Build the relationship as a genuine partnership, not a reporting relationship.
Type 5
Hospital / Health System SLA
Service Level Agreements
Agreements with receiving hospitals may govern data sharing protocols, diversions, cardiac arrest follow-up, community paramedicine arrangements, and training partnerships. As EMS integrates more deeply into the healthcare ecosystem — through mobile integrated health programs, treat-and-refer pathways, and behavioral health co-response — these agreements become increasingly operationally significant.
Executive rule: Hospital SLAs often receive less attention than franchise agreements but can generate significant operational disruption when violated. Assign a point of contact on both sides and review annually.

Core Elements of an EMS Service Delivery Agreement

Regardless of agreement type, the executive officer must be fluent in the elements that define every EMS service contract. These provisions establish the legal and operational boundaries within which the agency functions — and the points at which the agency becomes vulnerable to penalties, audit, or loss of franchise.

Know these provisions in your own contract well enough to quote them from memory. The executive who must look up their response time standard before a council question is the executive who loses credibility.

Response Time Compliance — Executive Governance

Response time agreements create real financial and legal exposure. In high-performance EMS systems, penalties can reach thousands of dollars per non-compliant call. A pattern of sustained non-compliance can trigger contract renegotiation, public scrutiny, or loss of franchise. The executive officer must lead — not delegate — the organization's response time governance program.

◆ RESPONSE TIME GOVERNANCE CYCLE — EXECUTIVE FRAMEWORK
Five Practices That Prevent Compliance Crises
Response time compliance is not primarily an operational problem — it is an executive governance problem. The operational levers (deployment, unit positioning, dispatch protocols) are managed at the supervising and managing level. The executive's job is to ensure those levers are being pulled correctly, consistently, and before a penalty trigger is reached.
Practice
1
Real-Time Awareness
Someone in your organization must own daily compliance tracking — and that person must have a line to the executive before any external party sees the data. Executive officers should not learn about compliance trends from auditors, city managers, or journalists. Build the internal reporting chain that ensures you are always the first to know your own numbers.
Operational note: Weekly compliance dashboards reviewed by the Chief are the minimum. Daily review during periods when performance is trending adverse.
Practice
2
Root Cause Analysis Before Operational Response
When compliance dips, act analytically before acting operationally. Is this a system deployment issue? A dispatch processing time problem? A specific geographic zone where demand patterns no longer match unit positioning? Treating a systemic demand-supply mismatch with overtime adds cost without solving the underlying problem.
Executive rule: The presenting problem is rarely the root cause. Data drives the remedy. A month of root cause analysis is worth more than a year of symptomatic response.
Practice
3
Corrective Action Documentation
Document what you found, what you decided, and when you implemented changes. This record protects the agency when penalties are disputed — it demonstrates good-faith compliance effort and a systematic organizational response. Documentation created after the fact carries far less weight than documentation created contemporaneously with the corrective action.
Legal note: A well-documented good-faith compliance program does not eliminate penalties but frequently mitigates them and always strengthens the agency's negotiating position.
Practice
4
Proactive Governing Authority Communication
When trends suggest a compliance issue is emerging, brief your governing authority before the penalty notice arrives. Officials who learn about problems from you first remain partners. Those who learn about problems from audits or media become adversaries. This principle — brief up before the news travels up — is one of the most important disciplines in executive leadership.
Communication rule: Your City Manager and Council liaison should never read about your agency's performance in the newspaper before receiving a call from you.
Practice
5
Proactive Contract Language Review
Annually review performance standards against operational reality. If standards have become operationally unachievable due to changed circumstances — population growth, call volume increases, geographic expansion, infrastructure changes — initiate renegotiation with data, not with apologies. A request to renegotiate a standard that is no longer realistic is a sign of mature contract management, not weakness.
Negotiation principle: Negotiate what you can actually measure. Performance standards your CAD cannot accurately track create audit liability regardless of actual performance.

⏱ 14 min📘 Public Administration · Policy ManagementNEMSMA Pillar 6 — EMS Administration · Bucket 3 — Organizational Culture

Governance is how an organization makes decisions — who has authority to decide what, how those decisions are documented, and how accountability flows to the people the organization serves. For public EMS agencies, governance is not abstract or theoretical: it is the legal and ethical framework within which every operational and administrative decision is made and must be defensible.

The executive officer does not simply operate within the governance structure — at this level, they help build and maintain it. This includes policy architecture, board and commission relationships, reporting frameworks, and the formal processes that ensure decisions are transparent and accountable.

The EMS Governance Architecture

◆ PUBLIC EMS GOVERNANCE ARCHITECTURE — FIVE LAYERS
Authority, Accountability, and the Executive Officer's Position
Understanding where each layer begins and ends — and where the executive officer's authority is bounded — is fundamental to effective governance. Acting outside your authority layer creates institutional damage; acting below it creates organizational stagnation.
Layer 1
Legislative Authority
City Council / County Commission
Enacts ordinances that create the EMS function, appropriates budgets, and holds final authority over major policy. The executive officer communicates with this body through the City or County Manager, or directly during formal presentations and budget hearings. Ordinances cannot be overridden by administrative action — when they conflict with operational need, the path forward is amendment through the legislative process.
Executive rule: Council members who understand EMS are allies. Invest time in educating them before they form opinions from other sources.
Layer 2
Executive Authority
City Manager / County Administrator
The EMS Chief's direct or near-direct supervisor in most governmental models. Provides administrative oversight, mediates inter-departmental resource conflicts, and represents EMS interests in executive-level budget processes. The executive officer's relationship with the City Manager is among the most important they manage — it is the conduit for every resource request, every policy change requiring administrative approval, and every crisis communication that must reach elected officials.
Relationship principle: The City Manager should trust that they will never be surprised by EMS. That trust is built one proactive communication at a time.
Layer 3
Advisory Bodies
EMS Advisory Committees / Regional Councils
EMS Advisory Committees, Regional EMS Councils, Medical Direction Oversight Committees, and Trauma Advisory Boards. These bodies provide formal input and may have specific statutory authority over licensure, protocol approval, or system designation. In Colorado, the Emergency Medical and Trauma Advisory Council (CEMTAC) exercises significant advisory authority over state EMS system design. Executive officers cultivate these relationships strategically — advisory bodies that support your direction amplify your influence; those that oppose it create significant friction.
Layer 4
Departmental Administration
EMS Chief / Director and Direct Reports
The executive officer and their direct reports — Operations Chiefs, Administrative Officers, QA/QI leadership, Training Director. Responsible for policy implementation, budget execution, and operational decisions within the authority delegated from the layers above. This is the layer where the executive officer exercises full authority and is accountable for everything that flows from it. Every decision made at this layer can be reviewed by the layers above.
Executive discipline: Exercise your authority fully within your layer. Avoid seeking approval for decisions that are legitimately yours to make — it signals weakness upward and uncertainty downward.
Layer 5
Operations
Officers, Crews, and Administrative Staff
Execute within the policy and operational framework established by the layers above. The executive officer's visibility at this layer should be intentional and meaningful — station visits that reinforce culture, recognition that acknowledges values-aligned behavior, presence during significant operational events. Not micromanagement. Not absenteeism. Deliberate, culturally purposeful presence.

Policy Architecture — From Ordinance to Post Order

A well-constructed policy architecture is one of the most powerful governance tools available to the executive officer. Policies are not just rules — they are the documented expression of the organization's values, risk tolerance, and decision-making logic. Poorly written policies create liability. Missing policies create chaos. Policies that exist on paper but are not followed create the most dangerous condition: an organization that cannot defend its decisions in court or before a review board.

◆ POLICY HIERARCHY — FIVE LEVELS
From Legislative Ordinance to Tactical Field Guide
Each level derives its authority from the level above it. The executive officer is responsible for ensuring alignment across all five levels — that what the ordinance authorizes, the policy implements, the SOG operationalizes, the protocol clinically governs, and the post order tactically directs are all consistent and mutually reinforcing.
Level
1
Ordinance / Statute — Legislative Authority
Created by the legislative body. Establishes the EMS function, defines its authority, and sets the boundaries within which all subordinate policy operates. The executive officer cannot override an ordinance — when ordinance language conflicts with operational need, the path is formal amendment through the legislative process, not administrative workaround.
Level
2
Administrative Policy — Executive Authority
Issued by the EMS Director or Chief. Governs agency-wide decisions: hiring standards, leave administration, disciplinary framework, fleet management, and high-level operational standards. These carry the full weight of the executive officer's authority and are reviewed for legal compliance before issuance. Administrative policy should not be changed casually — each change affects the entire organization and creates a documentation obligation in personnel files, training records, and operational logs.
Level
3
Standard Operating Guidelines — Operational Authority
Govern specific operational procedures: unit deployment, scene management, documentation standards, equipment maintenance protocols. SOGs translate policy intent into field-level behavior. Supervising Officers enforce SOGs and are responsible for knowing when field conditions require deviation — and for documenting that deviation appropriately. SOGs that cannot be complied with as written are a policy architecture failure, not a field discipline failure.
Level
4
Medical Protocols — Clinical Authority
Issued under the authority of the Medical Director. Govern clinical decision-making by scope of practice and certification level. The executive officer does not override or amend protocols — but does ensure the infrastructure (training, equipment, QA) supports consistent protocol compliance. Protocols exist at the intersection of administrative and clinical authority and require careful coordination between the executive officer and Medical Director to maintain.
Level
5
Post Orders / Field Guides — Tactical Authority
Deployment guidance, resource positioning documents, and field reference materials. Updated frequently based on system demand analysis and supervisor input. These are the documents most visible to front-line staff — and the ones most likely to drift from current policy without a systematic review process. The executive officer is ultimately responsible for ensuring that what is in the field guide reflects what the organization's policy actually requires.

Applying the Framework

The Policy Gap — The Most Dangerous Governance Condition

A "policy gap" occurs when organizational behavior has drifted from written policy — and no one has updated the documentation. This is among the most dangerous conditions in a public EMS agency. When an incident triggers investigation or litigation, auditors examine three things: what your policies said, what your training records show, and what your supervisors actually did. The gaps between these three are where liability lives. Closing those gaps — proactively, on a defined schedule, before an incident forces the issue — is core executive governance work.

⏱ 13 min📘 Stakeholder Management · Interagency RelationsNEMSMA Pillar 7 — EMS & Society · Bucket 5 — Community Relationships

The executive officer who works exclusively within their own agency will always be limited by what that agency can accomplish alone. The chief who builds strong external relationships can leverage resources, influence, and support that no internal budget line can produce. External relations — with government partners, healthcare systems, community organizations, media, and peer agencies — is not peripheral at the executive level. It is core work.

A network is a collection of relationships. A coalition is a network that has been activated around a shared purpose. The executive who knows how to build coalitions can accomplish things that would be impossible to achieve unilaterally.

The External Stakeholder Landscape

◆ EXTERNAL STAKEHOLDER CATEGORIES — STRATEGIC VALUE AND CULTIVATION APPROACH
Four Domains of External Relationship — Each With Distinct Strategic Value
Different external stakeholders provide different forms of value — and require different cultivation approaches. Treating all external relationships with the same generic engagement approach is ineffective. Map each relationship to the specific value it provides and the specific investment it requires.
Government Partners
Authority and Resource Access
City and county administration, elected officials and their staff, fire and law enforcement leadership, public health departments, emergency management agencies. These relationships determine the agency's resource base, political environment, and inter-departmental coordination capacity. Strategic value: budget authority, policy alignment, mutual aid frameworks, shared capital infrastructure, and political insulation during adverse events. Investment required: consistent communication, proactive briefings, demonstrated responsiveness.
Healthcare Partners
Clinical Integration and Coalition
Hospital system leadership, ED medical directors, Medical Director network, community health organizations, behavioral health providers, long-term care facilities. These relationships govern clinical alignment, data integration, community paramedicine pathways, and MIH program development. Strategic value: clinical outcome improvement, grant partnership, community health impact, and healthcare system advocacy support. Investment required: regular joint review, shared data, collaborative QI processes.
Peer EMS Agencies
Benchmarking and Political Coalition
Regional EMS councils, state EMS directors association, national professional organizations (NEMSMA, NAEMSP, NAEMSE), mutual aid partners, collaborative training and research partners. These relationships provide operational benchmarking, professional development resources, workforce pipeline options, and collective political voice for EMS advocacy. Strategic value: shared intelligence, collaborative grant applications, legislative coalition weight, workforce sharing.
Community and Media
Public Trust and Political Capital
Neighborhood and civic organizations, faith communities, local news media, schools and youth programs, business and chamber of commerce. These relationships constitute the agency's public trust reserve — the credibility reservoir that determines how the public and elected officials respond when the agency faces scrutiny. Strategic value: political insulation, positive narrative generation, recruitment pipeline, community health impact, and the face-saving margin that allows officials to support EMS during difficult periods.
The most important rule in external relations: cultivate relationships before you need them. The executive officer who meets the County Commissioner for the first time when requesting a budget increase is already at a disadvantage. The one who has briefed that commissioner on EMS performance quarterly for two years is a trusted partner making a supported request. Relationship capital compounds over time — but only if the investment begins before the withdrawal is needed.

Coalition Building — From Network to Action

A coalition is not a committee. It is not a regular meeting or a memorandum of understanding. A coalition is a group of aligned stakeholders who have agreed — explicitly or through demonstrated behavior — to act together when a specific objective requires collective effort. Building effective coalitions is one of the highest-leverage skills at the executive level.

◆ THE COALITION BUILDING CYCLE — FIVE DISCIPLINES
Building Durable Alliances That Activate When Needed
Coalitions are built in quiet periods and activated in consequential ones. The executive officer who waits until they need a coalition to start building one will find themselves working alone at the moment it matters most.
Step
1
Identify the Shared Interest
Coalitions form around interests, not relationships. What does the hospital system want that also requires strong EMS performance? What does the City Manager need from the public safety budget that EMS can help deliver? What does the neighborhood association want that EMS community programs can support? Map your objective to what others already care about — then build the coalition around the intersection.
Coalition principle: Find the overlap between your need and their existing priority. Asking people to care about something they don't yet care about is hard. Showing them it connects to something they already care about is much easier.
Step
2
Cultivate Relationships Before You Need Them
The worst time to introduce yourself to a County Commissioner is when you need their vote on your capital budget. The executive officer invests in external relationships during operationally quiet periods specifically because they will need those relationships during difficult ones. Introduce yourself. Brief regularly. Find opportunities to provide value before asking for it. The relationship account must be in surplus before the withdrawal is made.
Step
3
Define a Specific, Actionable Ask
Vague coalitions produce vague outcomes. When the moment comes to activate your network, know exactly what you are asking each partner to do — a phone call to an official, a letter of support, testimony at a budget hearing, a joint press statement, co-authorship on a grant application. The more specific the ask, the more likely the partner will fulfill it. "Please support EMS" produces nothing. "Please call the City Manager this week and express support for the cardiac response unit replacement" produces a call.
Step
4
Deliver on Your Commitments
Coalitions are sustained by reciprocity. When a hospital partner supported your budget request, look for ways to support theirs. When a peer agency chief needs EMS outcome data for a state grant, provide it quickly and completely. When a council member who championed your capital request faces a difficult vote on another matter, show up if you appropriately can. Trust compounds over time. So does its absence.
Step
5
Protect the Coalition's Credibility
Do not activate your coalition for small or low-stakes asks. Partners who are mobilized for routine matters learn to treat your requests as routine. Reserve collective action for objectives that genuinely require it. An executive whose asks are always strategic, well-timed, and proportionate to the relationship investment builds coalitions that last for years. An executive who overdraws the relationship account exhausts their allies.

Media Relationships at the Executive Level

At the Supervising Officer level, media interaction is primarily reactive — responding to incidents on scene. At the Managing Officer level, it is limited and escalated upward. At the executive level, media relationships are strategic assets. The executive who has cultivated a relationship with local reporters will find their side of a controversial story more fairly represented than the executive who only appears in news during crises. Be proactively accessible. Share positive stories. Return calls promptly. These habits do not guarantee favorable coverage — but they build a foundation of credibility that matters during difficult news cycles.

EMS Advocacy —
State & Federal Influence

⏱ 14 min📘 Legislative Relations · Professional AdvocacyNEMSMA Pillar 7 — EMS & Society · Bucket 5 — Community Relationships
Fast Track
Frameworks + OB cards only

EMS advocacy is the organized effort to influence legislation, regulation, and policy in ways that advance the mission, resources, and recognition of EMS as an essential public health and safety service. It is not partisan politics — it is professional stewardship. Every major funding source that supports EMS operations today, from PSAP infrastructure grants to Medicaid supplemental reimbursement to federal preparedness funding, exists because EMS advocates made the case for it before legislators and regulators who would not have acted otherwise.

The executive officer who does not engage in advocacy is not neutral — they are leaving the advocacy space to others whose interests may not align with EMS. That vacuum will be filled.

The EMS Advocacy Landscape — Local to Federal

◆ EMS ADVOCACY LANDSCAPE — FOUR LEVELS OF INFLUENCE
Where EMS Policy Is Made and How Executive Officers Engage
Effective advocacy requires understanding which level governs which issue — and which relationships, organizations, and strategies are appropriate at each level. Local advocacy and federal advocacy require entirely different approaches.
Level 1
Local Government
Budget Hearings, City Council, Department Head Meetings
Focus areas: unit staffing levels, fleet replacement cycles, compensation parity with comparable public safety, capital infrastructure investment. The executive officer is the primary advocate — these relationships are managed through the chain of command and the annual budget process. Local advocacy is the most direct and the most continuously active. It never stops.
Key skill: Translate operational need into constituent impact. "We need two additional units" becomes "without two additional units, 14,000 residents in the northeast district will experience response times four minutes longer than the citywide standard."
Level 2
Regional / County
County Commission, Regional EMS Councils, Trauma Committees
Focus areas: mutual aid frameworks, regional system designation, shared training resources, consolidated dispatch, regional grant coordination. Coalition work is essential at the regional level — no single agency has sufficient standing to act alone. The executive officer's value here is as both an individual advocate and a coalition organizer.
Level 3
State Level
State Legislature, CDPHE, CEMTAC, Governor's Office
Focus areas: EMS licensure reform, Medicaid reimbursement rate improvement, state grant program funding, scope of practice modernization, community paramedicine and MIH enabling legislation, rural EMS sustainability programs. Work through the Colorado EMS Association (CEMSA) and the Emergency Medical and Trauma Advisory Council (CEMTAC). Individual agency testimony carries weight but coordinated organizational positions carry more.
Colorado note: CDPHE Office of Emergency Medical and Trauma Services is the primary regulatory contact. Build that relationship proactively — it matters in rulemaking, licensure issues, and emergency waivers.
Level 4
Federal Level
U.S. Congress, NHTSA, CMS, HRSA, DHS/FEMA
Focus areas: EMS Agenda 2050 implementation framework, Medicare ambulance add-on payment sustainability, No Surprises Act implementation affecting EMS billing, rural EMS sustainability and workforce programs, FEMA preparedness grant programs. Work through NEMSMA, the National EMS Advisory Council (NEMSAC), American Ambulance Association, and state Congressional delegations. Individual agency contact with Congressional offices — particularly during appropriations season — is effective and underused by most EMS agencies.
Federal rule: Congressional staff do constituent meetings. Your U.S. Representative's health policy staff will take a meeting with an EMS Chief from their district. Request it. Bring data. Come with a specific ask. Follow up within 48 hours.

The EMS Advocacy Message Framework

Effective advocacy at any level requires one core skill: translating operational realities into the language of the audience. Legislators and commissioners do not think in clinical or operational terms — they think in constituent impact, cost, and political risk. The executive officer who can frame the EMS need in those terms is far more effective than one who presents operational metrics without context.

◆ ADVOCACY MESSAGE ARCHITECTURE — FOUR COMPONENTS
Translating Operational Reality Into Legislative Language
Every effective advocacy message contains these four components. Missing any one of them weakens the case significantly. Prepare all four before any legislative or governing authority meeting.
Component 1
The Problem Statement
What is the specific challenge — in terms the audience will understand? Who is affected and how many people? What does the current data show? What has already been done, and why is it insufficient? What happens to constituents if this problem is not addressed? Quantify everything that can be quantified. Numbers provide political cover for supporting your position.
Component 2
The Solution
What specific action are you requesting — a vote, a funding allocation, a regulatory change, a pilot program authorization? What will it cost, and over what timeline? Who else supports it? Is there precedent in comparable jurisdictions? What is the implementation path? Vague solutions generate vague responses. Specific requests generate specific decisions.
Component 3
Constituent Impact
How many people are affected, and what does it mean in everyday terms? Can you attach a human face to the policy question — a patient whose outcome was affected, a family who experienced the problem firsthand, a provider whose career was shaped by the issue? Data moves analysis. Stories move decisions. The most effective advocacy combines both — the number that establishes scale, and the story that makes it real.
Component 4
The Ask
Be specific: vote yes on HB 1234, co-sponsor the Rural EMS Stabilization Act, include $400,000 in the supplemental budget for unit replacement. Give a timeline — when is the vote, when does the budget close, when do comments on the proposed rule close? Make it as easy as possible to say yes. Follow up within 48 hours of every meeting, every letter, every testimony. Advocates who follow up win more than those who don't.

What Constitutes an Executive-Level Crisis

Defining the Executive Communications Event

Crisis Communications — Five Phases

◆ EXECUTIVE CRISIS COMMUNICATIONS — FIVE-PHASE MODEL
From Pre-Crisis Preparation Through Post-Crisis Learning
Every phase of a crisis communications event requires a different posture, different priorities, and different outputs. The executive who tries to be in the acute phase before completing the initial response phase will make errors. Sequence matters.
Pre-Crisis
  • Build media relationships before needed
  • Develop agency communications plan
  • Designate PIO or spokesperson
  • Train leadership on messaging
  • Identify likely scenarios; pre-draft responses
  • Establish internal notification protocols
Initial (0–2 hrs)
  • Confirm facts before any public statement
  • Brief chain of command first — always
  • Issue acknowledgment statement only
  • Establish single spokesperson
  • Do NOT speculate on cause or fault
  • Set a defined time for next update
Acute (2–48 hrs)
  • Regular briefings on defined schedule
  • Update as confirmed facts develop
  • Address public concern directly, factually
  • Coordinate all statements with legal
  • Document every statement made
  • Monitor media and social coverage
Recovery (2–30 days)
  • Share findings when review complete
  • Announce specific corrective actions
  • Rebuild stakeholder trust actively
  • Respond to ongoing media inquiries
  • Support affected personnel visibly
Post-Crisis
  • After-action on communications process
  • Update communications plan with lessons
  • Debrief executive leadership team
  • Capture for leadership training
  • Monitor long-tail media coverage

The Acknowledgment Statement — Structure and Use

◆ THE ACKNOWLEDGMENT STATEMENT — FOUR-PART STRUCTURE
What to Say Before You Have All the Facts
The acknowledgment statement buys time, establishes credibility, and signals organizational competence — without making any statement that must later be retracted. Every executive should be able to deliver this template from memory.
1
Confirm the Event — Only What Is Confirmed
"Our agency responded to an incident this [morning/afternoon/evening] involving [general nature of event, e.g., 'a patient transport that resulted in a serious injury']. We can confirm that [only the specific facts you have verified: location, time, general circumstances]."
2
Express Appropriate Concern — Genuine, Not Performative
"The safety and wellbeing of the patients we serve — and of our personnel — is our highest priority. We take this event seriously, and we are committed to understanding fully what occurred."
3
State What You Are Doing Right Now
"We are conducting a thorough review of this incident. [When appropriate: We are cooperating fully with [oversight body / law enforcement / regulatory agency].] [If personnel are involved: We are providing support to the personnel involved.]"
4
Commit to a Specific Follow-Up — With a Time
"We will provide an updated statement at [specific time today]. We appreciate the public's patience as we gather complete and accurate information. I am [name, title], and I am the point of contact for further inquiries."
What this statement does: It establishes that the organization knows about the event, is in control of its response, is concerned about impact, and will provide more information at a defined time. What it does not do: speculate on cause, assign blame, make legal admissions, or provide information that may later require correction. Both elements are equally important.

Oral Board Preparation — The Executive Level

◆ WHAT EXECUTIVE ORAL BOARDS ARE ACTUALLY EVALUATING
Five Dimensions of Executive Assessment
These dimensions are assessed simultaneously through every answer — not through separate questions for each. The candidate who understands what is being evaluated can craft every response to demonstrate all five, not just the one the question appears to target.
Dimension
Strategic Vision
Can this candidate see beyond today's operational state? Do they demonstrate awareness of where EMS is heading — regionally, nationally, clinically — and have credible ideas about how to position this organization for that future? Do they speak in terms of multi-year direction, not quarterly metrics?
Dimension
Political Intelligence
Does this candidate understand how government and public organizations actually work — budget cycles, governing authority dynamics, inter-departmental politics, media relationships, public trust? Do they demonstrate the relational sophistication that the executive level requires?
Dimension
Composure Under Pressure
How does the candidate respond to a difficult question, a challenge to their answer, or an unexpected scenario? Executive officers face high-stakes communications moments regularly — the board is testing directly for this. Candidates who become defensive, over-explain, or lose their analytical frame under pressure communicate executive fragility.
Dimension
Values-Based Leadership
TCHOEDS and L·E·A·D are not frameworks to recite — they must be embodied in how the candidate speaks, frames situations, and describes their leadership philosophy. Candidates who can name frameworks but cannot connect them to concrete experience are performing knowledge, not demonstrating leadership. The board wants to hear TCHOEDS through behavior, not quotation.
Dimension
Organizational Humility
Does the candidate speak positively about the organization, acknowledge its challenges without disparagement, and demonstrate genuine commitment to the mission? Do they show self-awareness about their own limitations and a willingness to surround themselves with excellent staff who complement them? Executives who claim individual omnipotence are red flags. Those who describe building strong teams are credible.

STAR-P at the Executive Level

◆ STAR-P RESPONSE ARCHITECTURE — EXECUTIVE LEVEL
Sample Question: "Describe a time you made a decision that was politically unpopular but organizationally necessary."
At the executive level, STAR-P responses must reflect executive-scope situations — system-wide decisions, multi-stakeholder navigation, budget authority, governing body dynamics. Crew-level scenarios in a Chief-level answer demonstrate that the candidate is thinking at the wrong level.
Situation
Set the organizational context — what was at stake, who the key stakeholders were, and why this decision had political dimensions. 2–3 sentences. Establish that this was an executive-level situation, not an operational one.
Task
Define your specific role and the decision that needed to be made. Establish that the authority and accountability rested with you — and that you understood the stakes involved before you acted.
Action
Describe the deliberative process: who you consulted, what data you reviewed, how you weighted competing stakeholder interests. Show executive rigor — this is not a quick decision, it is a considered one. Include how you communicated the decision and why you chose that approach.
Result
State the measurable outcome — operationally, financially, politically. Be honest if there was short-term political cost. Show the long-term organizational benefit was worth it. Quantify where possible. Acknowledge what you would do differently if anything.
Principle
Name the specific TCHOEDS value that guided the decision — Courage, Ownership, Service. Connect explicitly to your stated leadership philosophy. This is what separates a story from a leadership response.
