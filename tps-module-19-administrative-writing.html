Why Administrative Writing Matters —
The Officer's Written Record

⏱ 10 min✍️ Written Communication · Record

Administrative writing is the company officer's primary mechanism for creating the organizational record — the documented history of what occurred, what was decided, and why. The officer who cannot write clearly, accurately, and appropriately is a liability to the organization and a vulnerability to themselves. Every significant event the company officer manages will eventually be summarized in writing, and that summary may be reviewed by administration, labor relations, legal counsel, or the media.

The "Would It Hold Up?" Standard

Before submitting any administrative document — incident report, counseling memo, disciplinary recommendation, training record — the company officer asks: "If this document were reviewed by a labor arbitrator, a city attorney, or a news reporter, would it be accurate, professional, and defensible?" Documents that couldn't survive that review should not be submitted. Documents that could, should be submitted exactly as written, without softening or inflation.

Incident Reports —
NERIS, Accuracy, and What Gets Reviewed

⏱ 14 min📋 NERIS · Incident Reporting · Accuracy

The incident report — submitted through the National Emergency Response Information System (NERIS) — is the primary operational documentation of fire service activity. That system is new: NERIS launched in 2024 and replaced the 40-year-old National Fire Incident Reporting System (NFIRS), which sunset in 2026. NERIS is cloud-based and near-real-time — it feeds analytics back to your department instead of disappearing into a federal archive for a year, and it captures all-hazards activity, not just fires. It is also a legal document, a billing record for EMS-billing departments, a data source for performance measurement, and evidence in potential litigation. The company officer who treats NERIS as a checkbox is missing the full consequence of what they're certifying.

ACC
Accuracy — Every Field, Every Time
The incident report must accurately reflect what occurred — apparatus and personnel, times, actions taken, patient care, and outcomes. Errors of convenience (routing calls through wrong call types to manipulate statistics) and errors of omission (not documenting property damage, injuries, or protocol deviations) are both documentation integrity failures.
TIM
Timeliness — The 24-Hour Standard
Most departments require incident reports within 24 hours of the incident. Delayed reports are completed from memory rather than from contemporaneous observation — reducing accuracy and creating gaps. The company officer who completes reports at the end of the shift completes them with the freshest and most accurate information available.
EMS
EMS Report Integration — Two Systems, Both Required
EMS incidents require both a PCR — which goes to NEMSIS, the national EMS patient-data system — and a NERIS incident report. The two should be consistent: same times, same demographics, same disposition. Inconsistencies create credibility questions and documentation gaps that surface in quality review and litigation.
REV
The Review Process — What Happens to Reports
NERIS data is reviewed by administration, state fire marshal offices, FEMA, and the US Fire Administration. Aggregated data from NERIS informs national fire safety policy. The company officer who submits accurate, complete data is contributing to the evidence base that shapes the profession.

From NFIRS to NERIS — and Why NEMSIS Didn’t Go Anywhere

Two changes an officer has to be current on. First, the system modernized: NERIS replaced NFIRS, and a candidate who still calls it “the NFIRS report” on a board sounds a decade behind. Second — and this is the one candidates get wrong — NERIS did not replace NEMSIS. They are two different systems doing two different jobs, and an EMS call generates a record in both.

The Incident
NERIS
Captures: what happened operationally — units, personnel, response, deployment, all-hazards.
Replaced: the 40-year-old NFIRS (2024—2026); cloud-based, near-real-time analytics.
Read by: administration, the state fire marshal, FEMA and the USFA — it drives staffing, budget, and risk decisions.
Rule: every incident gets one — including every EMS run.
The Patient
NEMSIS
Captures: the clinical story — patient condition, assessment, treatment, outcome.
Contains: protected health information, so it stays inside the medical-data system.
Status: not replaced by NERIS — it is the separate, complementary EMS patient database.
Rule: every patient encounter gets a PCR here.

One call, two records: NEMSIS tells the story of the patient; NERIS tells the story of the incident and the department. A complete EMS run produces both.

Here is the leadership stake hiding in the paperwork. If your crew runs an EMS call and never files the NERIS incident report — “it was just a medical, the PCR is done” — that call vanishes from your department’s local, state, and national data. Multiply it across a year and the fire service’s single largest workload becomes statistically invisible — which is exactly the data a city manager uses to set your staffing and your budget. Complete documentation is not clerical. It is how the work you actually do stays fundable.

Documentation of Unusual Occurrences

When something unusual occurs at an incident — a protocol deviation, a crew member injury, a vehicle accident, a near-miss, or anything that may generate future inquiry — the company officer documents it immediately and completely. The officer who thinks "I'll document it if it becomes an issue" has already compromised the integrity of the record. Document at the time, completely, and factually.

Memoranda and Formal Correspondence —
Structure, Tone, and Purpose

⏱ 12 min📝 Memo Format · Professional Tone

The administrative memorandum — the primary formal written communication in most fire service organizations — follows a consistent structure and requires a specific professional tone. The company officer who can produce a clear, well-structured memo is demonstrating an organizational capability that distinguishes them in assessment centers and in daily professional operations.

Standard Memo Structure
TO: FROM: DATE: RE: + Body
The standard memo format: TO (recipient), FROM (author with title), DATE, RE (subject line that is specific and searchable). Body: opening paragraph states purpose directly (not "I am writing to inform you..."), subsequent paragraphs provide supporting information in logical order, closing paragraph states the requested action or outcome. No narrative throat-clearing.
Tone — Professional Without Being Cold
Formal + Direct + Respectful
Administrative writing is formal — not casual — but not cold. Direct sentences in active voice. No bureaucratic hedging ("it would seem that..." or "it appears as though..."). State what happened, what the issue is, and what you are requesting or recommending. One clear main message per memo. If there are multiple unrelated issues, write multiple memos.
CLR
Clarity — One Main Message
A memo with multiple unrelated points is a memo that doesn't get fully read and doesn't get filed correctly. One main message per memo. If there are five things to communicate, write five memos or one memo with a clearly organized list.
AVO
Avoid Jargon and Ambiguity
Administrative writing that relies on fire service jargon (codes, acronyms, technical terms) assumes the reader shares the writer's vocabulary — which administrators, HR professionals, and legal counsel may not. Use plain language, define technical terms where necessary, and write for the least-familiar reader in the anticipated audience.
DOC
Document the Request or Decision
Every memo should conclude with a clear statement of what is being requested or decided: "Please advise on approval by [date]," "This memorandum documents my concurrence with [decision]," "I request your approval for [action]." Administrative writing that ends without a clear requested action creates ambiguity about what happens next.
The Email Standard

Most administrative communication now occurs via email — which is also a formal organizational record. The same standards that apply to memos apply to email: specific subject lines, direct professional tone, clear request or decision statement. Emails that are vague, emotional, or unprofessionally written become organizational evidence when they are printed for a grievance or a lawsuit.

The Five-Paragraph Formula — The Structure That Scores

Knowing how to format a memo is table stakes. What separates a 3 from a 5 on a written exercise is structure — and the TPS structure for any substantive memo, recommendation, or written exercise is the Five-Paragraph Formula. It is the same architecture assessors use to score written communication, so writing in it means writing inside the rubric.

1
Situation
Define the problem with specifics, and why it matters now. Not “overtime is a concern” but “overtime is up 23% this quarter, $47K over budget.”
2
Mission
State your purpose directly — what you are recommending or requesting. One sentence, active voice.
3
ExecutionThe paragraph that scores
The plan: who does what, by when, with what accountability. Vague execution caps you at a 3 no matter how good the rest reads.
4
Resources
What it requires — budget, people, time, equipment — and the trade-offs. A recommendation with no resource awareness reads as incomplete.
5
Command
Your clear recommendation, closed with a value anchor naming the principle behind it.

Not every email needs five paragraphs — a quick scheduling note doesn’t. But any document that makes a case — a recommendation, a budget request, a policy proposal, an after-action memo — earns its score in Paragraph 3. Thin execution is the single most common reason a strong-sounding memo lands at a 3.

Counseling Memos and Disciplinary Documentation

⏱ 14 min📋 Counseling Memos · Disciplinary Records

The counseling memo — a written record of a supervisory conversation about a performance issue — is covered in depth in Module 30 (Personnel Management). This lesson focuses specifically on the writing standards that make counseling documentation effective, defensible, and professionally appropriate.

FAC
Factual and Behavioral — Not Interpretive
Document observable behavior: "Member arrived 12 minutes late for shift briefing on [date]." Not: "Member has an attitude problem and doesn't respect the schedule." Observable behavioral facts are documentable and defensible. Characterizations and interpretations are not.
SPE
Specific and Dated — Not General
"Member has been late several times recently" is not a documentation standard. "Member arrived late for shift briefing on [date 1], [date 2], and [date 3]" is. Specific, dated, behavioral records create the foundation for progressive discipline and, if needed, arbitration defense.
SND
Standard Referenced — What Rule Was Violated?
The counseling memo identifies the specific standard, policy, or expectation that was not met: "Department policy requires arrival at least 15 minutes prior to shift for briefing preparation. Member's tardiness on [dates] was inconsistent with this standard."
EXP
Expectation Going Forward — What Happens Next?
Every counseling memo concludes with the expectation going forward: "Member is expected to report for shift briefing by [specific time] beginning with the next scheduled shift. Failure to meet this expectation will result in [next step in progressive discipline]." The member signs to acknowledge receipt of the expectation — not agreement with the content.
The Retaliation Landmine

Any disciplinary documentation that occurs within close temporal proximity to protected activity (grievance filing, union activity, safety reporting) requires HR review before submission. The "temporal proximity test" — described in Module 33 (Labor Relations) — makes discipline that follows protected activity look like retaliation regardless of whether it actually is. HR consultation before submitting is not bureaucratic caution — it is legal protection.

Reports to Administration —
Shift Reports, Special Reports, and Recommendations

⏱ 12 min📊 Admin Reports · Recommendations

The company officer communicates upward through the chain of command through shift reports, special incident reports, and formal recommendations. Each document type serves a different organizational function and requires a different approach.

Shift Reports
Daily Operational Summary
The shift report — or daily activity log — summarizes the shift's operational activity, training, maintenance, and significant events. It is the primary communication channel between the company officer and the battalion chain on non-operational matters. A shift report that contains accurate, complete information about significant events provides upstream visibility that protects both the officer and the organization.
Special Incident Reports
When a Significant Event Requires Additional Documentation
Serious injuries, property damage, officer-involved decisions that may be questioned, or incidents with potential legal significance require a special incident report — a narrative document that provides complete context, sequence of events, and the officer's decisions with their rationale. Written from memory is inadequate; written from contemporaneous notes taken at the scene is the standard.
Recommendations
Making a Case for a Decision
When the company officer recommends a course of action — equipment purchase, policy change, personnel action, program investment — the recommendation document follows the same evidence-based structure as any resource justification: problem statement with data, options with trade-offs, recommended course of action with rationale, and requested decision. Recommendations without supporting evidence are opinions.
When to Escalate
What Goes Up the Chain?
Not everything that happens on a shift needs to go up the chain. The company officer exercises judgment about what requires upward communication: anything that may generate public, media, or governing body interest; anything that involves potential liability; anything that requires resources beyond the company officer's authority; and anything the battalion chief would want to know before they hear it from someone else — which is everything significant.

Making the Case — Monroe’s Motivated Sequence

A recommendation that is merely correct still loses if it does not move the decision-maker. When you write up the chain to persuade — a budget request, a new program, a policy change — the TPS framework is Monroe’s Motivated Sequence. Its core discipline is to lead with constituent impact, not operational metrics. “UHU is 0.47” is your language; “12,000 residents wait four minutes longer for an ambulance” is the city manager’s.

A
Attention
Open with a constituent-impact hook, not a statistic.
N
Need
The problem in the decision-maker’s language — risk, equity, liability, compliance.
S
Satisfaction
Your specific, data-supported solution.
V
Visualization
Paint the after: what success looks like once it is funded.
A
Action
The specific ask — with a timeline and a dollar figure.

The Five-Paragraph Formula structures the document; Monroe’s structures the persuasion inside it. For a routine report, Five-Paragraph is enough. For anything you need someone to say yes to, build the case in Monroe’s.

The "Up First" Principle

The battalion chief should never learn about a significant event in the company officer's shift from someone other than the company officer. This is the company-level application of the "no surprises" principle that Module 38 identifies as foundational for the Fire Chief-city manager relationship. It applies at every level of the chain. Report up before the information travels laterally or externally.

Assessment Center Application —
Administrative Writing Scenarios

⏱ 10 min🎯 Scoring

Administrative writing in assessment centers typically appears as written exercise prompts — draft a memo, write a counseling summary, prepare a recommendation. The scoring evaluates clarity, accuracy, professional tone, appropriate structure, and completeness.

Written Exercise Prompt

"You are the company officer. During your shift, a crew member backed the apparatus into a parked vehicle in the station bay, causing minor damage to the crew member's personal vehicle. No injuries occurred. Draft the memo documenting this incident."

3/5 Response
"TO: Battalion Chief [Name] FROM: Lt. [Name] DATE: [Date] RE: Apparatus accident On [date] during the B shift, Firefighter [Name] backed Engine 4 into their personal vehicle in the apparatus bay. There was minor damage. No injuries occurred. The incident has been reported."
5/5 Response
"TO: Battalion Chief [Name], Station [X] Battalion FROM: Lieutenant [Name], Engine [X], B-Shift DATE: [Date] RE: Apparatus Involved in Minor Vehicle Contact — [Date] Purpose: This memorandum documents a minor apparatus contact that occurred during B-Shift on [date] and provides the relevant details for administrative and vehicle maintenance follow-up. Incident Summary: At approximately [time], while returning Engine [X] to quarters following [call or routine return], Firefighter [Name] (personnel #XXX) was operating Engine [X] in the apparatus bay. During backing, the apparatus made contact with Firefighter [Name's] personally-owned vehicle (make/model), which was parked in the designated personal vehicle area. Impact occurred at an estimated speed of less than 5 mph. Damage Assessment: Minor scrape to the right rear bumper of the personal vehicle, approximately 6 inches. No visible damage to apparatus. No injuries to personnel. Actions Taken: [Name] was advised to contact their personal insurance carrier. The apparatus was inspected; no damage found. The apparatus was returned to in-service status. This report prepared per department policy for apparatus contacts. Recommended Follow-Up: Vehicle maintenance inspection of apparatus bumper at next routine service. [Name] should submit personal insurance documentation to verify no organizational claim will follow. I am available to provide additional information."
Chapter 17 Complete

Here is what “writing inside the rubric” looks like in practice. Take the assessment-center prompt above — the apparatus that backed into a parked car. A 3/5 candidate writes a tidy paragraph that says what happened. A 5/5 candidate writes the Five-Paragraph Formula without ever naming it:

The 5/5 Written Exercise Answer

Situation: At 1420 on [date], while repositioning Engine 12 in the station bay, the apparatus contacted a crew member’s personally owned vehicle, causing minor cosmetic damage to the left rear quarter panel. No injuries; the apparatus was undamaged and remained in service.
Mission: This memo documents the incident, the actions taken, and my recommendation for follow-up.
Execution: I secured the scene, confirmed no injuries, photographed both vehicles, and notified the on-duty battalion chief at 1428. I completed the vehicle-incident report and obtained statements from the backer and the spotter — the specifics are the paragraph that scores.
Resources: Repair estimate pending; no apparatus downtime or staffing impact. I recommend a bay-backing refresher at the next crew training.
Command: I recommend processing this as a minor preventable backing accident with a documented spotter refresher. This reflects Ownership — it happened on my shift, and prompt, accurate documentation is how I protect both my crew member and the department.

Administrative writing is professional self-presentation in permanent form. The officer who writes accurately, specifically, structurally, and professionally is demonstrating the organizational competency that every administrative question about their work will encounter. Write as though it will be read — because it will be.
