The Discipline Scenario —
What Assessors Are Scoring

⏱ 16 min 📋 Captain Track

Progressive discipline scenarios are the most frequently encountered Captain-level scenario type in assessment centers — and the one where candidates most commonly score exactly 3/5. Not because they get it wrong. Because they get it technically right without demonstrating the diagnostic depth, documentation standard, and due process awareness that assessors are specifically trained to look for at the Captain level.

The Lieutenant who was tested on whether they could identify a performance problem and address it directly has now promoted to Captain. The Captain is tested on something more demanding: whether they can manage the entire arc of a performance problem — from first identification through documentation, escalation, due process, and organizational protection — while maintaining the relationship and the crew's confidence in their leadership.

What Assessors Are Specifically Scoring

01
Diagnostic Accuracy
Did the candidate correctly identify which U this situation represents — and is the discipline response matched to that diagnosis? Discipline applied to an Unable or Unaware situation is a scored error. Every assessor writes this down.
02
Step Selection
Is the candidate applying the correct step of the progressive discipline process for this situation? Jumping to written reprimand without a prior verbal counseling — or skipping to suspension when a written reprimand is appropriate — demonstrates a lack of process knowledge.
03
Documentation Discipline
Does the candidate reference documentation as an ongoing practice — not just a reactive measure? The Captain who documents consistently protects the organization. The one who documents only when problems escalate demonstrates a reactive approach assessors score as below standard.
04
Due Process Awareness
Does the candidate demonstrate awareness of the employee's rights at each step? Ignoring due process — or being unaware it exists — is a significant scored error at the Captain level that it was not at the Lieutenant level.
05
Principle Close
Does the candidate close with a named T·C·H·O·E·D·S element connecting their discipline approach to their leadership philosophy? This is the move from 3/5 to 4/5. The Captain who names a principle demonstrates that discipline is not an administrative function — it is a leadership act.

The Single Most Common 3/5 Pattern

The most common way a well-prepared Captain candidate scores 3/5 on a discipline scenario is this: they correctly identify the behavior, correctly apply the right disciplinary step, correctly reference documentation — and then stop. They provide no diagnostic reasoning, no due process acknowledgment, and no leadership principle close.

The response is technically correct. It demonstrates competent administration. And it scores exactly at the level of competent administration — Meets Standard.

❌ 3/5 — Correct but Shallow
"I would have a private conversation with FF Martinez, document the tardiness pattern, issue a verbal counseling per department policy, and set a 30-day improvement timeline." Correct. Complete. No diagnostic reasoning. No due process. No principle. Meets Standard.
✅ 4/5 — Principled and Deep
"Before I act I need to diagnose — this appears to be Unwilling, not Unaware or Unable, based on the pattern and prior conversation. That diagnosis determines my entire approach. I will issue a verbal counseling, document specifically — behavior, date, impact, expectation — ensure Martinez understands their right to union representation, and close with the Ownership element of my philosophy: I own this crew member's performance environment." Above Standard.
The Frame Shift from Lieutenant to Captain

A Lieutenant addresses a performance problem. A Captain manages a performance process. The Lieutenant's frame is: what is the right response to this situation right now? The Captain's frame is: what is the right next step in an ongoing process that protects the employee's rights, the organization's interests, and the crew's confidence in consistent leadership — while giving this person every opportunity to succeed? That frame shift is what assessors are listening for.

The
Progressive Discipline Ladder

⏱ 20 min 📋 Core Framework

Progressive discipline is a sequential process — not a menu. You start at the appropriate step for the current situation, and you move up the ladder only when the previous step has failed to produce the needed change. The Captain who jumps steps does two things: deprives the employee of a fair opportunity to correct their behavior, and exposes the organization to legal and labor challenge.

The critical prerequisite before the ladder applies at all: the 3 U's Diagnostic. Only an Unwilling situation — where the employee knows the standard, can meet the standard, and is choosing not to — belongs on the progressive discipline ladder. Unaware and Unable situations require a different response entirely. This is the most important distinction in this entire module.

The Diagnostic Gate — Run This Before Every Step

Unaware: The employee does not know the behavior is a problem. Response: communicate clearly. Discipline is inappropriate and legally indefensible.

Unable: The employee knows but lacks skill, training, or resources. Response: coach and develop. Discipline is inappropriate and managerially wrong.

Unwilling: The employee knows, can perform, and chooses not to. Response: the progressive discipline ladder applies — starting at the appropriate step.

Diagnosing Unwilling when the situation is actually Unaware or Unable — and disciplining accordingly — is a scored error at the Captain level. Name the diagnostic gate explicitly in every discipline scenario response.

The Four-Step Progressive Discipline Ladder

1
Verbal Counseling
A private, direct conversation identifying the specific behavior, its impact, and the expected standard going forward. Documented by the supervisor — a note to file — but typically not placed in the formal personnel record at this step.
When: First documented occurrence of Unwilling behavior. First step — not skipped.
2
Written Reprimand
A formal documented warning that becomes part of the employee's personnel file. Specific behavior, dates, prior counseling referenced, expected standard, improvement timeline, and consequence of continued non-compliance stated explicitly. Employee receives a copy and typically signs acknowledging receipt — not agreement.
When: Verbal counseling failed to produce sustained change. Pattern documented.
3
Suspension
Removal from duty without pay for a defined period. A significant disciplinary action requiring HR involvement and typically Battalion Chief or above authorization at the Captain level. Documentation must be complete, specific, and legally defensible before this step is taken.
When: Written reprimand failed. Pattern is persistent. Prior steps documented. HR consulted.
4
Termination
Separation from employment. Requires complete documentation of every prior step, HR involvement, legal review in most departments, and the employee's opportunity to respond. A Captain-level scenario involving termination tests whether you know the limits of your authority — and when to escalate above your rank.
When: All prior steps documented and failed. Only with HR and above-rank authorization.

Accelerated Discipline — When Steps Are Skipped

Some behaviors are serious enough to bypass the standard sequence. Understanding when acceleration is appropriate — and naming it explicitly — is a scored Captain behavior.

Acceleration Triggers
When Steps Can Be Skipped
Conduct that immediately endangers safety. Harassment, discrimination, or hostile work environment behavior. Insubordination — direct refusal of a lawful order. Theft, fraud, or dishonesty. Drug or alcohol use on duty. These warrant written reprimand, suspension, or termination as the first step.
The Assessment Center Application
Name the Acceleration
When a scenario involves an acceleration trigger, say so explicitly: "The nature of this behavior — [specific type] — warrants bypassing the verbal counseling step. This is a direct-to-written-reprimand situation because [specific reason]. I am also notifying my Battalion Chief and HR immediately." Naming the reasoning is what scores.
The Captain's Authority Boundary

At the Captain level in most departments, you have clear authority for verbal counseling and written reprimands. Suspension and termination typically require Battalion Chief authorization and HR involvement. In every discipline scenario — name your authority level and the escalation path for steps above it. "I have the authority to issue this written reprimand. If the behavior continues, I will recommend suspension to my Battalion Chief and initiate an HR consultation." Demonstrating authority awareness scores at the Captain level. Candidates who act beyond their authority — or who are unaware of their authority boundary — score below standard.

Documentation
That Protects

⏱ 18 min 📄 Documentation Standard

Documentation is not paperwork. It is protection — for the employee, for you, and for the organization. The Captain who documents correctly creates a defensible record that supports fair outcomes. The Captain who documents poorly — or not at all — creates liability, undermines the discipline process, and scores below standard in every assessment center that tests this skill.

There are two documentation errors that appear in virtually every 3/5 discipline scenario response. The first is treating documentation as reactive — something you do when a problem escalates instead of something you do consistently from the first observed behavior. The second is documenting conclusions instead of behaviors — writing "FF Jones has a bad attitude" instead of documenting the specific observable actions that constitute the performance problem.

The Five Elements of Defensible Documentation

Every documentation entry — whether a supervisor's note to file after a verbal counseling or a formal written reprimand — must contain all five elements to be defensible and scoreable.

B
Behavior — Observable and Specific
What did the person specifically do or fail to do? Describe the observable behavior — not an interpretation of it. "FF Jones arrived at 0815 on three consecutive Tuesdays" — not "FF Jones has been chronically late." The behavior. Specific. Observable. Free of conclusion.
D
Date — Specific and Complete
Every documented incident requires a specific date, time, and location. "On multiple occasions" is not documentation. "On March 4, March 11, and March 18 between 0800 and 0830" is documentation. Date specificity is what transforms an observation into a record.
I
Impact — Organizational Consequence
How did this behavior affect the crew, the operation, or the organization? "The late arrivals required Firefighter Chen to staff the apparatus without relief for 15 minutes on each occasion, creating a safety gap during emergency response readiness." Impact connects the individual behavior to organizational consequence — and makes the documentation relevant beyond the incident.
E
Expectation — The Standard Stated Clearly
What is the performance standard the employee is expected to meet? State it specifically and connect it to policy or operational need. "Per department policy Section 4.2, all personnel are expected to be in uniform and operationally ready at the start of their assigned shift." The standard must be stated — not assumed.
N
Next Steps — Consequence and Timeline
What will happen if behavior does not improve, and by when? "If the tardiness pattern continues after this counseling, a formal written reprimand will be issued within the next 30 days." The next steps must be clear, specific, and followed through. Documenting a consequence and not enforcing it is worse than not documenting at all.

What Good Documentation Looks Like

This is a model documentation entry for a verbal counseling — the kind of documentation a Captain would place in a supervisor's file after the conversation. Study the specificity, the neutrality, and the structure.

Supervisor Documentation · Verbal Counseling
Model Entry — All Five B·D·I·E·N Elements Present
Behavior
"Firefighter Martinez arrived for the 0800 shift at 0814, 0817, and 0822 on February 6, February 13, and February 20 respectively. On each occasion FF Martinez had not completed apparatus check or donned full PPE by the established shift start time."
✓ Observable. Specific dates and times. No conclusions about intent or character.
Impact
"Each late arrival required the on-duty crew to delay apparatus checkout and reduced station readiness during the transition period. On February 13, a call was received at 0803, and FF Martinez was not operationally ready at dispatch."
✓ Organizational consequence named. Specific incident cited.
Expectation
"Per Station 7 operational protocol and Department Policy 3.1, all personnel are required to be in uniform, PPE accessible, and available for emergency response at the designated start of their shift."
✓ Policy reference included. Standard stated specifically.
Next Steps
"FF Martinez was counseled verbally on February 21. FF Martinez acknowledged the expectation and the pattern. If tardiness continues, a formal written reprimand will be issued. FF Martinez was informed of this consequence during the counseling conversation."
✓ Consequence stated. Timeline clear. Employee acknowledgment documented.

What Poor Documentation Looks Like — and Why It Fails

Documentation That Fails Assessment Center and Legal Review

"FF Jones has been difficult lately and has shown a poor attitude toward the crew."
This fails on every element: no specific behavior, no dates, no observable actions, no impact, no standard, no next steps. "Difficult" and "poor attitude" are conclusions — not behaviors. A union representative or HR director will dismiss this documentation immediately. An assessor will score it as below standard.

The rule: If you could not defend this documentation in a grievance hearing — rewrite it before it leaves your desk.

Due Process
+ Labor Relations Awareness

⏱ 16 min ⚖️ Captain-Level Knowledge

Due process is one of the most significant scoring gaps between Lieutenant-level and Captain-level discipline responses. Lieutenants are tested on whether they address a performance problem. Captains are tested on whether they address it in a way that protects the employee's rights, the organization's legal standing, and their own authority.

You do not need to be a labor relations attorney to score 4/5 on a discipline scenario. You need to demonstrate awareness — that you know due process exists, that you know what it requires at each disciplinary step, and that your approach incorporates it as a matter of practice rather than as an afterthought when challenged.

The Four Due Process Essentials at Captain Level

WR
Weingarten Rights — Union Representation
In a unionized department, an employee has the right to union representation at any meeting they reasonably believe could lead to disciplinary action. This right — established by NLRB v. Weingarten — must be offered at formal discipline meetings. In your assessment center response: "I will conduct this counseling privately and inform FF Martinez of their right to union representation at this meeting." Naming this right demonstrates Captain-level awareness.
NO
Notice — Prior Knowledge of the Standard
Discipline is only defensible when the employee had prior notice of the standard they are being held to. If the expectation was never clearly communicated, the documentation and discipline process is compromised from the first step. Your assessment center response should address notice: "Before I proceed — I need to confirm this expectation was clearly communicated and that FF Martinez had prior notice of the standard."
HR
HR Consultation — Before Steps 3 and 4
Suspension and termination require HR consultation before action is taken — not after. At the Captain level, you initiate the HR consultation, you do not wait for HR to find out. In your response: "Before taking any action beyond a written reprimand I will consult with HR to ensure the process is legally defensible and organizationally consistent." Proactive HR engagement is a 4/5 behavior.
RP
Right to Respond — Employee's Opportunity
At every step, the employee has the right to respond to the documentation before it is finalized. This does not mean they can change the facts — it means they have the opportunity to provide their perspective. "I will present the documentation, give FF Martinez the opportunity to respond, and note their response in the record — regardless of whether I agree with it." This demonstrates procedural fairness.

The Firefighter Bill of Rights Awareness

Many states have enacted Firefighter Bill of Rights or Law Enforcement Officers' Bill of Rights statutes that provide specific procedural protections to firefighters during investigations and disciplinary proceedings. While the specific provisions vary by state, the Captain-level assessment center expectation is awareness — not expertise.

What You Need to Say

"I am aware that our department operates under [state] Firefighter Bill of Rights provisions, which govern the procedural requirements of this process. Before proceeding beyond a verbal counseling, I will confirm with HR that our process meets all applicable requirements." That single sentence demonstrates the Captain-level legal awareness assessors score. You are not expected to recite statutes. You are expected to know they exist and to consult the appropriate resource before proceeding.

The Labor-Management Relationship

In a unionized fire department, the Captain's relationship with the union is one of the most complex leadership dynamics you will manage. Assessors watch for one specific behavior in discipline scenarios involving union members: does the candidate treat the union as an adversary, or as a participant in a shared process?

❌ Adversarial Frame — Scores Low
"I don't need the union involved at this stage. This is between me and my firefighter." This language signals to assessors that the candidate views due process as an obstacle rather than a requirement. It is a below-standard response regardless of whether union involvement is technically required at this step.
✅ Partnership Frame — Scores High
"I will inform FF Martinez of their right to union representation at this meeting. The union's role is to ensure the process is fair — and a fair process protects the organization as much as it protects the employee. I want this process conducted correctly." This language signals procedural maturity. Assessors write it down.
The Humility Close for Due Process

Due process scenarios close naturally with the Humility element of T·C·H·O·E·D·S: "The Humility element of my leadership philosophy requires me to acknowledge that I am not the only safeguard in this process. HR, the union, and our department's policies exist to ensure that discipline is applied consistently and fairly — not just in this case but across every case. My job is to follow the process correctly, not just to get the outcome I think is right." This close demonstrates institutional maturity — a quality assessors specifically score at the Captain level.

Chapter 26 · Lesson 5 of 6

Captain-Level
Discipline Scenarios

⏱ 22 min 🎯 Scenario Application

Captain-level discipline scenarios are more complex than Lieutenant-level scenarios in three specific ways. First, the situations involve patterns — not single incidents — requiring the candidate to manage an ongoing process rather than a first response. Second, they often involve complicating factors: prior counseling that was inconsistently applied, union involvement, or a performance problem that straddles the line between Unwilling and Unable. Third, they test the candidate's awareness of the organizational implications of their decisions — not just the individual implications.

The Five Most Common Captain-Level Discipline Scenarios

Scenario Type 1 — The Performance Pattern

A firefighter has received a verbal counseling from the previous Captain for tardiness. The pattern has continued. You have just assumed command of the station and inherited this situation.

The Trap
Starting Over
Candidates who give the firefighter "a fresh start" ignore the prior documentation and restart at verbal counseling. This signals to assessors that documentation has no organizational value — and it undermines the consistency of the discipline process.
The 4/5 Response
Building on Prior Process
Review the prior documentation. Meet with the firefighter to establish your expectations and confirm they are aware of the prior counseling. If the pattern continues, the next appropriate step is a written reprimand — not a second verbal counseling. Name why: the prior process is part of the record.

Principle close: "This comes back to the Consistency element — and specifically to the Dedication element of T·C·H·O·E·D·S. My commitment to this firefighter's development and to the crew's confidence in fair leadership means I honor the prior process rather than restart it. The documentation exists for a reason."

Scenario Type 2 — The Insubordination Incident

A Lieutenant reports that a firefighter directly refused a lawful order in front of the crew. No prior documented performance issues.

The Insubordination Diagnostic

Before any action — apply the 3 U's and the bias check. Was this a clear Unwilling refusal, or was there a misunderstanding of the order? Was the order lawful and clearly communicated? Is there any safety concern that might explain the refusal? A firefighter who refuses an order they believe is unsafe is in a different situation than one who refuses out of defiance. Name the diagnostic out loud before describing any disciplinary response.

If the diagnostic confirms clear Unwilling insubordination: this is an acceleration trigger. Written reprimand as the first formal step, with documentation of the specific refusal, witnesses, and impact. Inform the firefighter of union representation rights. Notify the Battalion Chief. "The nature of this incident — a direct refusal of a lawful order in front of the crew — warrants an accelerated step to written reprimand. I will document specifically, notify my BC, and ensure due process at every step."

Scenario Type 3 — The Inability Misdiagnosed as Unwillingness

A Lieutenant has referred a firefighter to you for "refusing to follow procedures" on the apparatus. When you investigate, you discover the firefighter recently transferred from a department with different SOPs and was never properly oriented to yours.

This is an Unable situation that has been misdiagnosed as Unwilling. The correct response: stop any pending discipline, conduct orientation, and provide coaching. The assessment center tests whether you recognize the misdiagnosis and correct it — even when a Lieutenant has already framed it as a discipline problem.

Principle close: "The Humility element of my leadership philosophy required me to investigate before acting — and what I found changed the entire approach. Applying discipline to a training failure is not leadership. It is the wrong prescription for the right problem."

Scenario Type 4 — The Senior Firefighter

Your most experienced firefighter — 18 years, respected by the crew, close to retirement — has been arriving late and performing below standard for two months. The Lieutenant is hesitant to address it and has asked you to handle it directly.

What Assessors Watch For
Consistency Under Social Pressure
Candidates who give the senior firefighter different treatment than they would a junior firefighter — because of their tenure or proximity to retirement — demonstrate inconsistent leadership. Assessors specifically score whether the candidate applies the standard equally regardless of seniority.
The Nuance
Empathy Without Exemption
Acknowledging the firefighter's career and investigating whether personal factors (health, family, stress) are contributing to the pattern — without exempting them from the standard — is the 4/5 response. The standard applies. The approach is humane. Both can be true simultaneously.

Scenario Type 5 — The Complaint Against a Lieutenant

A firefighter comes to you with a complaint that one of your Lieutenants has been issuing discipline unfairly — targeting this firefighter for minor infractions while ignoring the same behavior from others.

This scenario tests Captain-level authority awareness. You are now managing upward (the Lieutenant's behavior) and lateral (the complainant's experience) simultaneously. The assessment center tests: do you investigate before concluding? Do you maintain confidentiality appropriately? Do you recognize that if the complaint has merit, the discipline process itself may need to be revisited?

Principle close: "This situation calls on the Courage element of T·C·H·O·E·D·S — specifically the willingness to investigate a complaint against one of my own officers with the same rigor I would apply to a complaint against anyone else. Consistency in how I apply scrutiny is as important as consistency in how I apply the standard."

Every framework in this module connects to a single pre-response sequence. The 3 U's Diagnostic is your Label tool. The progressive discipline ladder and B·D·I·E·N documentation framework are your Engage structure. Captain-level scope and due process awareness are your Apply discipline. L·E·A·D in Discipline Scenarios

L
Label — Bucket + Diagnostic
Personnel Issue bucket. Then immediately: run 3 U's. Before naming any action — name the diagnostic. "Before I determine my response I need to establish which U this represents." If you cannot confirm Unwilling — you cannot confirm that progressive discipline is the right intervention at all. Say this out loud. Assessors hear it and write it down.
E
Engage — STAR-P + Discipline Structure
Your STAR-P structure applies to discipline scenarios. In the Action section — name the disciplinary step, name B·D·I·E·N as your documentation framework, name due process elements (Weingarten rights, notice, HR consultation if applicable). Action section is 50% of your response. Do not rush to Result before you have walked through the complete process with specificity.
A
Apply — Captain Scope + Authority Boundary
Every decision must belong to Captain-level authority. You have clear authority for Steps 1 and 2. Steps 3 and 4 require your BC and HR. Name the boundary: "I have the authority to issue this written reprimand. Any suspension would require Battalion Chief authorization and an HR consultation, which I would initiate if the behavior continues." Scope discipline is scored at the Captain level just as it is at every other rank.
D
Deliver — Named T·C·H·O·E·D·S Close
Close with the specific element that connects your discipline approach to your leadership philosophy. Ownership for taking accountability for the performance environment. Courage for addressing the hard conversation. Humility for investigating before concluding. Trust for applying the standard consistently. Name it specifically. Connect it to this scenario. Deliver it with conviction — because it is genuinely true of how you lead.

The Complete Discipline Response — L·E·A·D Integrated

This is what a 4/5 discipline scenario response looks like. Read it against the five scoring dimensions from Lesson 1 and identify where each scored behavior appears.

Model Response — Performance Pattern · Captain Level · 4/5 Range

L — Label: "Before I determine my response I need to diagnose this situation. FF Martinez has received a prior verbal counseling for tardiness from the previous Captain. The pattern has continued. This indicates an Unwilling situation — the standard is known, the capability is present, and the behavior is continuing despite prior intervention. That diagnosis confirms that the progressive discipline process applies."

E — Action (STAR-P): "My response has three components. First — I will meet with FF Martinez privately, inform them of their right to union representation, review the prior counseling, and establish that this meeting is the next step in a formal process. Second — I will issue a written reprimand, documented using specific behavior, dates, organizational impact, stated expectation, and the consequence of continued non-compliance. Third — I will set a 30-day review timeline and document the meeting outcome immediately after."

A — Scope: "A written reprimand is within my Captain authority. If the pattern continues beyond this step, I will consult HR and recommend suspension to my Battalion Chief — I will not take that step without appropriate authorization and legal review."

D — Principle: "Ultimately this response reflects the Ownership element of T·C·H·O·E·D·S — I own the performance environment of this station. That means I own the consistency of how standards are applied and the integrity of the process that protects both this firefighter's rights and this organization's standards. Not one or the other. Both."

That is the 4/5 Captain.
