Project Management
for the Company Officer

Nobody promotes to manage projects, and every officer ends up managing them. Hose testing, equipment rollouts, pre-plan updates, the station remodel. This chapter gives you a four-phase method that fits on one page: define it, plan it, run it, close it. It is built for three shifts, interrupted days and people who do not report to you, and it is exactly what assessors listen for when they ask how you would handle an assigned project.

Chapter 44 · Lesson 1 of 5

The Project —
The Work Nobody Trained You For

⏱ 9 min 📋 Planning & Organizing

The week you promote, somebody hands you a project. Annual hose testing for the battalion. The new SCBA rollout at your station. Pre-plan updates for forty target hazards. A station remodel that needs a point of contact. Nobody calls it project management. They say, "Can you take this?" and you say yes.

Most company officers were promoted for how they run a crew and a scene. A project is a different animal. A scene ends in hours and the incident forces the pace. A project runs for weeks or months, crosses three shifts, depends on people who do not work for you, and has no tones dropping to make it urgent. It fails slowly and quietly, and it fails with your name on it.

Assessors know this. That is why in-baskets, written exercises and oral boards keep asking about assigned projects. They are checking whether you can turn a vague assignment into finished work without your chief having to chase you.

A Task, a Program, and a Project

Task
One person, one sitting
Restock the engine. Write the memo. If it can be finished by one person before the end of shift, it is a task. Use the Eisenhower Matrix and get it done.
Project
A start, an end, and a deliverable
Multiple steps, multiple people, a deadline and a defined finished product. Hose testing complete and documented by June 30 is a project.
Program
Ongoing, no end date
The station fitness program or the preceptor program. A program is sustained. Most programs are launched as a project and then handed off.

The first scored behavior is recognizing which one you were handed. Candidates who treat a project like a task say "I'd get it done." Candidates who recognize a project say what done means, who is involved, and when the chief hears from them next.

What Assessors Are Scoring

Scored Behavior 1
Clarifies the Assignment
Asks or states the outcome, the deadline, the budget and the limits of authority before starting work.
Scored Behavior 2
Builds a Visible Plan
Breaks the work into steps, sequences them, and puts dates on them. A plan the assessor can repeat back.
Scored Behavior 3
Assigns With Names
Every piece has one named owner. "The crew will handle it" is not an assignment.
Scored Behavior 4
Anticipates Problems
Names the two or three things most likely to go wrong and what the plan is for each.
Scored Behavior 5
Reports Up Unprompted
Sets the reporting rhythm with the supervisor and brings bad news early.
Scored Behavior 6
Closes the Loop
Finishes, documents, hands off, and captures the lesson. Most candidates stop at "it got done."
The Core Idea of This Chapter

Your chief is not grading the project plan. Your chief is grading how much of their attention the project costs. An officer who can be handed a problem and return a finished product, with updates along the way, gets handed the next opportunity. That is the behavior the board is listening for.

Define It —
The Five-Line Charter

⏱ 10 min 🎯 Clarifying the Assignment

Projects are lost in the first conversation. The chief says "get the pre-plans updated," pictures forty finished pre-plans in the CAD by September, and walks away. You hear "work on pre-plans when you can." Three months later both of you are frustrated and both of you are right about what was said.

The fix takes five minutes. Before you do any work, write five lines and send them to the person who gave you the project. If they agree, you have a charter. If they correct you, you just saved the project.

The Five-Line Charter

1
Outcome — What Finished Looks Like
One sentence a stranger could verify. "All battalion supply and attack hose service-tested to the current NFPA standard, results logged in the records system, failed lengths tagged and replaced."
2
Deadline — The Date and Why
A calendar date, and the reason behind it. A deadline tied to the ISO review gets treated differently than one somebody picked.
3
Resources — People, Money, Time
Who can I use, what can I spend, and can crews work on it during shift or only between calls.
4
Authority — What I Can Decide
Can I schedule other stations directly or do I go through their officers? Can I approve the purchase or only recommend it?
5
Reporting — When You Hear From Me
A short written update every other Friday, and a call the same day if the deadline is at risk.

This is commander's intent applied to administrative work. You already do it on the fireground when you repeat back an assignment. The charter is the repeat-back for a project.

Make the Outcome SMART

The Outcome line is where SMART Goals earns its place: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound. Test the line by asking whether two different people would agree, on the deadline date, that it was met.

❌ Vague Assignment Accepted — Loses Points
"Yes Chief, I'll get on the pre-plans. We'll knock out as many as we can this summer and I'll keep you posted." No outcome. No date. No limits. No reporting plan. The assessor notes: accepted an undefined assignment.
✅ Charter Confirmed — Scores High
"Chief, to make sure I deliver what you need: forty target hazards re-surveyed and entered in the CAD by September 15, using on-duty crews, with me scheduling directly with the other two station officers. I will send you a count every other Friday. Did I get that right?" Outcome, deadline, resources, authority, reporting. Twenty seconds.
In the In-Basket

When an in-basket item assigns you a project, your written reply should contain the five lines. You are not expected to finish the project in the exercise. You are expected to show that it is defined, owned and scheduled.

Plan It —
Steps, Names, Dates, Risks

⏱ 11 min 🗂 Building the Plan

A plan answers four questions: what are the steps, in what order, who owns each one, and by when. If you can put it on one page, your crew can follow it and your chief can read it in a minute.

Work Backward From the Deadline

1
List the pieces
Write every piece of work that has to happen. For hose testing: inventory, test schedule, equipment reserved, coverage arranged, testing days, records entry, failed hose replaced, final report. Do not sequence yet. Just get them all on paper.
Ten minutes with the crew at the kitchen table
2
Sequence them
Which pieces block others? You cannot schedule test days until you know the footage. You cannot close out until replacements are ordered. Put the blockers first.
Find the two or three that everything waits on
3
Date them backward
Start at the deadline and walk back. Report due June 30, so records done June 23, so testing done June 16, so the schedule published by May 15. Now you know whether today is early or already late.
Build in one week of slack before the real deadline
4
Name them
One name on each piece. Not a shift, not a station. A person.
If two people own it, nobody owns it

RACI — Who Does What

The RACI Matrix settles the arguments before they start. For each piece of work, decide four roles.

R
Responsible — Does the Work
The person whose hands are on it. Engineer Ortiz runs the test pump on B shift.
A
Accountable — Answers for It
One name only. For the project as a whole, that is you. You can delegate the work. You cannot delegate the answer.
C
Consulted — Asked Before
People whose input changes the plan. The other station officers. The training division if you need the drill ground.
I
Informed — Told After
People who need to know but do not get a vote. Your BC. Dispatch on test days.

The Three-Shift Problem

Fire service projects break at shift change. You plan it on A shift, B shift never hears, C shift does it a different way. Solve it on purpose: one written plan posted where all three shifts see it, one named lead per shift, and a pass-on note for the project every morning it is active. If you only brief your own shift, you have planned a third of a project.

The Pre-Mortem

Before you launch, ask the crew one question: "It is the deadline and this failed. What happened?" You will get the real risks in five minutes. Rank them on the Risk Matrix by how likely and how damaging, then plan for the top three.

Risk
Call volume eats the test days
Plan: schedule two more days than you need, and arrange move-up coverage through the BC a week ahead.
Risk
Another station does not buy in
Plan: call that officer first, ask what dates work for them, and let them shape their piece.
Risk
Failed hose has no budget
Plan: get the replacement cost estimate to the chief in week one, not in the final report.

Run It —
Rhythm, Status, and Slippage

⏱ 10 min ⚙️ Execution

Plans do not fail on launch day. They fail in week three, when the first deadline slips and nobody says anything. Running a project is mostly two habits: a fixed rhythm for checking it, and telling the truth about where it stands.

The Weekly Fifteen

Pick one fixed time each week and spend fifteen minutes on the project. Three questions for each open piece: what got done, what is next, what is in the way. Then update the one-page plan. A project that is looked at every week rarely surprises anyone. A project that is looked at "when things slow down" is already behind.

Status Up the Chain — BLUF

Your chief should never have to ask how it is going. Send the update on the schedule you set in the charter, and lead with the bottom line.

❌ Activity Report — Loses Points
"Chief, we have been working hard on hose testing. A shift got some done last week, and B shift is planning to get to it. We ran into a few issues with the pump but we are working through it." Effort, not status. The chief still does not know whether June 30 is safe.
✅ BLUF Status — Scores High
"Chief: hose testing is on track for June 30. 14,000 of 24,000 feet tested, 350 feet failed and tagged. One risk: the test pump is leaking and may not last. I have asked Station 4 to hold theirs for us on the 12th. Nothing needed from you today." Bottom line, numbers, risk, what you are doing about it, what you need.

When It Slips

Something will slip. The scored behavior is what you do in the first twenty-four hours.

1
Say it early
Tell your supervisor the day you know, not the day it is due. Bad news does not improve with age.
Same day
2
Bring options
Come with two ways to recover and your recommendation: add a test day, borrow a crew, or move the date by a week.
Never a problem without a proposal
3
Own it
No blaming call volume or the other shift. "I underestimated the time. Here is the fix." That is Ownership, and assessors write it down.
Internal locus of control

Delegating Without Dumping

You are accountable, but you should not be doing all of it. Use the Skill/Will Matrix to decide how much direction each person needs. A capable, motivated engineer gets the outcome and the date and is left alone. A newer firefighter gets the same piece with a walkthrough and a check at the halfway point. Either way, say why this piece matters and why you picked them. Handing someone a piece of a project is one of the best development tools you have.

Give the Credit Away

When you report up, use names. "Firefighter Kim built the inventory spreadsheet and found 300 feet we did not know we had." It costs you nothing, the chief learns who is doing good work, and your crew will volunteer for the next one.

Chapter 44 · Lesson 5 of 5

Close It —
and L·E·A·D Applied

⏱ 10 min ⚡ Full Integration

Most projects end at ninety percent. The testing is done but the records are not entered. The equipment is on the rig but C shift never got trained. Closing is its own phase, and it is where candidates separate themselves, because almost nobody talks about it.

The Four-Part Close

Close 1
Verify Done
Go back to the Outcome line of the charter and check it line by line. Done means what you agreed to, not what you got to.
Close 2
Document
Records entered, final numbers, money spent, open items listed. A one-page closeout your chief can file.
Close 3
Hand Off
If the project created something ongoing, name who owns it now and what the upkeep is. A project with no handoff slowly comes undone.
Close 4
Review
A fifteen-minute After-Action Review with the people who did the work: what was planned, what happened, why, and what we do next time. Write it down so next year's officer starts ahead.

L·E·A·D on a Project Question

L
Label
In-Basket & Priority. The assessor is scoring planning and organizing. Some project questions also touch Strategic & Organizational at Captain level.
E
Engage
STAR-P if they ask for an example. If they ask how you would handle it, walk the four phases in order.
A
Apply
Name the tools as you use them: charter, RACI, pre-mortem, BLUF status, AAR. Two or three named tools is enough.
D
Deliver
Close with a T·C·H·O·E·D·S value. Ownership and Dedication fit most project answers.

The Complete Response

Model Response · Lieutenant Level · "Coordinate annual hose testing for the battalion"
Define
"The first thing I do is confirm the assignment with you, Chief. My understanding is all battalion hose tested to standard, logged, and failed lengths replaced by June 30, and that I can schedule directly with the other station officers. I will send you a short update every other Friday."
Plan
"I work backward from June 30. I need the inventory first, then a test schedule that gives each shift at each station its days, with two spare days for call volume. Each shift has a named lead. Before we start I ask my crew what is most likely to sink this, and I plan for the top three. My guess is coverage, the test pump, and replacement cost, so I get the cost estimate to you in week one."
Run
"Every week I check it for fifteen minutes and update the one-page plan. If a date slips, you hear it from me that day with two options."
Close
"When testing is finished I verify it against what we agreed, enter the records, give you a one-page closeout, and run a short after-action with the crews so next year is easier."
Principle
"For me this comes down to Ownership. When you hand me a project, my job is to hand you back a finished product, not a list of reasons."
Assessor
Clarified. Planned backward. Named owners. Anticipated risk. Reported up. Closed the loop. Value close tied to the scenario.

Your Practice Target for This Chapter

Pick a real project you have been part of and write its five-line charter after the fact. Then record a ninety-second answer to the OB card below. Listen for whether a stranger could repeat your plan back.

On the Job

Keep a one-page plan template in your notes. The next time someone says "can you take this?", you will have the five lines sent before the end of shift. Do that twice and you will be the officer who gets the interesting assignments.
