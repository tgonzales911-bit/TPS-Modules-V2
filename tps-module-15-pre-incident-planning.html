Pre-Incident Planning —
Why the Walk-Through Saves Lives

⏱ 12 min📋 PIP Purpose · NFPA 1620

Pre-incident planning is the process of systematically gathering, documenting, and distributing information about buildings in a fire company's service area before an emergency occurs. The officer who arrives at a working commercial fire with a current pre-incident plan — and whose crew has reviewed it — makes better tactical decisions in the first critical minutes than the officer who encounters the building for the first time under smoke.

The Research Case

Studies of fireground decision-making consistently show that information gathered before the incident produces better decisions than information gathered under time pressure at the incident. Pre-incident planning is the primary mechanism for moving information from the building-as-built into the officer's mental model before they need it. It is not administrative work — it is preparation for superior performance.

NFPA 1620
Standard for Pre-Incident Planning
NFPA 1620 establishes the standard for pre-incident planning programs. It defines what information should be gathered, how it should be documented, and how plans should be distributed and updated. The company officer who follows NFPA 1620 is building plans that transfer knowledge across shifts, crews, and personnel changes.
Target Hazards vs All Occupancies
Prioritize the Highest Risk
Target hazards — high-occupancy, high-hazard, or unusual occupancies where standard tactical approaches may not apply — receive full pre-incident plans first. Every occupancy benefits from some level of planning, but the limited time available means prioritizing the buildings where a plan makes the most difference.

What to Document —
Building Information That Changes Tactical Decisions

⏱ 14 min📐 Construction · Utilities · Hazards

The Six Information Categories

CON
Construction Type and Features
Ordinary, legacy wood frame, lightweight truss, fire-resistive, non-combustible. Building age. Concealed space locations. Attic access points. Roof construction. Any modifications from original construction that change the structural behavior under fire load.
UTL
Utility Shutoffs — Gas, Electric, Water
Location of gas meter and shutoff. Location of electrical main disconnect. Location of water supply shutoff and FDC connection. Include photographs. Utilities that cannot be quickly shut off create prolonged hazard exposure.
HAZ
Hazardous Materials
Locations of stored chemicals, compressed gas cylinders, flammable liquids. OSHA-required chemical inventories for commercial occupancies. Underground storage tanks. Hazardous processes. This information must be current — inventory changes.
ACC
Access and Egress
All entry and exit points. Locked gate combinations or key box locations. Stairwell locations and configurations. Elevator status and floor numbering. Back and side access routes for secondary apparatus.
OCC
Occupant Information
Normal occupancy load, hours, and typical occupant type. Special population considerations (mobility-limited, non-English speaking, dependent populations in care facilities). Night watchman or security contact information.
SYS
Fire Protection Systems
Sprinkler system type, coverage, and main control valve location. Standpipe system connections and hose valve locations. Fire alarm system type and main panel location. Emergency generator and HVAC override locations.
The Photograph Standard

A pre-incident plan without photographs is a plan that requires the reader to visualize what is described. A plan with photographs — utility shutoffs, access points, hazardous material storage areas, FDC connections — produces accurate mental models. Photograph every plan. Update photographs when the building changes.

The Company Walk-Through —
How to Conduct a Productive Pre-Incident Survey

⏱ 14 min👣 Walk-Through Protocol · Relationships

The walk-through is the primary data-gathering event for pre-incident planning. It is also a community relations interaction — a chance to build the relationship with the building owner or manager that will pay dividends when the company needs access for a second survey, when an inspection reveals compliance issues, or when the company needs current information during an active incident.

Contact the Occupant First
Call Ahead, Not Cold Entry
A walk-through conducted without advance notice intrudes on business operations and generates defensiveness. A scheduled walk-through — presented as a routine community service that helps the fire department serve the building more effectively in an emergency — is almost universally welcomed. Most building managers genuinely want the fire department to know their building.
Bring the Whole Crew
Shared Mental Model
A pre-incident plan review is most valuable when all crew members participate in the walk-through. The firefighter who has physically walked the building — who has touched the walls, seen the utility shutoff, walked the stairwells — retains the spatial information in a way that a map review does not produce.
Document on Scene
Don't Trust Memory
Bring a tablet, form, or camera. Document as you go — don't rely on recall from the truck later. The information that doesn't make it into the plan before the company returns to quarters typically doesn't make it in at all.
Relationship First
Build the Partnership
The walk-through relationship with building managers, business owners, and facility directors is a community relations investment that produces long-term dividends. The building manager who knows the company officer by name calls them directly when there's a problem — before it becomes an emergency.
Update Frequency

A pre-incident plan that is 5 years old for a building that has undergone renovation, changed occupancy, or updated its chemical inventory may be worse than no plan — because it creates false confidence in outdated information. Target hazards should be surveyed annually or when significant building changes occur. Lower-priority occupancies: at least every 3 years.

Distributing Plans + Pre-Incident Drills

⏱ 12 min🗂️ Distribution · Drills · Retention

A pre-incident plan that exists only in a file is a pre-incident plan that doesn't function. Distribution — ensuring that the plans reach all personnel who may respond to the building, in a format they can access and review — and drilling on the information ensure that plans actually shape responses when they're needed.

DIS
Distribution Channels — Paper, Digital, CAD Integration
Plans should be available in multiple formats: paper copies in the station, digital access through tablets on apparatus, and ideally integrated into CAD so responding companies receive plan information automatically when dispatched. The plan that requires a responding officer to remember to check a binder in the station before leaving doesn't get consulted.
DRL
Pre-Incident Drills — Walking the Plan Before the Incident
A tabletop or functional drill using the pre-incident plan — "we have a working fire in the main floor assembly area, what do we know?" — exercises the mental model built by the plan and identifies gaps. The crew that has walked through the plan verbally, discussed the tactical implications of the building's features, and identified the most likely problem scenarios is better prepared than the crew that has only read the document.
Multi-Company Sharing
Buildings Respond to Multiple Companies
High-risk occupancies that could generate multi-company responses need plans distributed to all first-alarm companies, not just the first-due. The mutual aid company arriving at a 3-alarm commercial fire that has never seen the building is in the same position as the company without a plan — unless the plan was shared in advance.
Plan Currency Maintenance
Who Owns the Update Process
Assign specific responsibility for plan updates — by occupancy, by shift, or by staff assignment. Plans that have no designated owner don't get updated. The officer who conducts the annual walk-through, updates the photographs, and confirms utility information is maintaining an organizational asset.

Pre-Incident Planning for Special Occupancies

⏱ 12 min🏥 Healthcare · High-Rise · Industrial

Certain occupancy types require specialized pre-incident planning considerations that go beyond standard residential and light commercial surveys. The company officer builds specific competency for the high-risk special occupancies in their service area.

Healthcare Facilities
NFPA 101 + Evacuation Complexity
Hospitals, nursing homes, and care facilities have complex evacuation requirements — non-ambulatory patients, complex HVAC systems, pure oxygen environments, and radiation hazards. Defend-in-place protocols mean that not all occupants evacuate during fire alarms. The pre-incident plan identifies evacuation staging areas, non-ambulatory patient counts by floor, oxygen shutoff locations, and the facility's own emergency response team capabilities.
High-Rise Buildings
Floors, Systems, and Lobby Control
High-rise pre-incident plans must include: floor numbering and stairwell configuration, HVAC smoke control system operation, emergency generator and fire pump locations, the building's fire control room capabilities, and the lobby control and base layout for major incident operations. Floor plans for key floors are essential.
Industrial and Hazmat Facilities
Chemical Inventory + Process Hazards
Industrial facilities are required under EPCRA to provide Tier II chemical inventory reports to the LEPC. The company officer ensures their plan includes the most current chemical inventory — not just what was there when the plan was written — along with process hazard areas, emergency contact for the facility's safety officer, and shutdown procedures for processes that could escalate a fire.
Places of Assembly
High Occupancy + Egress Complexity
Theaters, arenas, houses of worship, and event venues have high occupancy loads that strain egress systems. The pre-incident plan accounts for maximum occupancy, egress routes and exit capacities, staging areas for occupant accountability, and the building's life safety system interactions.
The Night Hours Factor

The building that has 200 employees during business hours and two security guards at 2am is a fundamentally different response environment. Pre-incident plans should document typical occupancy by time and day — because the company that arrives at a 3am commercial fire in a building they've only planned for daytime occupancy may be missing the critical information about afterhours access, occupant counts, and the absence of daytime staff who know the building.

Assessment Center Application —
Pre-Incident Planning Scenarios

⏱ 10 min🎯 Scoring

Pre-incident planning most often appears in oral boards as part of administrative responsibility questions or supervisory judgment scenarios. The 5/5 response demonstrates systematic approach, relationship-building awareness, and the connection between planning and operational effectiveness.

Oral Board Prompt

"You are a newly promoted Lieutenant assigned to a station with no formal pre-incident planning program. How would you build one?"

3/5 Response
"I would start visiting buildings in our response area and documenting what I find. I would focus on the larger buildings first and make sure my crew is familiar with the information. I would store the plans on the apparatus for easy access."
5/5 Response
"I would start with an inventory and prioritization — what are our target hazards? High-occupancy, high-hazard, unusual construction. That list becomes my planning schedule for the first year. I'd follow NFPA 1620 as the documentation standard so plans are consistent across buildings and shifts.

The walk-through approach matters: I'd contact occupants in advance, bring the full crew so everyone builds the same spatial knowledge, document on scene with photographs, and close every visit by establishing the ongoing relationship with the building contact. Pre-incident planning is a community relations investment as much as a tactical one.

Distribution: I want plans accessible in three places — paper in the station, digital on our tablet, and ideally feeding into our CAD system so we see relevant plan information on dispatch. A plan no one can access isn't a plan.

I'd build drilling into the routine — monthly tabletop on one of our high-hazard occupancies. Twenty minutes reviewing the plan, walking through likely scenarios, identifying tactical implications of the building's features. That's how the plans become muscle memory rather than documents.

Update cycle: target hazards get reviewed annually or after building modifications. I'd assign specific update ownership by building so nothing falls through the shift change."
Chapter 13 Complete

Pre-incident planning is the company officer's primary tool for converting the surprise of an emergency into informed tactical action. The 30 minutes invested in a thorough walk-through and plan documentation can determine the outcome of the incident that happens years later in that building.
