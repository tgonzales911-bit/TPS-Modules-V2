Chapter 15 · Lesson 1 of 6

EMS as the Primary Call Volume Driver —
The Company Officer's EMS Responsibility

⏱ 12 min🚑 EMS Volume · CO Role

In most fire departments, EMS calls represent 60–80% of total call volume. The company officer who treats EMS as a secondary responsibility — something the paramedics handle — is abdicating leadership over the majority of their crew's operational work. The officer who owns their EMS leadership role provides better patient care, better crew wellness, and better operational performance across the full call spectrum.

EMS Call Volume Reality
The Majority of What You Do
National fire service data consistently shows 60–80% EMS call volume in career departments. The "fire department" is, by the numbers, an EMS department that also does fire suppression. The company officer who leads EMS with the same rigor applied to fire operations is managing their actual work.
Medical Direction
The Physician Oversight System
EMS operations are conducted under the medical direction of a licensed physician — the medical director — who is responsible for the clinical protocols that guide field treatment. The company officer understands the medical direction system, knows who the medical director is, and ensures crew compliance with current protocols. Protocol deviations are reported, not covered.

EMS Is Where Your Liability Now Lives

For a generation, the fire service ran on an unspoken assumption: EMS was the paramedic’s job, and if something went wrong on a medical call, it was the highest-trained provider’s problem. The officer ran the scene; the medic ran the patient; the line between them was also assumed to be the line of liability. That assumption is no longer true — and believing it is now one of the most dangerous gaps in an officer’s preparation.

Every EMS provider carries a legal duty to act and to perform a complete assessment on every patient. The officer carries something more — a supervisory duty. You cannot hand care to a less-qualified or distracted crew and look away. When a call goes wrong, the courts no longer ask only “who held the syringe?” They ask “who was in charge?”

Case Study — Aurora, Colorado
When the Law Reached the Company Officer
The death of Elijah McClain, 2019 — and what it surfaced for every ranking officer
In August 2019, an Aurora Fire Rescue crew responded to Elijah McClain, a 23-year-old man being restrained by police. The crew diagnosed “excited delirium” — a concept since discredited and restricted in Colorado — and administered a dose of ketamine too large for his size. McClain went into cardiac arrest and died. The paramedic who gave the drug and the lieutenant on scene were both criminally prosecuted. On the stand, the lieutenant — Peter Cichuniec — said it plainly: “I was the highest rank and made the decisions for the crew.” Of the three first responders convicted, he drew the heaviest sentence and was the only one sent to prison — and the judge pointed to his rank as the reason.
He never touched the syringe. He was the officer. That was enough.
Status, June 2026: A Colorado appeals court reversed the homicide convictions and ordered new trials, while affirming the lieutenant’s felony assault conviction; the Attorney General is appealing. The case is unresolved — but the principle it surfaced is not: a ranking EMS officer can be held criminally responsible for clinical decisions made under their command.

Aurora is not an outlier. In Springfield, Illinois, a supervising paramedic and her EMT partner were charged with first-degree murder after they strapped Earl Moore Jr. face-down to a stretcher and he died of positional asphyxia; the senior clinician’s open contempt for the patient, captured on body camera, became part of the case. In Memphis, three fire department members — including a lieutenant who never left the engine — were fired and a provider’s license suspended after they failed to assess Tyre Nichols. Different states, different outcomes — criminal charges in two, career destruction in the third — but one thread runs through all of them: a failure to truly assess the patient, and a willingness to let someone else, often the police, decide what the medical scene required.

The lesson for a candidate is not fear — it is ownership. The ranking officer on a medical call owns the assessment, owns the clinical decisions the crew makes, and owns the obligation to step in when something is wrong, even when the person acting is more highly trained or more senior in years. “The medic had it” is not a defense. It is, increasingly, the indictment.

The Officer Who Treats EMS as a Second Service

There is a second cost to getting EMS wrong, and it has nothing to do with a courtroom. The officer who treats EMS as a lesser calling — a nuisance, “not real firefighting” — is signaling something to the crew about the majority of the work they do and the people they do it for. By the numbers, that signal lands on almost everything: in many systems roughly 68% of 911 calls are medical and only about 4% are fires. An officer who is cold to EMS is cold to most of the job.

Culture is set from the top. The fire service has a documented spectrum of how departments relate to their EMS mission, and it is driven by tone. Some leaders broadcast, in a hundred small ways, “we do EMS, but if I had my way we wouldn’t.” That attitude trickles down into thin training, weak supervision, and a crew that learns to treat patients as an interruption. A candidate who wants to lead does not get to pick and choose which parts of the mission to support — to be enthusiastic about the fire and resentful of the medical call. Not when the medical call is who your community actually is when they need you.

Tolerate
“We do EMS, but we’d rather not.”
Minimal training, thin supervision, quality improvement run as paperwork. The crew hears the tone and disengages on the calls that make up most of the job.
Accept
EMS is real work, and gets real resources.
Adequate training, staffing, and a functioning QI program. The competent baseline — but the officer’s energy still lives on the fireground.
Embrace
EMS is named as core mission.
The officer leads medical calls with the same intensity as a working fire, develops their medics deliberately, and treats clinical excellence as a point of pride. The only posture that earns the crew’s full effort.

An officer who is EMS-neutral or EMS-negative does not stay neutral in effect — they ostracize the very people they were promoted to support, and they quietly license the whole crew to care less on the calls that matter most. Servant leadership is not selective. You support the mission your community actually calls you for, or you are not yet leading it.

The Patient Assessment Framework —
Scene Safety, Primary Survey, Secondary Survey

⏱ 14 min🏥 Patient Care · Assessment · BLS/ALS

The company officer on an EMS call is responsible for the scene — safety, resource management, crew deployment — and for the quality of patient care delivered. Understanding the patient assessment framework ensures the officer can monitor care quality, identify when additional resources are needed, and manage the scene effectively while the medical team works.

SAF
Scene Safety — Before Touching the Patient
The company officer ensures scene safety before crew members approach: mechanism of injury or illness (what happened?), environmental hazards, behavioral indicators (agitated patient, bystander threats), and BSI (body substance isolation) compliance. Scene safety is an officer function — not just a checklist item.
PS
Primary Survey — The Life Threat Assessment
ABCDE: Airway (is it open?), Breathing (is the patient ventilating adequately?), Circulation (is there a pulse? Is there hemorrhage?), Disability (mental status — AVPU scale), Exposure (expose the relevant body area to assess). Life threats identified in the primary survey are treated immediately.
SS
Secondary Survey — Head-to-Toe Assessment
After life threats are addressed: systematic head-to-toe assessment, patient history (SAMPLE: Signs/Symptoms, Allergies, Medications, Past medical history, Last oral intake, Events leading to the emergency), and vital signs. The secondary survey provides the comprehensive picture that guides treatment and hospital notification.
ACU
Acuity Assessment — What Level of Care Does This Patient Need?
The company officer makes or supports the acuity determination: is this a BLS (basic life support) or ALS (advanced life support) patient? Does the patient need ALS intercept? Rapid transport vs on-scene treatment? The acuity decision determines resource deployment and affects patient outcomes.
The Officer's Clinical Role

The company officer on an EMS call is not a passive observer and not a competing clinician. Their role: ensure scene safety, support the crew's patient care activities, request additional resources when needed, manage the transport decision, and document. They are the manager of the patient care scene — not the primary clinician.

Special EMS Situations —
Mass Casualty, Behavioral Health, and Difficult Scenes

⏱ 14 min⚡ MCI · Behavioral Health · Difficult Calls

Special EMS situations require the company officer to adapt their management approach. Three categories appear with sufficient frequency that company officers benefit from specific preparation: mass casualty incidents, behavioral health emergencies, and difficult scene dynamics.

Mass Casualty Incidents
START Triage and MCI Command
When the number of patients exceeds the available treatment resources, the company officer activates MCI protocols. START (Simple Triage and Rapid Treatment) triage categorizes patients by immediate survival potential: RED (immediate — life threat, treatable), YELLOW (delayed — serious but not immediately life-threatening), GREEN (minor — walking wounded), BLACK (expectant or deceased). The MCI company officer is triaging and managing resource deployment — not treating individual patients.
Behavioral Health Emergencies
Crisis Intervention and Safety
Behavioral health emergencies — psychiatric crises, suicidal ideation, substance-induced altered mental status — require different approach than medical emergencies. Scene safety considerations are distinct (agitated patients, potential for sudden violence). De-escalation techniques, non-confrontational positioning, and calm, direct communication are the primary tools. The company officer manages the scene dynamics while the clinical team manages the patient.
Difficult Scene Dynamics
Managing Bystanders, Family, and Media
EMS calls frequently involve high-emotion environments: family members in distress, bystanders who want to help (or film), neighbors with concerns. The company officer manages the scene environment — not because the emotional bystanders are wrong to feel what they feel, but because the clinical team needs space and calm to work effectively. This is patient advocacy, not crowd control.
The EMS Grief Conversation

The company officer on a pediatric cardiac arrest or other high-acuity call where resuscitation is unsuccessful will face a family notification situation. This is among the most emotionally demanding conversations in the profession. The company officer's role: be present, be honest without being brutal, sit with the grief rather than rushing through it, and ensure the family knows what comes next (medical examiner, personal items, support resources). There is no script that makes this easier — only the commitment to be fully present for it.

EMS Documentation and Quality Assurance

⏱ 12 min📋 PCR · QA · HIPAA

EMS documentation — the Patient Care Report (PCR) — is simultaneously a clinical record, a legal document, and a quality assurance data source. The company officer ensures that PCRs are completed accurately, completely, and in compliance with HIPAA, medical direction requirements, and department standards.

PCR
The Patient Care Report — What It Must Contain
Complete PCR documentation includes: patient identification and chief complaint, primary and secondary assessment findings, interventions performed and patient response, vital signs timeline, medications administered, transport destination, and provider information. Incomplete PCRs are a quality problem, a legal exposure, and a billing issue in departments where EMS generates revenue.
QA
Quality Assurance — The Improvement Loop
EMS QA programs review PCRs and run data against clinical protocols to identify: protocol deviations, documentation gaps, response time performance, patient outcome trends. The company officer who participates in QA review — understanding what the data shows and acting on identified gaps — is applying the same data-driven improvement approach to EMS that Module 36 describes for organizational performance.
HIP
HIPAA — Patient Privacy in the EMS Context
The Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act requires protection of patient health information. In EMS, this means: PCRs are confidential; incident photos including patient identification are prohibited without consent; discussion of patient information in public settings is prohibited; EMS run reports are not sharable with non-involved parties. The company officer who allows HIPAA violations — however inadvertent — is creating organizational liability.
The Call Count vs Quality Debate

High call volume environments create pressure to complete PCRs quickly and move on to the next call. The company officer who allows documentation quality to decline under volume pressure is accepting a degradation of the organization's clinical, legal, and financial position. Brief and complete is achievable. Brief and incomplete is a different matter entirely.

This is also where legal exposure is won or lost. Every EMS provider carries a duty to perform a full assessment on every patient — and the assessment you actually documented is the one that will defend you, or convict you, months later in a deposition or a courtroom. In the cases that have ended EMS careers and put providers in front of juries, the recurring failure was rarely a subtle missed diagnosis under impossible conditions — it was a patient who was never properly assessed at all. “If it wasn’t documented, it wasn’t done” is not a billing slogan. It is the standard a court will hold your crew to — and the officer is responsible for the standard.

EMS Leadership in Cardiac Arrest —
The Highest-Stakes EMS Call

⏱ 14 min❤️ Cardiac Arrest · Code Leadership · Family

Cardiac arrest management is the highest-stakes EMS call type — both because survival depends on the quality of resuscitation and because it is the call that most demands the full integration of clinical leadership, crew coordination, and family care that characterizes the excellent company officer.

STR
Strong Continuous CPR — The Primary Determinant of Survival
Research is unambiguous: the quality of CPR — depth, rate, compression fraction (minimizing hands-off time), and adequate ventilation — is the primary modifiable determinant of cardiac arrest survival. The company officer who monitors and maintains CPR quality — ensuring adequate depth, minimizing interruptions, rotating providers to prevent fatigue — is making the most important resuscitation contribution available to them.
LDR
Code Leadership — The Team Coordination Function
The company officer in a cardiac arrest typically functions as the code leader: directing CPR rotation, monitoring rhythm and shock decisions with the ALS provider, managing airway, coordinating medication administration, and communicating with incoming ALS resources. Code leadership is a coordination function — the company officer is managing the team, not performing all procedures simultaneously.
FAM
Family Presence and Communication
ACLS and resuscitation guidelines support the option of family presence during cardiac arrest resuscitation. Research shows that family members who are present during resuscitation — with a support person to explain what is happening — have better bereavement outcomes than those who wait in another room. The company officer who manages family presence during a code — assigning a crew member to be with the family, providing brief updates, and preparing them for likely outcomes — is demonstrating EMS leadership at the human dimension.
Post-Arrest Crew Debriefing

Cardiac arrests — especially pediatric arrests, witnessed arrests, and unsuccessful resuscitations — are significant emotional events for the crew. The company officer who conducts a brief post-call debriefing — acknowledging what was done well, addressing what could be improved, and checking in on crew wellness — is applying the Module 32 wellness principles to the most emotionally demanding EMS call type.

Assessment Center Application —
EMS Leadership Scenarios

⏱ 10 min🎯 Scoring

EMS questions at Lieutenant level typically appear as part of supervisory judgment scenarios — a crew member's protocol deviation, a quality concern, or a difficult call type management question. The 5/5 response integrates clinical knowledge with leadership values.

Oral Board Prompt

"You review a PCR from last week's shift and notice that your most experienced paramedic administered a medication that was not indicated by the patient's documented vital signs at the time — a potential protocol deviation. How do you handle this?"

3/5 Response
"I would talk to the paramedic about it and review the protocol with them. I would document the conversation. If it happened again I would escalate to my supervisor."
5/5 Response
"Before I do anything, I review the full PCR and confirm my understanding of the protocol is accurate — I want to be sure I'm seeing a deviation before I have the conversation. I also check whether there were documented clinical findings that might not be reflected in the vitals alone (e.g., patient presentation that justified clinical discretion). If this is confirmed as a protocol deviation, I'm required to report it through our QA process to medical direction — that's not discretionary. I cannot handle a protocol deviation internally and not report it.

The conversation with the paramedic: private, non-accusatory, fact-based. 'I reviewed the PCR from [call]. Walk me through your clinical reasoning at that point in the call.' I want to understand what happened before I reach conclusions. There may be context not captured in the PCR, or there may be a knowledge gap that needs addressing. Either way, the outcome is: accurate QA report, and if there's a knowledge gap, specific training to close it.

What I'm not doing: covering it, minimizing it, or treating QA reporting as optional. Patient safety and medical direction oversight exist for a reason. My loyalty to the paramedic is served by helping them stay within protocol — not by protecting them from the QA process."
Chapter 15 Complete

The strongest answers to this scenario do something most candidates miss: they treat the protocol deviation not as a paperwork problem but as a leadership and liability problem the officer owns. You diagnose before you act — is this Unaware, Unable, or Unwilling under the 3 U’s, and is the gap technical or adaptive? You address it directly with the provider, correct the record and the practice through your QI process and medical direction, and document it. And you own the larger question: did your crew feel safe enough to question that decision in the moment? In the cases that reached a courtroom, the tragedy was rarely that no one could see the problem — it was that no one felt able to say “stop.”

The 5/5 EMS Leadership Answer

“I’d label this as both a Personnel and a Safety & Compliance question — it’s a performance issue and a patient-safety issue at once. Before I act, I diagnose with the 3 U’s: was my paramedic unaware the order wasn’t indicated, unable to catch it under pressure, or did they choose to deviate? The intervention is different for each. I’d address it directly and privately, correct the clinical record through our QI process, loop in medical direction, and document it. But I’d own the bigger question too — did my crew feel safe enough to question that call in the moment? The clinical decisions made under my command are mine. I close on Ownership: the standard exists to protect the patient, and holding it is the job I was promoted to do.”

EMS leadership is leadership at the intersection of clinical performance, crew wellness, and community service — applied to the majority of what fire service companies actually do. The company officer who takes EMS leadership as seriously as fire operations is managing their actual work, protecting their crew, and providing better service to the community.
