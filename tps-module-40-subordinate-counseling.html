The Subordinate
Counseling Exercise

The counseling exercise is the most interpersonally demanding component of any assessment center — because it is not hypothetical. A role player is sitting across from you. They will respond to what you actually say, push back on how you say it, and create the kind of real-time pressure that no oral board question can replicate. This module gives you the structure, the language, and the real-time discipline to lead a counseling session that assessors score as Above Standard.

The Counseling Exercise —
What It Is and How It's Scored

⏱ 15 min 👥 Universal Exercise Type

The subordinate counseling exercise is fundamentally different from every other assessment center exercise — and most candidates underestimate that difference until they are in the room. In an oral board you deliver a response to a panel. In a written exercise you produce a document. In both cases, the assessors receive whatever you give them.

In a counseling exercise, the role player gives it back. What you say determines what happens next. The role player is trained to respond to your words, your tone, your structure — and your hesitation. Assessors are not watching what you know. They are watching what you do when another person pushes back on what you know.

How the Counseling Exercise Works

The format is consistent across departments and ranks. You receive a briefing document — typically 15 minutes of prep time — describing the situation, the subordinate's background, and the issue to be addressed. You then meet with the role player, who plays the subordinate, for 15–20 minutes. Assessors observe from a corner of the room or behind a one-way mirror, scoring every behavior in real time.

1
Prep Time
You receive the briefing document. Read it twice. Run your 3 U's diagnostic before you walk in the room. Know your SBI elements — what Situation, what Behavior, what Impact — before your first word. Plan your opening. Know where you want the session to end.
Typical: 15 minutes
2
Opening Phase
Establish the context and purpose of the session. Not a preamble — a clear, direct statement of why you are meeting. This is the most scored phase of the exercise. How you open determines whether the role player cooperates or escalates, and whether assessors hear leadership or hesitation.
First 2–3 minutes — highest scoring density
3
Information Gathering
Before presenting your assessment — gather. Ask open-ended questions. Listen to the role player's perspective. This is where assessors score whether you diagnose before you act. The candidate who gathers before concluding demonstrates 4/5 thinking. The candidate who arrives with a verdict and delivers it scores 3/5.
3–5 minutes
4
Feedback Delivery
Using SBI — state the Situation, describe the specific Behavior, name the Impact. This is the core of the session. Specific. Behavioral. Non-judgmental in language. Directional in purpose. The feedback must be clear enough that the role player — and any assessor — knows exactly what behavior needs to change and why.
5–8 minutes — longest phase
5
Close and Commitment
State the expected standard going forward. Establish a follow-up timeline. Invite the subordinate to ask questions. Close with your leadership principle — the T·C·H·O·E·D·S element that guides how you approach development conversations. Do not leave the room without a stated expectation and a named next step.
2–3 minutes — close matters as much as the open

What Assessors Are Scoring

Scored Behavior 1
Opens with Purpose
Does the candidate clearly state the purpose of the meeting within the first 60 seconds? Vague or tentative openings signal avoidance — a scored negative behavior at every rank.
Scored Behavior 2
Gathers Before Concluding
Does the candidate ask questions and listen before delivering their assessment? Candidates who arrive with a verdict score lower than candidates who investigate even when they already know the answer.
Scored Behavior 3
Behavioral Specificity
Is the feedback specific to observable behavior — or is it about character and attitude? "You have been arriving late on these three dates" scores above "you don't seem to care about the job."
Scored Behavior 4
Manages Resistance
Does the candidate maintain structure when the role player pushes back, deflects, or becomes emotional? Candidates who lose their framework under pressure demonstrate that the framework was not genuinely theirs.
Scored Behavior 5
Clear Close
Does the session end with a stated expectation, a follow-up plan, and an invitation for questions? Sessions that drift to a close — or end awkwardly — lose points that the candidate earned earlier in the exercise.
Scored Behavior 6
Principle Close
Does the candidate close with a named T·C·H·O·E·D·S element — not as a scripted phrase, but as a genuine statement of why this conversation matters to them as a leader? This is the separator.
The Core Difference from an Oral Board

In an oral board you describe what you would do. In a counseling exercise you do it. The role player will not give you the cooperative, attentive response you rehearsed. They will deflect, minimize, get emotional, or go quiet at the worst moment. The discipline you are building in this module is not content knowledge — it is behavioral consistency under real-time interpersonal pressure. That is a different kind of preparation entirely.

The
First 60 Seconds

⏱ 16 min 🎙 Highest Scoring Density

The first 60 seconds of a counseling exercise carry more scoring weight than any other 60 seconds in the session. Assessors form their initial impression of your command presence and interpersonal authority in the first minute — and research on assessment center scoring consistently shows that initial impressions are difficult to reverse.

Most candidates lose points in the opening because of two errors. The first is tentative language — apologetic preambles, hedging, over-explaining the purpose of the meeting before stating it. The second is the opposite: arriving too hard — launching directly into the issue before establishing the relational context that makes the conversation productive. Both errors signal to assessors that the candidate is more focused on their own discomfort with the conversation than on the subordinate's development.

The Opening Formula

The TPS counseling exercise opening has four elements — delivered in under 90 seconds. Every word before these four elements is filler that costs you time and signals avoidance. Every word after them should be a question.

P
Purpose — State it in One Sentence
"I wanted to meet with you today to talk about [specific topic]." Not "I've been meaning to talk to you" or "I hope this won't take long." One sentence. The purpose of this meeting. Clear and direct. Assessors write this down within the first thirty seconds.
P
Posture — Set the Relational Frame
"This conversation is about your development — not your character. I want to be direct with you because I believe that is what good leadership looks like." This sentence does three things: it separates the behavior from the person, it names your intent, and it signals that you are here to help rather than to punish. It takes twelve seconds. It changes the entire dynamic of the session.
P
Perspective — Ask Before You Tell
"Before I share what I've observed, I want to hear from you. How have things been going for you lately?" This question accomplishes two things assessors specifically score: it demonstrates that you gather before concluding, and it gives the role player an opening to provide context that may change your approach. It is also the question that reveals whether this is an Unwilling, Unable, or Unaware situation.
P
Patience — Listen Without Filling the Silence
After you ask the Perspective question — stop talking. The most common error in the opening phase is answering your own question because the silence feels uncomfortable. Silence is information. A role player who goes quiet after your opening question is telling you something. A role player who responds with defensiveness or deflection is telling you something. Listen to both.

The Opening in Practice — Scored and Unscored Versions

❌ Tentative Opening — Loses Points
"Hey, so... I wanted to check in with you. I don't want this to be a big deal or anything, but I've noticed a few things lately and I just wanted to talk about it, you know, just between us. Is that okay?" Apologetic. Minimizing. No stated purpose. No relational frame. Signals avoidance. Assessors note: candidate uncomfortable with difficult conversation.
✅ Clear Opening — Scores High
"FF Martinez, I want to meet with you to talk about your attendance pattern over the past three weeks. This conversation is about your development — not your character — and I'm going to be direct with you because I think that's what good leadership looks like. Before I share what I've observed, I want to hear from you. How have things been going?" Purpose. Posture. Perspective. Then silence.

The Bias Check — Before You Walk In

During your 15-minute prep time — before you enter the room — run your four bias checks from Module 5. This is not a formality. It directly affects how you open the session and how assessors score your interpersonal awareness.

Pre-Session Bias Check Protocol

Affinity Bias: Am I more likely to give this person a pass because I like them or because they remind me of myself?

Confirmation Bias: Have I already decided what the problem is before I gather any information? Am I walking in to confirm a conclusion rather than investigate a situation?

Recency Bias: Am I overweighting the most recent incident relative to the person's overall performance history?

Attribution Bias: Am I attributing this behavior to character rather than circumstance — before I have asked a single question?

Run this check in the last two minutes of your prep time. If you identify a bias — name it silently and consciously set it aside. This is the Humility element of T·C·H·O·E·D·S in practice.

The One Sentence That Changes Everything

"This conversation is about your development — not your character." Practice saying this sentence out loud until it sounds natural and genuine. It is the most important sentence in the opening phase. It reframes the entire session from evaluation to development — and assessors score the moment they hear it because it signals that you understand the difference between managing behavior and judging a person.

The SBI
Feedback Framework

⏱ 20 min 📋 Core Feedback Tool

The SBI Feedback Model — Situation, Behavior, Impact — is the TPS core framework for delivering feedback in any counseling scenario. It is the most important tool in this module because it gives your feedback three qualities that assessors specifically score: specificity, neutrality, and organizational relevance.

Candidates who deliver feedback without SBI tend to deliver it in one of two ways. The first is vague — "you need to take your responsibilities more seriously." The second is personal — "you don't seem to care about this crew." Both approaches fail the scored criteria, provoke defensive responses from the role player, and demonstrate that the candidate is operating from instinct rather than framework. SBI prevents both errors simultaneously.

The Three SBI Elements

S
Situation — When and Where
The specific context in which the behavior occurred. Not a general pattern statement — the specific situation. "On the morning of March 4th, during shift changeover at Station 7." The situation grounds the feedback in observable reality. It signals to the role player — and to assessors — that you are working from documented observation, not impression.
B
Behavior — Observable and Specific
What the person specifically did or failed to do. "You arrived at 0814 — fourteen minutes after the start of your shift — and had not completed your apparatus check by 0830." Not "you were late and didn't do your job." The behavior. Observable. Specific. Free of interpretation. A behavior statement describes what a video camera would have captured — nothing more.
I
Impact — Why It Matters
The organizational consequence of the behavior. "This required FF Chen to staff the apparatus without relief for fourteen minutes. On March 13th, when we received a call at 0803, you were not operationally ready at dispatch." Impact connects the individual behavior to the crew, the mission, and the organization. Without it, the feedback is personal. With it, the feedback is institutional.

SBI Delivered — Scored and Unscored Versions

This is the difference between a 3/5 feedback delivery and a 4/5 feedback delivery for the same behavior. The situation is identical. The score is determined entirely by the framework application.

❌ No SBI — 3/5 Maximum
"Look, I've noticed you've been late a lot recently and it's creating problems. The crew is frustrated and honestly so am I. You need to get it together and start taking this more seriously." Personal. Vague. Emotional. No Situation. No specific Behavior. No organizational Impact. Invites defensiveness. Demonstrates the candidate is reacting rather than leading.
✅ Full SBI — 4/5 Range
"On March 4th, 11th, and 18th during shift changeover, you arrived after the 0800 start time — fourteen, seventeen, and twenty-two minutes late respectively. On each occasion the apparatus check was not complete at the time of your arrival. The impact is this: FF Chen had to staff the apparatus without relief on all three mornings, and on March 13th when we received a call at 0803, you were not operationally ready at dispatch." Specific. Behavioral. Organizational impact named. Defensible.

The SBI Dialogue — What It Sounds Like in Real Time

This is a model counseling session excerpt showing SBI delivered with the gathering phase included. This is the Lesson 3 integration target — not a script to memorize, but a language pattern to internalize.

SBI Delivery — Model Exchange · Lieutenant/Captain Level
Officer
"Before I share what I observed, I want to hear from you. The last few weeks — how have things been going?" (Perspective question — gathering phase)
Role Player
"Things have been fine. I don't know what you're talking about." (Defensive — minimal information)
Officer
"I appreciate you saying that. Let me share specifically what I've observed — and I want you to tell me if I'm missing something. On March 4th, 11th, and 18th, during shift changeover, you arrived after 0800 — on those dates, fourteen, seventeen, and twenty-two minutes late. On each morning, the apparatus check had not been completed by your arrival." (S and B delivered — specific and behavioral)
Role Player
"I've had some stuff going on at home. It's not like I do it on purpose." (Partial disclosure — possible Unable or Unaware signal)
Officer
"I hear you, and I want to understand what's happening. Before we get to that — let me share the impact so you understand why I needed this conversation. On March 13th we received a call at 0803. You were not operationally ready at dispatch. That created a safety gap for this crew." (Impact delivered — organizational relevance named)
Officer
"Now — tell me what's been going on at home." (Information gathering resumes — diagnosis continues)
Coach Note
This sequence delivers full SBI before gathering more information about the contributing factor. The officer does not delay the SBI to avoid discomfort — they deliver it, then return to gathering. This is the 4/5 sequence.
SBI Source

The SBI Feedback Model was developed by the Center for Creative Leadership and is used by leadership development programs worldwide. It is one of the most empirically validated feedback frameworks in organizational psychology. When you deliver SBI in an assessment center, you are demonstrating familiarity with a recognized professional development standard — which itself signals to assessors that you take development seriously as a leadership practice.

Managing
Resistance in Real Time

⏱ 18 min ⚡ Real-Time Performance

Resistance is not a complication in the counseling exercise. It is the exercise. The role player is trained to push back — because assessors need to see how you lead when the person across from you is not cooperative. The candidate who maintains their structure, their tone, and their purpose under resistance demonstrates genuine leadership. The candidate who loses their framework when challenged demonstrates that the framework was borrowed — not internalized.

There are four resistance patterns role players use consistently across assessment center counseling exercises. Knowing them before you walk in the room is the difference between being surprised by resistance and managing it with practiced confidence.

The Four Resistance Patterns and Responses

DE
Deflection — "That's Not the Real Issue"
The role player redirects the conversation to a different problem — typically something involving you, another crew member, or the organization. "You want to talk about my tardiness? What about [other officer] who shows up late every Tuesday?" Your response: acknowledge and return. "I hear you, and if there are other concerns I want to address them. Right now I need to finish this conversation about [the specific behavior]. Let's come back to the other concern after." Do not chase the deflection. Return to your structure.
MI
Minimization — "It's Not a Big Deal"
"I was late a few times — everyone does it. You're making this into something it isn't." Your response: name the impact again. Not with more emotion — with more specificity. "I understand it may not feel significant. The reason I'm raising it is [specific Impact statement]. That is the reason it is a concern." Impact makes the minimization untenable without requiring an argument. You are not debating the severity — you are naming the organizational consequence.
EM
Emotional Escalation — Anger or Tears
The role player becomes visibly upset — angry, tearful, or withdrawn. Your response: pause and acknowledge before proceeding. "I can see this is difficult. I want to give you a moment." Then wait. Do not continue the SBI delivery until the role player is emotionally available. Continuing to deliver feedback to someone who is visibly dysregulated signals to assessors that you prioritize your agenda over the person. Once they are regulated: return to where you were. "Are you okay to continue?"
SI
Silence — Complete Withdrawal
The role player goes quiet. One-word answers. Withdrawn posture. Avoidance of eye contact. Your response: name what you observe without interpreting it. "I notice you've gone quiet. I want to make sure I'm not missing something. Is there anything I haven't asked about that you want me to understand?" This gives the role player an opening without pressuring them — and it signals to assessors that you are attentive to interpersonal cues, not just agenda completion.

The CALM Model Under Resistance

The CALM Model from Module 5 applies directly to resistance management in the counseling exercise. When you feel the session losing structure — run CALM internally before your next sentence.

C
Curious — Not Reactive
When the role player resists, pushes back, or deflects — your first internal move is curiosity. What is this resistance telling you about their experience? What information are you missing? Curiosity prevents the defensive reaction that escalates the session.
A
Acknowledge — Before You Proceed
Name what you heard before you respond to it. "I hear that this feels unfair to you." Not agreement — acknowledgment. The role player needs to know their perspective has been received before they can receive yours.
L
Listen — Completely
While the role player is speaking — you are listening, not preparing your response. Assessors score whether you appear to actually hear the role player or whether you are visibly waiting for your turn to speak. The candidate who listens looks different in a room from the candidate who waits.
M
Manage — Your Response, Not Theirs
You cannot control how the role player behaves. You can control your tone, your pace, your language, and your structure. Managing your response is what assessors are watching. A candidate who stays measured and purposeful while the role player escalates demonstrates the composure assessors want to see at every rank.
The Rule for Resistance

Acknowledge every resistance before you respond to it. Never argue. Never escalate. Never abandon your structure. The role player's resistance does not change your purpose — it may change your pace, your tone, or your information-gathering approach. But the session still ends in the same place: a stated expectation, a follow-up plan, and a principle close. Resistance is the test. Maintaining structure under resistance is the answer.

The Five
Counseling Scenario Types

⏱ 20 min 🎯 Scenario Application

Every counseling scenario in every assessment center at every rank falls into one of five types. Knowing the type before you enter the room changes your preparation, your diagnostic approach, and your principle close. The Situation changes — the structure never does.

Type 1 — The Performance Gap

A measurable, observable performance standard is not being met. Attendance, apparatus maintenance, report completion, physical fitness standards. The most common counseling scenario type at every rank.

Diagnostic Priority
Which U Before Any Action
Is this Unaware (standard not communicated), Unable (standard known but capability lacking), or Unwilling (standard known, capable, choosing not)? The diagnostic answer determines whether the session is a coaching conversation, a development plan, or the opening of a discipline process.
Principle Close
Ownership or Dedication
"Ownership element — I own the performance environment of this crew, which means I own this conversation." Or "Dedication element — my commitment to your development is the reason I am having this conversation directly rather than documenting it and moving on."

Type 2 — The Interpersonal Conflict

A crew member's behavior is creating friction with teammates — complaints from peers, disrupted dynamics, or observed interpersonal aggression. The counseling session involves both the interpersonal behavior and its impact on crew cohesion.

The specific challenge: the role player will frequently minimize interpersonal behavior ("I was just joking" or "they're too sensitive") in ways that invite you to adjudicate the conflict rather than address the behavior. Your SBI must focus exclusively on the observable behavior and its organizational impact — not on who is right in the interpersonal dispute.

Principle close: "The Empathy element of T·C·H·O·E·D·S is what guides this conversation — not just empathy for you, but genuine understanding of the experience of every person on this crew who is affected by the dynamic I've described."

Type 3 — The Personal Crisis Affecting Performance

Performance has deteriorated and the contributing factor is a personal situation — health, family, financial stress. The counseling session requires the candidate to address both the performance impact and the human dimension simultaneously.

The Balance This Scenario Tests

Assessors are specifically watching whether the candidate can hold both truths simultaneously: this person is going through something difficult AND the performance standard still applies. The candidate who excuses the behavior because of the personal situation scores below the candidate who addresses the impact with compassion and maintains the standard with clarity. "I hear what you're going through, and I want to support you — and the crew's operational readiness still requires [specific expectation]. Those two things are both true."

Principle close: "The Empathy element of T·C·H·O·E·D·S doesn't mean lowering the standard — it means holding the standard in a way that communicates genuine care for the person I'm holding it to."

Type 4 — The Conduct Issue

A behavior that violates department policy, professional standards, or safety protocols — not a performance gap but a conduct problem. Insubordination, inappropriate language, policy violation. The counseling session is the opening of a formal discipline process.

The specific requirement: name the conduct as distinct from performance. Use SBI. State the policy violated specifically. Name the due process elements — Weingarten rights, documentation intent, follow-up timeline. Do not soften the purpose of the session: "This conversation is the first step in our formal progressive discipline process."

Type 5 — The Rank-Aspirant Who Is Underperforming

A crew member who openly wants to promote is not meeting performance standards — and their current performance would not support a positive reference from you. This scenario tests whether the candidate can hold the development conversation honestly, without false encouragement, while maintaining the relationship and the crew member's motivation.

The Honesty Requirement

The most common error in this scenario is false reassurance — telling the crew member they are "on the right track" when they are not, to avoid the discomfort of an honest developmental conversation. Assessors score this error severely. The Courage element of T·C·H·O·E·D·S is specifically relevant here: "The Courage element of my leadership philosophy requires me to be honest with you about where you are — not just encouraging. Honest feedback from your supervisor is one of the most valuable things you can receive as someone who wants to promote."

L·E·A·D
Applied to Counseling

⏱ 14 min ⚡ Full Integration

The counseling exercise uses L·E·A·D differently than an oral board does — because the exercise unfolds in real time across fifteen to twenty minutes rather than in a single response. L·E·A·D governs your preparation and your opening. The rest of the session is governed by the structure you committed to in those first sixty seconds.

L·E·A·D in the Counseling Exercise

L
Label — Scenario Type + Diagnostic Gate
During prep time — label the scenario type (performance, interpersonal, personal crisis, conduct, rank aspirant). Then run the 3 U's diagnostic on what you know from the briefing document. Label your preliminary diagnostic: Unaware, Unable, or Unwilling — and note that you will confirm this in the gathering phase. Write your SBI elements — Situation, Behavior, Impact — before you walk in the room. The Label step is your entire prep time, used deliberately.
E
Engage — Four-P Opening + Session Structure
Your structure is the Five-Phase session arc: Open (four-P formula) → Gather (perspective question + listening) → Deliver (SBI) → Manage resistance (CALM) → Close (expectation + follow-up + principle). Commit to this structure before your first word. The structure does not change when the role player resists. Your pace, tone, and sequence may adapt — the destination does not.
A
Apply — Rank-Level Authority and Scope
Every decision made during the session must reflect the authority of your target rank. A Lieutenant-level counseling session does not initiate formal HR processes — it identifies performance gaps and establishes clear expectations. A Captain-level session may open formal progressive discipline. Name your authority boundary where relevant: "As your Captain, the next step within my authority is a written reprimand. If the behavior continues I will recommend suspension to my Battalion Chief."
D
Deliver — The Closing Principle
The principle close in the counseling exercise is different from the oral board. It is not a monologue — it is a final statement of intent delivered directly to the role player. "Before we close, I want you to know why I had this conversation. The Empathy element of my leadership philosophy means I understand your experience matters. The Courage element means I have this conversation directly rather than documenting it and hoping it resolves itself. That is why we are here." Then invite questions. Then close.

The Complete Session — L·E·A·D Integrated

Full Session Arc — Performance Gap · Lieutenant Level · 4/5 Range

L (Prep): Type 1 — Performance Gap. Preliminary 3 U's: Unwilling (pattern documented, standard known). SBI prepared: March 4, 11, 18 / late arrivals / safety gap on March 13th call. Principle close: Ownership element.

E (Open): "FF Martinez, I wanted to meet with you to discuss your attendance pattern over the past three weeks. This conversation is about your development — not your character. Before I share what I've observed, I want to hear from you. How have things been going?"

E (Gather + SBI): Listen. Receive response. Deliver SBI: Situation March 4/11/18, Behavior late arrivals + incomplete apparatus check, Impact — FF Chen relief gap and March 13th dispatch readiness. Return to gather if personal factors emerge.

A (Scope): "I'm documenting this conversation as a verbal counseling. The expectation going forward is [specific standard]. If the pattern continues, the next step will be a formal written reprimand — which is within my authority to issue."

D (Close): "Before we close — I want you to know why I'm having this conversation directly. The Ownership element of how I lead means I own the performance environment of this crew. That includes making sure you have a clear expectation and a fair opportunity to meet it. What questions do you have?"

Your Practice Target for This Module

The counseling exercise requires a different kind of practice than the Practice Engine. Written reps help — but the real development happens in role play. Before your assessment:

  1. Run three Practice Engine sessions with counseling scenarios. Write your four-P opening and full SBI before submitting.
  2. Find a practice partner and run two live role plays — one where the role player cooperates, one where they resist using the deflection or minimization pattern.
  3. Record yourself on Loom. Watch the recording specifically for: did you ask a question before delivering SBI? Did you fill silence or let it exist? Did you close with a named principle?
  4. Submit your strongest Loom recording to your coach for CLEAR feedback.
